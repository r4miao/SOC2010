# Textbook Revision Principles

These principles govern revisions to the SOC 2010 textbook chapters.

1. **Preserve and build.** Do not delete existing concepts, examples, scholars, or substantive claims. Strengthen their placement, explanation, evidence, and connections. Correct genuine factual errors transparently rather than preserving them as errors.
2. **Explain mechanisms.** Do not stop at statements that society, culture, history, power, or institutions matter. Explain how they matter, through which relationships and processes, under what conditions, and with what consequences.
3. **Show reciprocal causation.** Explain how social orders emerge from individual and collective action while also enabling, directing, and constraining later action. Connect macro structures to micro interaction whenever the connection clarifies the topic.
4. **Situate ideas historically.** Place theories, institutions, categories, and evidence in intellectual and historical lineages. Show how timing, sequence, path dependence, and cohort location affect outcomes.
5. **Present genuine theoretical alternatives.** Develop each theory in a recognizable and intellectually strong form. Add consequential contrasting, competing, or synthetic explanations when they are missing, and explain what evidence could distinguish among them.
6. **Attribute contested interpretations.** Identify the scholars associated with disputed claims. Distinguish empirical descriptions, theoretical interpretations, moral judgments, and the textbook's synthesis.
7. **Include neglected contributors and cases.** Incorporate women, racial and ethnic minorities, non-Western scholars and societies, and institutionally excluded contributors when they materially improve the historical or theoretical account.
8. **Use comparisons pedagogically.** Employ parallel examples, cases, timelines, tables, and scholar boxes to expose similarities, differences, sequences, and levels of analysis. Items serving the same function should have comparable depth and a consistent narrative voice.
9. **Preserve controversy without caricature.** Do not sanitize political or disciplinary disagreements, but avoid reducing a theory or group to its weakest version. State strengths, limitations, incentives, blind spots, and counterarguments explicitly.
10. **Write as a coherent chapter.** Begin with a concrete question or tension, make each section create the need for the next, use transitions to connect ideas, and close by synthesizing and applying the chapter rather than merely listing terms.
11. **Keep evidence current and traceable.** Update time-sensitive statistics with recent authoritative sources, state dates and definitions, distinguish levels from trends, and avoid presenting projections as observed facts.
12. **Use companion media selectively.** Include a lecture, interview, documentary excerpt, visualization, or other companion only when it advances a specific learning objective. Verify that links work, identify the source, and explain what students should watch or notice.
13. **Maintain accessible rigor.** Prefer direct prose and concrete examples, but do not achieve simplicity by removing mechanisms, uncertainty, political implications, or disciplinary self-criticism.
14. **Verify the artifact.** Preserve the established visual system, keep navigation and section numbering coherent, check local and external links, and validate balanced HTML structure after every revision.
