Media and Technology

How communication technologies organize attention, identity, relationships, knowledge, and power.

Technology is not only a set of tools. It is a social environment in which people create meanings, distribute information, build identities, and organize collective life.

When a student opens a news link, the visible article is the end of a long sociotechnical chain. A source framed an event; a newsroom selected and edited it; a platform ranked it; advertisers financed an attention market; a recommendation system predicted engagement; and friends added social cues by sharing or commenting. The student still interprets and may reject the message. Media effects therefore arise neither from technology acting alone nor from completely free users, but from interaction among affordances, organizations, algorithms, cultures, and audiences.

Technology in social context

Technologies are designed, funded, adopted, regulated, and interpreted by people and institutions. The effects of a technology depend on who controls it, who can access it, what norms surround it, and how it is incorporated into daily routines. A device may increase connection for some people while producing surveillance, exclusion, or isolation for others.

Media evolved with communication technology: print separates messages from speakers and allows durable replication; broadcasting sends one message simultaneously to a mass audience; storage media allow delayed replay; digital networks make copying, searching, personalization, and interaction inexpensive; and generative AI produces new text, images, audio, video, and code in response to prompts. New media rarely erase older ones. They reorganize their roles: television becomes streaming, newspapers become digital subscriptions, and interpersonal messages become inputs to mass recommendation systems.

Technological determinism versus social construction of technology

Technological determinism treats a technology’s properties as a major driver of social change: movable type supports mass literacy; the clock disciplines time; social media accelerates circulation. The approach highlights material affordances but can make outcomes appear inevitable.

The social construction of technology, associated with Trevor Pinch and Wiebe Bijker, asks how relevant groups interpret a technology, compete over its design, and eventually stabilize one version. A platform’s feed, privacy defaults, moderation tools, and business model are social choices built into code. Yet construction is not unlimited: once infrastructure and standards are installed, they constrain later users. Technology emerges from society and then reorganizes society.

Concept: affordance

An action a technology makes possible or easier in relation to a user and context. Persistence, visibility, editability, anonymity, searchability, scalability, and algorithmic recommendation are digital affordances; none dictates one outcome by itself.

Concept: digital divide

Inequality in access to devices, internet connections, digital skills, quality of service, and the ability to use technology for valued social purposes.

From access to meaningful use

The digital divide has several levels. The first concerns infrastructure, devices, reliability, and price. The second concerns skills and patterns of use. The third concerns outcomes: whether connectivity improves learning, health, income, civic voice, or social support. An unreliable mobile connection may be enough for entertainment but inadequate for remote work, coding, telehealth, or a long application form.

NTIA’s 2023 Internet Use Survey found that 12% of people lived in U.S. households without any internet connection, down from 14% in 2021. Yet only 54% of people in households below $25,000 had both fixed and mobile connections, compared with 80% in households at $100,000 or more. Devices also differed across racial and ethnic categories. Connectivity has spread, while inequalities in quality, redundancy, skill, and productive use remain.

A knowledge-gap hypothesis predicts that when information expands, higher-resource groups may acquire it faster because they have more education, prior knowledge, time, and access. More information can therefore widen knowledge gaps unless institutions make it usable and trustworthy.

Media as a social institution

Mass media select, frame, package, and circulate information. News organizations, entertainment companies, social platforms, advertisers, and users participate in the production of culture. Media do not simply reflect reality; they help define which people, events, and problems become visible and how they should be interpreted.

Marshall McLuhan: the medium matters

Marshall McLuhan argued that the form of a medium can be as important as the content it carries. A new medium changes the scale, speed, and pattern of human association. The same message can have different social effects when delivered through a newspaper, television broadcast, or personalized digital feed.

McLuhan’s insight invites us to ask how a medium reorganizes attention and relationships, not only whether a particular message is true or false.

Public sphere, market, and platform models

Jürgen Habermas: the public sphere

The public sphere is a social space in which citizens exchange reasons about common affairs and form public opinion. Journalism can investigate power, supply shared facts, and connect strangers in democratic discussion. Habermas also traced how commercial publicity, unequal access, and organizational power can distort that ideal.

Feminist and race scholars note that historical public spheres excluded many people and that marginalized groups formed counterpublics with their own institutions and discourse. The democratic question is not merely whether everyone can speak but whose speech becomes visible, credible, and consequential.

A market model treats attention as consumer demand. Competition can generate innovation, expose audiences to varied voices, and punish products people do not value. It can also underprovide expensive public-interest reporting, reward sensationalism, and target affluent audiences. Advertising further creates a two-sided market: media sell content to audiences and audience attention to advertisers.

A platform model adds computational intermediation. Users produce and circulate content, while a platform controls discovery, rules, data, monetization, and technical infrastructure. The platform is neither a neutral carrier nor a conventional publisher. It governs an environment while profiting from interactions within it.

How media can influence audiences

Early “magic bullet” images assumed direct, uniform effects on passive audiences. Later research shows conditional processes: people select content, interpret it through prior identities, discuss it with others, and respond differently. Media influence is still possible, but it usually operates through identifiable mechanisms.

Agenda-setting, framing, and priming

Agenda-setting, associated with Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw, proposes that repeated attention helps determine which issues audiences regard as important. Framing selects aspects of reality and connects them into a problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, or remedy. A protest can be framed around grievances, public disorder, or democratic participation. Priming changes which standards people use when evaluating a leader or event.

These effects do not require false information. Selection, placement, sources, headlines, images, and omissions structure attention. Competing outlets and users can also reframe an issue.

Cultivation, social learning, and uses and gratifications

George Gerbner’s cultivation theory asks whether long-term exposure to recurrent television patterns shapes assumptions about social reality, such as danger or normality. Social-learning theory asks how observed models, rewards, and scripts influence behavior. Correlation is difficult to interpret because audiences self-select and social context matters.

Uses-and-gratifications theory turns toward active audiences: people choose media for information, identity, entertainment, companionship, escape, or coordination. The approach restores agency but can understate how menus, defaults, commercial incentives, and unequal resources structure choice.

Encoding and decoding

Stuart Hall argued that producers encode messages within frameworks of meaning, but audiences may decode them through a dominant, negotiated, or oppositional reading. A police drama intended as respect for authority may be read as entertainment, accepted with reservations, or criticized as propaganda. Meaning is neither fully controlled by producers nor invented without relation to the text.

Identity and interaction online

Digital platforms extend the interactionist processes discussed in earlier chapters. Profiles, posts, photos, likes, and comments are symbols in an imagined social setting. People perform identities for multiple audiences, manage impressions, and interpret the responses of others. The persistence and searchability of digital communication can make front-stage performances difficult to separate from back-stage life.

Bauman’s idea of liquid modernity helps explain why digital media intensify these pressures. Online identities can be edited quickly, relationships can be maintained across distance but ended with little institutional ritual, and attention becomes a scarce resource. The promise of flexibility and connection is accompanied by the expectation that people remain continuously available, visible, and adaptable.

Example: the algorithmic audience

A user may believe that a post is addressed to friends, while the platform distributes it according to an algorithm optimized for attention. The audience is therefore partly social and partly computational. Understanding the interaction requires examining both meaning-making and the institutional design of the platform.

Danah boyd calls this problem context collapse: people from different parts of life occupy one mediated audience, even though a joke or identity performance makes sense only within a particular context. Users respond with privacy settings, coded language, secondary accounts, deletion, and self-censorship. Platforms respond by redesigning controls, creating a continuing interaction between user practice and technical architecture.

Connection, displacement, and “alone together”

Digital communication sustains weak ties, diasporic families, disability communities, political networks, and friendships across distance. It can also interrupt co-present attention, intensify comparison, or substitute low-quality contact for needed support. Sherry Turkle’s phrase alone together captures the possibility of physical proximity combined with divided attention. The strongest question is not whether “social media” is good or bad, but which activity, for whom, displacing what, and under which relationship conditions.

Platform populations differ sharply. Pew’s 2025 survey found that 84% of U.S. adults used YouTube, 71% Facebook, 50% Instagram, and 37% TikTok. TikTok use ranged from 63% among adults ages 18–29 to 12% among those 65 and older. A finding about one platform, age group, or use pattern should not be generalized to all digital life.

Power, surveillance, and inequality

Media systems concentrate power when a small number of firms control infrastructure, data, advertising, and distribution. Algorithms can personalize information, but personalization may also narrow exposure to alternative perspectives. Surveillance technologies turn ordinary activity into data that can be used for marketing, management, policing, or political influence.

A sociological analysis avoids both technological optimism and technological pessimism. It asks who benefits, who bears risks, whose voices are amplified, how rules are made, and what forms of collective action are possible.

Political economy and ownership

Conflict theorists study concentration across infrastructure, cloud computing, advertising, entertainment, news, app stores, and social platforms. Ownership can shape priorities through budgets, hiring, risk tolerance, and the audience or advertiser a firm seeks, even without an owner dictating every story. Journalists and creators retain professional norms and autonomy, competitors expose failures, and users can organize alternatives; concentration creates power, not perfect control.

Cultural imperialism theory asks whether globally dominant producers diffuse their values and crowd out local culture. Reception and hybridization theories counter that audiences reinterpret imported forms and local industries adapt them. The empirical question is who controls distribution and revenue as well as what meanings audiences make.

Michel Foucault and Shoshana Zuboff: surveillance

Foucault’s panopticon metaphor explains how the possibility of observation can induce people to monitor themselves. Digital systems extend visibility through location, clicks, purchases, faces, social ties, productivity measures, and predictive scores. Zuboff calls the commercial extraction and prediction of behavior surveillance capitalism.

Surveillance can provide security, personalization, fraud detection, and organizational learning. Its risks depend on consent, purpose, error, retention, unequal exposure, and the ability to challenge decisions. A functionalist asks what problem monitoring solves; a conflict theorist asks who can watch whom and who sets the terms.

Algorithms, misinformation, and computational authority

An algorithmic feed ranks from an available pool; it does not simply show “the internet.” Ranking can increase relevance and help users navigate abundance, while optimization for predicted engagement can privilege emotionally arousing or familiar material. The effects are produced jointly by platform objectives, model design, creator strategies, social networks, and user choices.

Echo chambers describe networks in which repeated similar claims receive amplification and opposing claims are absent or discredited. Filter bubbles emphasize personalized ranking. Their prevalence and effects are contested: people often encounter disagreement online, and partisan selection can be stronger than algorithmic filtering. Exposure to opposing content also does not guarantee moderation; it can provoke counterargument or strengthen identity. The concepts should be hypotheses measured against actual networks and behavior.

From information to disinformation

Misinformation is false or misleading regardless of intent; disinformation is created or spread strategically to deceive. Bias can also operate without falsehood through omission, story selection, source selection, placement, and spin. Native advertising blurs editorial and paid content, while container collapse removes the visual cues that once distinguished a government report, news article, advertisement, blog, satire, and scientific paper.

Subtle distortion in science news

A 2011 study of 462 biomedical and health press releases from leading U.K. universities found exaggerated advice, causal claims, or human inference in many releases. Distortion did not begin only with careless journalists; it could enter through the institutional press office and then be amplified by headlines and repetition. Information quality is a production-chain outcome.

Useful verification practices include leaving the feed to inspect the original source, checking publication date and evidence, distinguishing a causal study from a correlation, comparing independent coverage, and tracing images or quotes to context. Triangulation is stronger than accepting or rejecting a claim solely because it matches prior belief.

Generative AI as a sociotechnical system

Generative AI is a medium, a production technology, and an institutional actor delegated tasks by humans. It can lower the cost of translation, tutoring, coding, creative experimentation, and accessibility. It can also generate plausible falsehoods, reproduce patterns in training data, obscure authorship, enable impersonation, and shift work from visible creators to hidden data labelers, moderators, and reviewers.

In 2025, 21% of U.S. workers told Pew that at least some of their work was done with AI, up from 16% a year earlier. Adoption is not an effect by itself. Outcomes depend on which task is automated, whether AI complements or substitutes for expertise, who verifies output, how productivity gains are divided, what data are used, and whether affected people can appeal.

Automation or augmentation?

A writing system may help a novice generate fluent prose while hiding weak reasoning; it may help an expert test alternatives while the expert catches errors. If an organization increases workload after introducing the same tool, time savings may not become worker leisure. Technology’s consequence is jointly produced by capability, expertise, management, and labor power.

Comparing the major perspectives

PerspectiveCentral questionTypical mechanisms
FunctionalismWhat manifest and latent functions or dysfunctions do media perform?Coordination, information, education, entertainment, socialization, warning, overload, disinformation, disrupted relationships.
Conflict and political economyWho owns infrastructure, controls visibility, extracts data, and captures value?Concentration, advertising, labor control, surveillance, digital divides, cultural imperialism, resistance.
Symbolic interactionismHow do people construct identities and meanings through mediated encounters?Framing, impression management, labeling, context collapse, interpretation, negotiated use.
Sociotechnical constructionHow do users, designers, laws, organizations, and material affordances jointly shape a system?Design conflict, standards, path dependence, feedback, appropriation, institutionalization.

The perspectives can analyze the same technology without being interchangeable. For digital surveillance, functionalism identifies safety or efficiency and possible dysfunctions; conflict theory maps asymmetric observation and control; interactionism examines how being watched changes self-presentation; sociotechnical analysis traces how specific data fields, thresholds, procurement rules, and user adaptations produce the system.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Technologies are sociotechnical arrangements shaped by material affordances, users, designers, organizations, law, culture, and power.
  • Technological determinism and social-construction approaches emphasize different causal directions.
  • Media select, frame, rank, and circulate reality rather than simply reflecting it.
  • Public-sphere, market, and platform models identify different purposes and incentives.
  • Agenda-setting, framing, priming, cultivation, social learning, uses and gratifications, and encoding/decoding specify different media mechanisms.
  • Digital interaction extends impression management, context collapse, identity work, and relationships across distance.
  • The digital divide includes access, devices, skills, quality, meaningful use, and outcomes.
  • Ownership, advertising, platform governance, surveillance, and algorithms distribute visibility and power unevenly.
  • Echo chambers and filter bubbles are empirical hypotheses, not automatic consequences of personalization.
  • Misinformation, disinformation, and subtler forms of selection or exaggeration should be distinguished.
  • Generative AI can automate or augment work depending on task, expertise, verification, ownership, and organizational choices.
  • Functionalist, conflict, interactionist, and sociotechnical perspectives can illuminate the same system at different levels.

Questions for study

  1. Why is technology more than a neutral tool?
  2. How do technological determinism and social construction differ?
  3. How do public-sphere and market models evaluate journalism differently?
  4. What is the difference between agenda-setting and framing?
  5. How does encoding/decoding preserve both producer influence and audience agency?
  6. How do digital platforms change the meaning of audience?
  7. Why can expanding information widen a knowledge gap?
  8. Who benefits from and who is exposed to the risks of data collection?
  9. What evidence would establish an echo chamber?
  10. Why is misinformation not the same as disinformation?
  11. Under what conditions does AI augment rather than displace expertise?
  12. Apply all four perspectives to one platform or technology.
