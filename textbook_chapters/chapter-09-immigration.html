Immigration

How migration, law, labor markets, race, ethnicity, and transnational ties have shaped the United States.

Immigration is a movement of people, but it is also a transformation of families, communities, economies, racial categories, and political boundaries.

A migration decision might begin with a job loss, political danger, a university acceptance, or a call from a relative abroad. Yet an individual cannot migrate on desire alone. Visas, money, transportation, employers, border controls, family networks, and beliefs about possible futures turn some aspirations into movement and block others. The receiving society changes too: migrants work and consume, create organizations, sustain families across borders, enter political debates, and are classified through laws that define who belongs. This chapter follows that reciprocal process from U.S. history to theories of migration, incorporation, and contemporary policy.

A four-era history

U.S. immigration history can be organized into broad eras rather than a single continuous story. Early immigration was dominated by migrants from Northwest Europe, including Irish, German, and Scandinavian populations. A later era brought larger numbers from Southern and Eastern Europe. Restrictionist legislation then established quotas and exclusions. Since 1965, immigration has increasingly involved Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East.

Concept: foreign-born population

People living in the United States who were not U.S. citizens at birth. This includes naturalized citizens, lawful permanent residents, temporary migrants, and people without authorized status.

Each era produced anxiety among some native-born groups. The targets changed—from Irish Catholics and Germans to Italians, Jews, Chinese, Japanese, Mexicans, and other groups—but the language of threat often repeated. Immigrants were accused of taking jobs, refusing assimilation, changing the national language, or weakening national identity.

Era 1: expansion, settlement, and selective openness

From the nation’s founding through the mid-nineteenth century, federal limits were light for many Europeans while citizenship and belonging were racially restricted. Irish migration grew sharply during the Great Famine, and political upheaval, religious conflict, land scarcity, industrial jobs, and inexpensive farmland drew German and Scandinavian migrants. Chinese migrants entered western mining, agriculture, and railroad construction. Openness was therefore never universal: Indigenous dispossession, slavery, the racial limits of naturalization, and violence against Chinese communities formed part of the same expansion.

Era 2: mass migration and exclusion, 1875–1920

Industrialization drew millions from Southern, Central, and Eastern Europe, including Italians, Poles, Russians, and Jews escaping persecution. Cities, mines, factories, and construction projects absorbed labor, while ethnic institutions helped newcomers find housing, work, worship, mutual aid, and political representation. Restriction also became national policy. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese labor immigration, and the 1907 Gentlemen’s Agreement sharply limited Japanese labor migration. Nativist arguments joined racial hierarchy, labor competition, religion, and fears about self-government.

Era 3: national-origins restriction, 1921–1964

Quota laws in the 1920s favored Northern and Western European origins and restricted groups considered less assimilable. The system reduced overall immigration and institutionalized a racialized image of the desired nation. Yet migration did not stop: Mexican labor was recruited and expelled in changing economic conditions, refugees confronted narrow admissions, and the Bracero Program brought temporary Mexican workers during and after World War II. Enforcement and labor demand repeatedly moved together rather than forming two separate stories.

Era 4: the post-1965 system

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 abolished the national-origins quota structure and emphasized family and employment preferences. Its consequences exceeded many lawmakers’ expectations as earlier immigrants sponsored relatives and migration expanded from Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East. Refugee policy, employer demand, unauthorized migration, temporary visas, and later enforcement laws created a diverse system rather than a single gateway.

The repeating anxiety—and the changing structure

Benjamin Franklin worried in the eighteenth century that German-speaking migrants would not adopt the language and customs of Pennsylvania. Later opponents directed similar claims toward Irish, Italian, Jewish, Asian, and Latin American newcomers. Recurring rhetoric does not mean every era is identical. Today’s visa system, welfare state, surveillance capacity, global travel, racial categories, and labor market differ substantially. Historical analogy is useful when it identifies both repetition and changed conditions.

Push and pull factors

Migration decisions are shaped by push factors that make departure more likely, such as persecution, war, famine, unemployment, land scarcity, or political instability. Pull factors attract migrants, including jobs, safety, education, family reunification, and political freedom.

This framework is useful but incomplete. Migration is also shaped by policy, racial classification, transportation, recruiters, employers, family networks, and the uneven distribution of opportunity between countries.

Why migration begins and continues

No theory explains every stream. Some explain why an individual moves; others explain why employers recruit, why particular countries become connected, or why movement continues after its original cause weakens.

Neoclassical and human-capital theory

A neoclassical account treats migration as an investment: people compare expected earnings and opportunities across places with the monetary, psychological, and legal costs of moving. Age, education, language, and risk affect the expected return. The approach explains why wage differences matter and why migrants are often positively or negatively selected rather than representative of their origin population.

Its limit is an overly isolated decision-maker. People may lack reliable information or legal access, and households rather than individuals often organize migration. The largest wage gap in the world produces little movement when routes, money, and networks are absent.

New economics of labor migration

This approach treats migration as a household strategy for diversifying risk. One member may migrate so a family has income that does not depend entirely on a local harvest, employer, or credit market. Remittances can finance daily needs, education, housing, or business investment. Migration may therefore occur even when the migrant’s own immediate wage gain is modest.

The theory clarifies why obligations and bargaining inside families matter. It also asks who bears separation and care work, who controls remittances, and whether migration redistributes authority between generations or genders.

Dual labor markets and segmented demand

Michael Piore argued that immigration is pulled by the structure of advanced economies. Employers need workers not only in highly skilled occupations but also in unstable, low-status jobs with limited advancement. Native-born workers may reject some positions because a job’s status matters as well as its wage, while migrants may evaluate earnings in relation to goals or status in the origin community.

This theory shifts attention from migrants who allegedly “take” jobs to employers and job design. It can understate migrants’ agency and the ways workers later seek mobility, organize, or leave the sector.

World-systems theory

Global investment, trade, colonial relationships, military intervention, and commercial agriculture can disrupt livelihoods while connecting peripheral regions to wealthier centers. The same flows of goods, capital, communication, and institutions that transform an origin society can create routes for people. Migration is then part of an expanding transnational political economy, not simply a response to timeless poverty.

The approach explains why movement follows specific historical links—former colonies often send migrants to former imperial states—but broad structural ties do not predict which household or person will leave.

Networks and cumulative causation

Douglas Massey and colleagues show how migration changes the conditions that cause later migration. Earlier movers provide information, temporary housing, job contacts, and practical help, reducing later movers’ costs and risks. Employers develop recruitment practices; businesses serve a transnational population; and communities reorganize around expected migration. A self-sustaining network can persist even after the original wage or political trigger changes.

This is cumulative causation: migration emerges from social structure and then creates new social structure. Networks expand access, but they can also channel newcomers into the same low-wage sectors and make opportunity depend on which connections a family happens to possess.

Law and the boundary of belonging

The Chinese Exclusion Act, the Gentlemen’s Agreement, national-origins quotas, and the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 show that immigration policy has never been separate from race. The post-1965 preference system gives priority to family reunification and employment categories, but annual limits and per-country caps create long waits and unequal access.

Legal status shapes work, housing, education, mobility, safety, and family life. Undocumented status is not simply a personal condition; it is produced by the relationship between demand for labor, family migration, border enforcement, visa limits, and naturalization rules.

A legal-status spectrum

“Immigrant” is not one legal status. U.S. citizens by naturalization possess a different bundle of rights from lawful permanent residents; temporary students and workers enter under nonimmigrant visas; refugees are selected abroad while asylum seekers request protection at or after arrival; and unauthorized residents may have entered without inspection or remained after an authorized period expired. Some people hold temporary protection or pending claims. Status can change, and mixed-status families can include citizens and noncitizens under one roof.

Law has constitutive power: it does not merely police a population that already exists. It creates categories, waiting periods, dependencies, and vulnerabilities. An employer-sponsored visa can tie mobility to a job; a backlog can postpone family formation; limited legal pathways can coexist with persistent labor demand. At the same time, states have legitimate responsibilities to decide admission rules, verify claims, secure borders, and maintain public confidence. The sociological debate concerns how rules distribute the costs of enforcement and how closely legal pathways match family, humanitarian, and labor realities.

Despite these differences, immigrants in different eras have often entered the labor market through low-wage work, formed ethnic enclaves, experienced discrimination, and negotiated pressures to assimilate. Immigration is therefore both a story of difference and a story of recurring social processes.

A current portrait: scale and diversity

The Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey five-year estimates counted about 47.35 million foreign-born residents. This statistical category includes naturalized citizens and noncitizens and should not be confused with the unauthorized population. The number is historically large, but population share offers a different comparison: the foreign-born share was also high during the early twentieth-century mass-migration era and reached a twentieth-century low around 1970. Whether immigration appears “unprecedented” therefore depends on whether one means number, percentage, origins, legal context, or pace of change.

Contemporary immigrants are internally polarized in education and occupation. U.S. firms and universities attract scientists, engineers, health professionals, entrepreneurs, and students, while agriculture, construction, care work, hospitality, food processing, and other sectors recruit or absorb workers with less formal schooling. National-origin averages conceal class, gender, generation, legal status, and selection differences. Treating “immigrants” as one economic type produces weak analysis.

Ethnic enclaves: shelter and constraint

An enclave can provide jobs, credit, customers, language access, cultural familiarity, and protection from hostility. It can also concentrate workers in low-wage niches, expose them to coethnic exploitation, or slow contact with wider institutions. Choice and constraint interact: people may value living near kin while also confronting discrimination or unaffordable alternatives. The effect depends on an enclave’s resources, industries, institutions, and links beyond the group.

Assimilation, segmented assimilation, and transnationalism

From straight-line assimilation to multiple pathways

Classical straight-line assimilation theory expected cultural differences and social distance to decline across generations. Language shift, intermarriage, residential mobility, and occupational advancement among many European-origin groups seemed to support the model. Richard Alba and Victor Nee later described assimilation as the decline of an ethnic distinction and its associated social differences, not necessarily the disappearance of every tradition.

Alejandro Portes and Min Zhou’s segmented assimilation theory argues that the second generation can follow different paths: upward incorporation into the middle class, downward mobility in disadvantaged settings, or selective acculturation that combines economic advancement with supportive community ties. Family resources, discrimination, legal reception, schools, neighborhoods, and the structure of available jobs shape the path.

Language illustrates both continuity and loss. Adult arrivals often remain most fluent in an origin language; many children become bilingual and English-dominant; later generations frequently become English monolingual. This pattern contradicts a simple claim that contemporary immigrants never learn English, but it also raises a different question: whether rapid language assimilation sacrifices useful bilingual skills and communication with older relatives.

Transnationalism

Peggy Levitt, Nina Glick Schiller, and other scholars use transnationalism for sustained practices linking migrants with more than one national society. Remittances, political participation, religious organizations, care, entrepreneurship, and digital communication can span borders. “Social remittances”—ideas, habits, and expectations carried through these ties—can influence gender roles, politics, education, and organizations in origin communities.

Transnational belonging does not necessarily prevent incorporation. Earlier migrants also wrote home, sent money, returned, and practiced circular migration; contemporary technologies make ties faster and more routine. People can become civically rooted in one country while maintaining family and cultural obligations in another.

The immigrant paradox and generational change

Some first-generation immigrant groups show unexpectedly favorable health, family, or crime outcomes despite low income and stressful work—a pattern called the immigrant paradox. Selection into migration, family and community support, and cultural practices may contribute. Advantages often weaken across generations as descendants encounter U.S. diets, institutions, inequality, and peer environments. The pattern cautions against equating assimilation with improvement in every domain.

Policy debates: jobs, crime, public budgets, and cohesion

Immigration debates combine empirical predictions with moral and political principles. Evidence can estimate labor-market or fiscal effects, but it cannot by itself decide how a nation should weigh growth, distribution, humanitarian protection, family unity, legality, cultural continuity, and sovereignty.

Do immigrants take jobs or expand the economy?

Labor is both a cost of production and a source of demand, entrepreneurship, taxation, and complementary skill. Immigration can place downward pressure on wages for workers who compete most directly with new arrivals while increasing demand for workers whose skills complement them. Effects vary by education, location, time horizon, employer power, and the economy’s ability to invest and expand. The Congressional Budget Office’s analysis of the 2021–2026 immigration surge projected slightly slower short-run wage growth for U.S. workers with twelve or fewer years of education, little initial change for more-educated workers, and small longer-run wage gains as investment and innovation raised productivity. It also projected a larger economy. National gains can coexist with concentrated local costs, so distribution and adjustment policy matter.

Immigration and crime

Political salience can make individual crimes seem representative of a whole population. The National Academies’ broad review concluded that immigrants are less likely than the native-born to commit crime and that higher immigration is generally associated with lower crime, although rates tend to converge toward native patterns across generations. This group-level evidence does not excuse any offense or settle border policy. It shows why claims about population risk require denominators and systematic comparisons rather than memorable cases.

Taxes, services, and levels of government

Fiscal effects depend on age, earnings, education, legal eligibility, children, and whether the analyst examines federal, state, local, or lifetime balances. Working-age migrants pay income, payroll, sales, and property taxes directly or through rent; governments also finance schooling, health care, administration, and infrastructure. CBO projected that the recent surge would lower cumulative federal deficits over 2024–2034 because additional revenues and economy-wide effects exceeded federal mandatory spending and interest costs. Separate CBO analysis found pressure on state and local budgets in 2023, illustrating that a positive federal balance can coexist with short-run local costs. “Immigration pays for itself” and “immigrants are a fiscal burden” are both too broad without a population, time period, and level of government.

Integration and a common civic life

One position emphasizes shared language, constitutional rules, civic knowledge, and allegiance as conditions for trust and democratic coordination. Another warns that calls for assimilation can demand cultural erasure or disguise unequal access to full membership. These concerns are not mutually exclusive. Institutions can teach a common civic framework while protecting religious and cultural freedom; migrants can adapt while receiving societies revise boundaries that once excluded earlier groups.

Evaluate a policy in four dimensions

Ask how a proposal affects (1) selection—who may enter; (2) status—what rights, duties, and path to permanence they receive; (3) enforcement—how rules are implemented and at whose cost; and (4) incorporation—how schools, labor markets, communities, and civic institutions integrate residents. A policy can be strict on one dimension and generous on another.

A sociological checklist for immigration claims

  1. Define the population. Foreign-born, naturalized, lawful permanent resident, refugee, temporary visa holder, asylum seeker, and unauthorized resident are not interchangeable.
  2. Choose the denominator. A record number may coexist with a familiar population share; an increase in total crimes may coexist with a lower per-person rate.
  3. Specify time and place. Short-run effects in one labor market or school district can differ from national, intergenerational effects.
  4. Separate totals from per-capita outcomes. Immigration nearly always raises total GDP by adding people; effects on GDP per person, wages, housing costs, and public finances require separate analysis.
  5. Identify distribution. Employers, consumers, workers, landlords, migrants, and different governments may experience different benefits and costs.
  6. Distinguish evidence from values. Empirical findings constrain claims, while decisions about admission, enforcement, refuge, family, and citizenship also require political principles.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Immigration changes social, economic, racial, political, and family relationships in both origin and destination societies.
  • Push and pull factors operate within legal, household, employer, network, and global systems.
  • Neoclassical, household, dual-labor-market, world-systems, and network theories explain different stages and levels of migration.
  • Migration can create cumulative causation by building networks and institutions that facilitate later movement.
  • Immigration policy has historically been connected to racial classification, labor demand, and ideas of national belonging.
  • Legal status is institutionally produced and shapes bargaining power, mobility, safety, and family life.
  • Older and newer immigrant groups differ in origin and legal context but share recurring experiences of work, enclave formation, discrimination, transnational ties, and assimilation pressures.
  • Straight-line and segmented assimilation theories predict different pathways of incorporation.
  • Ethnic enclaves and transnational ties can provide resources while also creating constraints.
  • Economic and fiscal effects vary across workers, employers, time horizons, places, and levels of government.
  • Evidence on crime, language, jobs, and budgets should be evaluated with defined populations, denominators, and comparisons.

Questions for study

  1. Why is immigration history best understood through both migration decisions and policy?
  2. How do household and network theories improve on a simple push-pull model?
  3. How can immigration produce cumulative causation?
  4. How does legal status shape everyday life and employer power?
  5. What similarities and differences exist across immigration eras?
  6. How do straight-line and segmented assimilation differ?
  7. When can an ethnic enclave promote mobility, and when can it constrain mobility?
  8. Why can immigration raise total GDP while affecting groups’ wages differently?
  9. Why might federal and local fiscal effects point in different directions?
  10. How can a society combine civic integration with cultural freedom?
