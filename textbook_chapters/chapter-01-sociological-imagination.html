Textbook · Chapter 1

Sociological Imagination

How biography, history, and social context shape the lives we experience as personal—and why sociology begins by looking beyond the individual.

Opening question

What can the price, location, and history of a house tell us about the people who live there? More than we might expect. A house is a physical structure, but it is also an address inside a network of schools, jobs, transportation, public services, relationships, expectations, and unequal opportunities.

Imagine two homes with similar bedrooms, bathrooms, and square footage. One is near a strong school district, stable employment, public transportation, and expanding services. The other is farther from those resources, in a neighborhood shaped by disinvestment or limited opportunity. The homes may look similar in a listing, yet their market values—and the lives organized around them—can be very different. The difference is not simply the building. It is where the building is located.

This is the lesson of “location, location, location.” A house is not valued solely by what it is; its location connects it to resources, risks, relationships, and opportunities beyond its walls. Sociology extends that insight to human life. To understand people, we must ask not only who they are or what choices they make, but also where they are socially located: in a family, neighborhood, school, workplace, class position, racialized system, gender order, historical period, and stage of the life course. Social location does not determine a person’s life mechanically, but it shapes the possibilities and constraints within which biography unfolds.

Core idea

Sociology studies the reciprocal relationship between human action and social order. It examines how social relationships, institutions, historical conditions, and cultural meanings organize human life, and how people, through individual and collective action and interaction, create, reproduce, challenge, and transform those arrangements. Sociology connects individual experiences to larger patterns while also asking how those patterns emerge and change.

1. From a personal experience to a sociological question

Everyday life presents us with experiences that feel immediate and personal: finding a job, paying rent, choosing a school, caring for a family member, feeling lonely, or deciding whether to continue an education. Personal experience matters. Sociology does not dismiss what people feel or do. Instead, it asks what else must be known in order to understand that experience fully.

A sociological question places the individual inside a wider setting. If one person cannot find work, we might ask about training, transportation, health, discrimination, or the person’s decisions. If unemployment rises across an entire region, we also ask about factory closures, economic policy, technology, trade, or the organization of labor. The individual story remains important, but it becomes one part of a larger explanation.

Example: from house location to social location

The market value of a home is not determined only by its walls and rooms. Location connects a household to schools, employers, tax bases, environmental risks, social networks, and public investment. The same principle helps us study people: social location links private biography to public arrangements.

2. What is society?

The word society refers to more than a collection of individuals. A society is an organized pattern of relationships, institutions, practices, meanings, and shared history that emerges from people’s repeated interactions and coordinated actions. Families, schools, workplaces, governments, markets, religious organizations, media systems, neighborhoods, and informal networks exist because people create roles, follow or challenge expectations, cooperate, compete, and respond to one another. Society is therefore made through human action rather than existing independently of it.

Once these patterns become established, however, they confront individuals as social arrangements that already exist. People are born into languages, laws, inequalities, organizations, and cultural expectations they did not personally choose. These arrangements make some actions possible, make others difficult, distribute resources and opportunities unevenly, and shape how people understand their choices. Everyday actions can reproduce this social order, but collective action, conflict, innovation, and changing interpretations can also transform it. Society thus both emerges from human action and enables or constrains the actions that follow.

Concept: social context

The surrounding relationships, institutions, cultural meanings, historical circumstances, and material conditions that give an individual experience its shape and significance.

Auguste Comte: naming a new science of society

Auguste Comte (1798–1857), a French thinker, helped establish the word sociology in the nineteenth century. He argued that social life could be studied systematically rather than treated only as a matter of philosophical speculation or moral opinion. Comte lived during a period of political upheaval and rapid economic change after the French Revolution, when industrialization and urban growth were unsettling older forms of authority and community.

Comte held a highly optimistic vision of the new discipline. He believed that scientific knowledge of society could restore order and guide moral progress, eventually replacing religion as society’s main source of unity. In his proposed secular “Religion of Humanity,” sociologists would replace traditional priests as moral guides and serve as a new positivist priesthood. This ambition proved unrealistic. Scientific evidence can clarify social conditions and likely consequences, but it cannot by itself resolve conflicts over values, determine a single moral order, or justify placing sociologists above democratic debate. Later sociologists rejected much of Comte’s grand program, while retaining his influential claim that society can be studied systematically and empirically.

3. Why sociology emerged in the nineteenth century

Sociology developed as a formal discipline during a period when familiar social arrangements were changing quickly. Industrialization moved production from households and small workshops into factories. Work became increasingly organized around wages, machines, schedules, and large-scale production. Urbanization brought growing numbers of people into cities, where strangers lived near one another and social problems became highly visible.

These changes altered family life, work, neighborhoods, politics, and ideas about progress. In a pre-industrial pattern, a farm or cottage might combine residence and work, and a large family might contribute labor to the household economy. In a post-industrial pattern, factories, cities, smaller households, and a separation between paid employment and domestic life became more common. These are broad patterns rather than universal rules, but they show why sociologists compare social arrangements across time.

The development of science also mattered. As scientific methods gained authority in the nineteenth century, scholars increasingly asked whether observation, comparison, evidence, and systematic explanation could be applied to social life. Sociology emerged from this intersection of social transformation and confidence in scientific inquiry.

From European foundations to American institutions

Sociology’s institutional history did not follow a single straight line. European theories and university programs influenced American departments, but American sociology also grew through settlement houses, historically Black institutions, investigative journalism, reform movements, and research by scholars who were often excluded from full recognition in predominantly white and male universities. The timeline traces these intersecting lineages.

  1. 1887–1895 Europe · Bordeaux

    Durkheim helps institutionalize sociology

    Émile Durkheim began teaching social science and pedagogy at the University of Bordeaux in 1887, bringing sociology into the French university system. By 1895, his teaching program and The Rules of Sociological Method had helped establish sociology as a distinct academic field; his work at Bordeaux is commonly associated with Europe’s first university program or department in sociology.

  2. 1889–1895 United States · Chicago

    Women build a civic and empirical sociology

    Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull-House in 1889. Addams, Florence Kelley, Julia Lathrop, and other residents combined neighborhood relationships, observation, surveys, maps, and reform. Their Hull-House Maps and Papers (1895) documented wages, nationalities, labor, and living conditions, demonstrating that sociological research could be created with communities and used to challenge inequality.

  3. 1892 United States · Departments and excluded voices

    American sociology develops along parallel paths

    The University of Chicago established the first sociology department in the United States under Albion Small. In the same year, Anna Julia Cooper’s A Voice from the South analyzed the linked positions of Black women, while Ida B. Wells’s Southern Horrors used evidence to expose the social organization and false justifications of lynching. Sociology was being formed both inside universities and by Black and female intellectuals working beyond their boundaries.

  4. 1895–1898 Transatlantic exchange

    Journals and new theories create a field

    Albion Small founded the American Journal of Sociology in 1895, with Durkheim among its international advisers. Durkheim organized L’Année sociologique, whose first volume appeared in 1898. That same year, Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s Women and Economics examined how economic dependence and domestic arrangements shaped gender inequality. Publications carried sociological ideas across institutions and national borders.

  5. 1897–1899 United States · Atlanta and Philadelphia

    Du Bois establishes an empirical sociology of race

    W. E. B. Du Bois joined Atlanta University in 1897 and helped direct a sustained program of research on Black communities. In The Philadelphia Negro (1899), Du Bois, with research assistance from Isabel Eaton, used surveys, interviews, mapping, and historical evidence to explain how discrimination and neighborhood conditions shaped social life. His work challenged biological racism and established a major empirical tradition that the white academic mainstream long marginalized.

  6. 1905 United States · National organization

    The American Sociological Society is founded

    Sociologists meeting in Baltimore created the American Sociological Society, giving the expanding discipline a national professional organization. The organization later adopted the name American Sociological Association in 1959. Its creation signaled sociology’s growing academic legitimacy, although access to recognition and leadership remained unequal.

  7. 1936 United States · Professional consolidation

    American Sociological Review begins publication

    The national association launched American Sociological Review as a journal for research of broad disciplinary importance. Its founding marked the consolidation of an American professional infrastructure built from European intellectual influences, university departments, empirical community studies, and lines of scholarship developed by people whom the formal institutions often excluded.

Timeline sources: the University of Bordeaux, the University of Chicago, the Library of Congress collections on Hull-House, Anna Julia Cooper, Ida B. Wells, and The Philadelphia Negro, the Smithsonian’s documentation of the Atlanta University studies, and the American Sociological Association’s institutional history.

Early sociologists and competing explanations

Karl Marx analyzed capitalism, class conflict, labor, and the relationship between economic organization and social power. Émile Durkheim studied social integration, institutions, and the forces that hold societies together. Max Weber examined meaning, authority, bureaucracy, religion, and the ways people interpret their actions. Other early scholars—including Herbert Spencer, Georg Simmel, W. E. B. Du Bois, and Jane Addams—contributed important analyses of social organization, urban life, race, reform, and inequality.

These thinkers did not agree about what society is or how it changes. That disagreement is productive. Sociology is not one single theory; it is a field of questions and methods for explaining patterned social life.

4. The sociological imagination

The phrase sociological imagination was developed by the American sociologist C. Wright Mills. It names the capacity to connect personal experience with larger social conditions. A person using sociological imagination asks how biography—the events and circumstances of an individual life—intersects with history—the broader stream of events, institutions, and transformations through which that life unfolds.

Concept: sociological imagination

The ability to see the relationship between personal troubles and public issues, and to understand biography in connection with history and social structure.

This is more than “thinking socially.” It is a disciplined shift in perspective. We move back and forth between a person’s immediate experience and the structures that organize the experience. We ask what is distinctive about one case, what is patterned across many cases, and how historical conditions make some outcomes more likely than others.

Personal troubles and public issues

Mills’s distinction becomes clearest when we compare one person’s situation with a widespread pattern. If one person is unemployed, the immediate problem may involve a personal trouble: a résumé, a missed opportunity, a health condition, or a strained relationship. But if millions of people cannot find work during a recession, or if an entire industry closes in a region, the problem is also a public issue. It is connected to institutions, markets, policy decisions, and historical change.

Personal trouble

A difficulty experienced in the context of an individual’s immediate relationships and biography.

Public issue

A problem rooted in social arrangements and experienced across a significant portion of a population.

The distinction is not absolute. A personal trouble can be intensified by a public issue, and a public issue is always experienced through individual lives. The analytical task is to determine how the two levels connect.

History and biography

History is not merely a list of dates that happened before the present. It is the changing context in which people form identities, make decisions, and encounter opportunities or barriers. A war, depression, migration wave, technological change, or policy reform can affect people differently depending on their age, family position, race, class, gender, location, and institutions available to them.

Biography is not merely a private story detached from history. The life course has timing. Being a child during an economic crisis is different from entering the labor market during that crisis. Graduating from high school before or after a war, a recession, or a major policy change can alter the choices available at the next stage of life.

Mills’s framework can be summarized as a relationship among biography, social structure, and history. Biography concerns the experiences and choices of a particular life. Social structure concerns the institutions and patterned relationships within which those choices are made. History concerns how those institutions and relationships developed and changed over time. The sociological imagination moves among all three rather than treating individuals as detached from the world that formed their opportunities, expectations, and constraints.

This way of thinking gives Mills’s sociology its radical edge. It interrupts the tendency to interpret every hardship as a private failure and opens existing institutions and distributions of power to investigation. The sequence of questions becomes: What appears to be “my problem”? How is the relevant part of society organized? Who has power within that organization? Could it be organized differently? Sociology therefore should not merely describe social arrangements; it should make their origins, consequences, and alternatives visible.

This orientation is strongly compatible with Marx’s insistence that people live within historically created social and economic relations. Marx gave Mills sensitivity to capitalism, class, inequality, alienation, and structural power. Yet the sociological imagination is not itself an orthodox Marxist concept: it does not assume that capitalism or class explains every outcome. Mills also drew heavily on Weber’s analyses of bureaucracy, organizations, and authority. His approach is radical because it challenges purely individualistic explanations, but it remains open to multiple structures, causes, and forms of power.

C. Wright Mills: biography, history, and public issues

C. Wright Mills (1916–1962) was an American sociologist whose work challenged narrow, overly technical approaches to social research. He wrote in a period marked by the Great Depression’s legacy, World War II, the expansion of mass organizations, the Cold War, and the growth of professional and bureaucratic power in the United States.

In The Sociological Imagination (1959), Mills argued that sociology should help people understand the relationship between their lives and the historical forces around them. He distinguished between personal troubles, which occur within an individual’s immediate relationships and biography, and public issues, which reflect arrangements in institutions and affect many people. Mills also criticized the separation of “grand theory” from concrete evidence: explanation should be historically informed, empirically grounded, and relevant to public life.

Mills’s other major works examined the concentration of power among political, corporate, and military leaders; the organization of white-collar work; and the effects of bureaucratic institutions on ordinary people. He was strongly influenced by Marxism, especially Marx’s analyses of capitalism, class, alienation, and historical change, although he combined these ideas with Weber’s analysis of bureaucracy and authority, American pragmatism, and elite theory rather than adopting an orthodox or economically deterministic Marxism. In his “Letter to the New Left” (1960), Mills directly encouraged an emerging generation of radical students and intellectuals to become agents of democratic change. His criticism of Cold War power, bureaucratic conformity, and complacent academic sociology made his work an important resource for the developing New Left. Mills was therefore an important forerunner and intellectual bridge to the radical and neo-Marxist turn in American sociology during the late 1960s and 1970s. His central contribution was not a formula for blaming society instead of individuals, but a method for asking better questions about how individual agency and social structure meet.

Applying the sociological imagination

Student debt illustrates the movement among biography, social structure, and history. At the level of biography, a graduate may experience anxiety over owing $50,000 and may reconsider earlier borrowing decisions. At the level of social structure, sociologists examine tuition, universities, lenders, family wealth, labor markets, and public policy. At the level of history, they ask how the cost and financing of higher education changed across generations. The debt is personally experienced, institutionally organized, and historically produced at the same time.

Example: education and timing

A student who leaves school may describe the decision as personal. Sociological analysis also asks about tuition, family income, school quality, transportation, labor-market expectations, public policy, and the historical period in which the student came of age. The goal is not to erase responsibility; it is to identify the conditions within which responsibility is exercised.

5. A cohort study: the Great Depression and different life courses

Glen H. Elder, Jr. and the life-course perspective

Glen H. Elder, Jr. is a sociologist and a leading figure in the development of life-course theory and research. His best-known study, Children of the Great Depression: Social Change in Life Experience, first published in 1974 and expanded in a 1999 anniversary edition, followed 167 people born in Oakland, California, in 1920–1921 from childhood into the adult years. By using longitudinal evidence—information collected about the same people repeatedly over time—Elder could observe two processes unfolding together: individuals were growing older and changing, while the economy, families, institutions, public policies, and national history around them were also changing.

Imagine a person traveling through two connected tunnels at once. The long hallway is historical time: everyone moves through the Great Depression, World War II, and the postwar period in the same calendar order. Within that hallway, the moving walkway is age: childhood, adolescence, and adulthood carry people forward through changing capacities, dependencies, roles, and institutions. Birth cohorts enter this dual passage at different points. Consequently, members of different cohorts encounter the same historical events at different ages, when they are differently prepared to act and differently vulnerable to disruption.

Elder’s life-course perspective asks us to study lives within this interaction of historical time and place, timing, linked lives, and human agency. Age is not merely a biological number; it helps determine what an event can mean, how strongly it enters family and individual life, and which responses and opportunities are possible. This perspective also changes how we understand resilience. Resilience is not simply a fixed personal trait. It can develop when the timing of later relationships, institutions, and public policies opens pathways that redirect a life. Elder’s research therefore joins biography to history by examining how people change within changing social conditions.

Elder’s study illustrates the dual movement of age and history through a comparison of two California cohorts. The Oakland cohort was born around 1920–1921; the Berkeley cohort was born around 1928–1929. Both moved through the same sequence of historical events—the relative prosperity of the 1920s, the Great Depression, World War II, and the postwar transition—but they reached each event at different ages. The Oakland children spent early childhood in the 1920s and encountered the Depression as older children and adolescents. They then reached young adulthood during wartime mobilization, when military service, work, marriage, and later veterans’ benefits could become important turning points. The Berkeley children encountered the Depression in early childhood, reached adolescence during World War II, and entered young adulthood during the postwar period. Their cohort position therefore created a different schedule of exposure, dependency, opportunity, and transition.

The distinction is not that one cohort suffered while the other escaped hardship. Both were shaped by the same changing history, but age altered how that history entered their lives. Younger children depended more completely on family resources and were especially vulnerable when economic loss disrupted parents and households. Older children and adolescents could understand the crisis differently, contribute labor, leave school, or assume new family responsibilities. During World War II, the Oakland cohort was old enough for military and adult work roles, while the Berkeley cohort was still moving through school and adolescence. The same event could therefore create vulnerability at one life stage and, through different institutions encountered later, the possibility of resilience at another.

Unexpectedly, many Oakland participants followed a trajectory of resilience into the middle years of life. Their earlier disadvantages did not disappear, but the timing of their passage into young adulthood placed them at the intersection of wartime mobilization and expanding postwar institutions. Three kinds of opportunity were especially important: higher education, marriage as a source of support during young adulthood, and military service. These experiences reinforced one another rather than operating as isolated benefits. Military service could provide training, income, new social networks, and contact with potential spouses. It could also connect veterans to the educational and housing opportunities created by the 1944 GI Bill of Rights. In Elder’s evidence, nearly half of the California veterans reported completing an educational degree through GI Bill benefits.

The effects were especially consequential for people whose earlier social locations had limited their options. Because members of the Oakland cohort reached military age when wartime service and postwar programs were available, some veterans who had entered the service young or had a history of delinquency gained access to training, credentials, and relationships that altered their later occupational possibilities. These later experiences were associated with higher job status, greater job stability, and improved economic well-being into the middle years, independent of some childhood differences and socioeconomic origins. Their resilience was therefore neither purely individual nor guaranteed by cohort membership. It emerged from the interaction between personal action, age at exposure, linked relationships, and institutions available at a particular historical moment.

This does not mean that war itself was beneficial, or that every veteran experienced the same outcome. It means that historically specific institutions can rearrange the opportunity structure. Elder’s broader lesson is that societies should not need a war to create pathways to education, training, mobility, and adult stability. A sociologist therefore asks not only how individuals cope, but also which public arrangements make coping more or less possible.

Concept: cohort

A group of people who experience a significant event or historical period at roughly the same stage of life and whose shared timing may shape their later experiences.

The comparison demonstrates why age, history, and biography must be considered together. “The Great Depression affected children” is true but incomplete. A sociological explanation asks: Which children? At what age? In which families and neighborhoods? With what resources? Which institutions did they encounter next? What opportunities were opened or closed by their location in history?

Another classic example: Émile Durkheim’s Suicide

Glen Elder shows how historical events affect people differently depending on when those events enter the life course. More than a century earlier, Émile Durkheim used a different kind of evidence to make a related sociological argument. In Suicide (1897), Durkheim examined patterns in suicide rates across social groups and argued that suicide could not be understood only as an individual psychological act. The act is personal, but its distribution across groups can reveal the organization of social life.

Émile Durkheim: social integration and regulation

Durkheim was one of the founders of sociology as an empirical discipline. His study of suicide asked why rates varied across religious communities, families, and periods of social change. He treated the suicide rate as a social fact: a patterned feature of social life that exists beyond any single individual and can be studied through evidence.

Two ideas organize Durkheim’s explanation. Social integration refers to the strength of a person’s connections to groups and communities. Social regulation refers to the norms and limits that organize desires and expectations. Durkheim proposed four ideal types: egoistic suicide, associated with insufficient integration; altruistic suicide, associated with excessive integration; anomic suicide, associated with insufficient regulation during periods of disruption or rapid change; and fatalistic suicide, associated with excessive regulation.

These categories should be read as analytical tools, not as labels for individual people. Durkheim’s larger contribution was to shift the question from “What is wrong with this person?” to “What patterns in social relationships, institutions, and norms help explain differences across groups?” That shift is an early example of sociological imagination.

Durkheim’s study illustrates social location in a different way from Elder’s cohort comparison. Elder asks where people are located in historical time and in the life course. Durkheim asks where people are located within networks of belonging and systems of regulation. A person’s family ties, religious community, work situation, and experience of social change can shape the resources, meanings, and constraints surrounding a crisis. These conditions do not mechanically determine an outcome, but they can affect the likelihood that people experience isolation, obligation, direction, or disruption.

Using both examples together

Elder’s children of the Great Depression and Durkheim’s study of suicide examine different questions, methods, and historical periods. Together they teach the same habit of mind: locate the individual experience in a larger pattern. Ask how age, cohort, family, community, institutions, norms, and historical change shape the possibilities people encounter.

Concept: social integration

The degree to which people are connected to social groups and participate in relationships that provide belonging, identity, support, and obligations.

6. Practicing sociological imagination

The sociological imagination can be practiced as a sequence of questions. Begin with the concrete experience, then widen the lens.

  1. Name the experience. What is happening, and how do people describe it?
  2. Locate the setting. Where does it occur, and what institutions or relationships surround it?
  3. Ask who is affected. Is this one person’s situation, a patterned experience, or both?
  4. Compare groups and places. Do outcomes vary by class, race, gender, age, location, or historical period?
  5. Trace the history. What policies, economic changes, conflicts, technologies, or cultural expectations shaped the situation?
  6. Connect biography to structure. How do personal decisions and social conditions interact?

A strong sociological explanation does not stop at “society caused it.” It identifies mechanisms: the rules, resources, institutions, meanings, and relationships through which social conditions influence lives.

Further reading: Glen H. Elder, Jr., Children of the Great Depression; Elder and Rockwell, “The Life-Course and Human Development”; Elder and Caspi, “Human Development and Social Change”; and Émile Durkheim, Suicide. These works extend the chapter’s discussion of historical timing, linked lives, social integration, and resilience.

7. Can sociology be value-neutral?

Sociologists have long disagreed about how the discipline should relate to moral and political values. One orientation seeks to contain values so that sociology can produce credible explanations of social life. Another seeks to acknowledge and engage values, arguing that social inquiry always begins from concerns about what matters and can responsibly contribute to social change. The disagreement is not simply between people who care about society and people who do not. It concerns where values enter research, whether their influence can be controlled, and what responsibilities follow from sociological knowledge.

Concept: value neutrality

The methodological ideal that sociologists should distinguish empirical claims about what is from moral judgments about what ought to be, examine evidence fairly, and prevent personal or political commitments from determining their findings.

Max Weber offered a more subtle position than the phrase value-free sociology sometimes suggests. Values help researchers select topics because no one can study everything; judgments about significance guide attention toward some questions rather than others. Weber called this value relevance. Once an investigation begins, however, researchers should not present their preferred values as if those preferences were scientific findings. Evidence can show how a policy operates, who is affected, and what consequences are likely, but evidence alone cannot decide which ultimate goals society ought to pursue.

A fuller Weberian account: where values enter research

Topic selection is only the beginning. Social reality does not arrive already divided into self-evident concepts and variables. Researchers must decide which similarities matter, which differences can be set aside, where a category begins and ends, and what observable evidence will represent an abstract idea. Weber’s own use of ideal types recognized this problem: an analytical concept accentuates selected features of a complex reality so that researchers can compare cases and explain patterns. The concept can be useful and empirically disciplined without being a photographic copy of the world.

Values and background assumptions can therefore enter at several stages:

  1. Selecting the problem. Why is this subject important enough to study?
  2. Constructing concepts. What will count as power, inequality, religion, family, discrimination, health, or harm?
  3. Operationalizing concepts. Which observations or indicators will stand for the concept, and how will they be combined?
  4. Interpreting evidence. What does an association mean, which mechanisms are plausible, and which rival explanations receive serious attention?
  5. Emphasizing explanations. Which findings appear in the title, abstract, conclusion, and public summary, and which are treated as secondary?
  6. Moving from findings to evaluation. What, if anything, should people or institutions do?

This does not mean that evidence is merely opinion. It means that objectivity requires reflexivity and criticism at every stage. Researchers should disclose how concepts were built, test whether alternative measurements change the result, seek rival explanations, and distinguish what the data show from the moral vocabulary used to evaluate it. Values may help organize inquiry; they should not guarantee its conclusions.

Case study: when does an indicator become “structural sexism”?

Patricia Homan’s “Structural Sexism and Health in the United States”, published in the American Sociological Review in 2019, developed measures of systematic gender inequality at the state, marital, and individual levels and tested their relationships with physical health. At the state level, the index included gender differences in employment, wages, and poverty; men’s representation in state legislatures; women’s geographic access to abortion providers; and the percentage of residents identified as religious conservatives—Evangelical Protestants or members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Homan reported that greater state-level structural sexism was associated with worse health among women and men, while marital-level patterns differed by sex.

The study is useful not only for its findings but also for examining how concepts are made. The percentage of religious conservatives is an observed quantity. Its status as an indicator of structural sexism, however, is not discovered by counting alone. It follows from a theory according to which traditional or complementarian gender arrangements contribute to a gender hierarchy that unequally distributes power and resources. The analytical sequence is therefore not simply:

objective measure of sexism → test its health consequences.

It is more accurately:

theory of what constitutes sexism → selection of indicators → construction of an index → test of its association with health.

That sequence does not invalidate the study. Every index—including measures of socioeconomic status, democracy, religiosity, or neighborhood disadvantage—embeds theoretical decisions. Reflexivity asks whether the label and indicators are defensible, whether different indicators produce similar findings, and whether the index combines arrangements that should be analyzed separately. Because American Sociological Review is the American Sociological Association’s flagship journal, the article also shows that morally charged concept construction is not confined to sociology’s margins. Critics may see its publication as evidence of an egalitarian framework becoming normalized at the discipline’s institutional center. Defenders may see it as evidence that the field takes structural gender inequality seriously. Publication in a leading journal establishes neither interpretation by itself, but it makes the conceptual choices especially important to examine.

A second case: gender, authority, and religious organization

Homan and Amy Burdette’s 2021 “When Religion Hurts” extended the argument to congregations. Using linked data from the General Social Survey and National Congregations Study, they defined congregational structural sexism as systematic gender inequality in power and resources. Their measures asked whether women were barred from serving on governing boards, acting as the primary religious leader, preaching, or teaching mixed-sex religious-education classes. They found that women attending congregations classified as more inclusive reported better health than women attending congregations classified as sexist; the estimated patterns for men were marginal or statistically insignificant.

The term resources requires unpacking. Leadership in a corporation commonly brings salary, career advancement, formal authority, and control over material resources. Congregational roles are more heterogeneous. Pastors, priests, elders, deacons, board members, ministry leaders, teachers, committee members, and volunteers may hold very different combinations of pay, prestige, decision-making authority, liturgical authority, service obligations, and theological responsibility. Many positions are unpaid or minimally compensated. Before treating exclusion from a role as unequal access to resources, a researcher should therefore ask: Which resource is distributed through this office? Salary, institutional authority, symbolic prestige, decision-making power, liturgical authority, and theological responsibility are not interchangeable.

Brendan Case and Tyler VanderWeele’s published response argued that the measures could be described more neutrally as a gender-leadership disparity, because they directly observe sex-specific restrictions on leadership rather than women’s treatment, respect, agency, influence, or well-being within the congregation. They also raised questions about geographic confounding, the cross-sectional design, the measurement of attendance, and how the findings were situated within the wider religion-and-health literature. Their response does not prove that the term structural sexism is wrong. It demonstrates that a concept can carry an evaluative interpretation beyond the observation from which it is built.

Interpretive frameworkThe same observation: only men may serve as senior pastor
Feminist or egalitarianMen have access to an institutional position of authority denied to equally qualified women. Sex-specific exclusion is structural gender inequality and therefore sexism.
Complementarian religiousWomen and men have equal worth but different ecclesiastical responsibilities. Reserving a pastoral office for men expresses a theological interpretation of scripture rather than a judgment that women are inferior.
Neutral organizational descriptionThe congregation has a sex-specific eligibility rule for a particular institutional office. Further evidence is required to determine its authority, resources, consequences, and meaning to participants.

These are not three different observations. They are three interpretations of the same organizational rule. The first two connect the rule to competing conceptions of equality and justice; the third deliberately postpones evaluation. Neutral wording can clarify what was directly observed, but it does not make normative disagreement disappear.

The structural-inequality reply

If women are categorically excluded from institutional offices because they are women, differential access to authority exists regardless of whether members express hostility or whether some women endorse the rule. Structural sexism concerns patterned access to power, not proof that individual participants hate or disrespect women.

The complementarian reply

Calling the rule sexism assumes that justice requires identical eligibility for every religious office. Participants may understand an office as a differentiated obligation rather than a fungible reward. Researchers cannot establish injustice merely by redescribing the egalitarian premise as an empirical finding.

What evidence can and cannot settle

Research can establish what percentage of congregations restrict particular offices; whether women report different health, belonging, influence, or treatment; what authority, compensation, prestige, and obligations attach to each role; whether participants endorse the theology; whether participation is voluntary; whether results survive alternative measures and controls; and which mechanisms connect organizational arrangements to health. Those are empirical questions.

Evidence alone may not settle whether every differentiation of roles by sex is unjust. That judgment depends partly on competing understandings of equality, authority, religious freedom, and the purpose of the organization. A careful Weberian sociology does not avoid the disagreement. It separates observation, causal inference, conceptual interpretation, and moral evaluation so that each can be debated openly.

Concept: sociological reflexivity

The practice of examining how a researcher’s social position, values, concepts, assumptions, and institutional setting shape the production and interpretation of knowledge.

Sources for the case study: Patricia Homan, “Structural Sexism and Health in the United States: A New Perspective on Health Inequality and the Gender System”, American Sociological Review 84(3), 486–516; Patricia Homan and Amy Burdette, “When Religion Hurts: Structural Sexism and Health in Religious Congregations”, American Sociological Review 86(2), 234–255; and Brendan Case and Tyler J. VanderWeele, “Religious Participation and Women’s Health: A Reply to Homan and Burdette.”

Containing values

This orientation protects inquiry from wishful thinking, cherry-picked evidence, partisan pressure, and the temptation to treat moral agreement as proof. William Fielding Ogburn argued that sociology as a science is directly concerned with discovering new knowledge, even though individual sociologists may choose socially useful research problems.

Acknowledging and engaging values

This orientation begins from the fact that researchers are socially situated. Values can influence the problems selected, categories used, data considered important, interpretations developed, and applications pursued. Lester Frank Ward argued that science becomes lifeless when it fails to benefit humanity.

A long-standing divide in American sociology

The tension between scientific detachment and reform did not appear recently. Early American sociology grew partly from abolitionism, the Social Gospel, labor reform, settlement houses, and efforts to understand the consequences of industrialization and urbanization. Jane Addams and the Hull-House researchers combined empirical investigation with social reform. W. E. B. Du Bois used surveys, interviews, maps, and historical analysis not only to produce knowledge but also to challenge racist explanations of inequality. Other sociologists sought disciplinary legitimacy by emphasizing measurement, statistical analysis, explanatory theory, and distance from political advocacy.

During the middle decades of the twentieth century, survey research, foundation support, government projects, and increasingly technical methods strengthened sociology’s scientific identity. Research such as An American Dilemma and The American Soldier also showed that rigorous empirical work could address urgent public problems. The apparent settlement was therefore never a complete separation between science and values. It was an effort to use disciplined research on socially important questions while maintaining credibility across political disagreements.

Why did American sociology become more value-laden and left-leaning?

Jonathan H. Turner, Christian Smith, and Musa al-Gharbi offer overlapping explanations for why American sociology became more activist, morally explicit, and left-leaning. Turner emphasizes the long conflict between scientific and activist sociology; Smith analyzes an institutionalized moral vision that he calls the discipline’s “sacred project”; and al-Gharbi locates the change within the growth and status competition of knowledge-economy professionals whom he calls “symbolic capitalists.” Taken together, their accounts identify several connected developments:

  • An inherited reform tradition. American sociology grew partly from abolitionism, the Social Gospel, settlement houses, labor reform, and efforts to address the disruptions of industrialization and urbanization. The discipline therefore attracted students, scholars, organizations, and publics concerned with social problems from its beginning. Its later activist orientation did not emerge from nowhere; it strengthened a reform impulse that had long coexisted, sometimes uneasily, with the aspiration to build an objective social science.
  • Social movements after the 1960s. The civil rights, antiwar, feminist, labor, environmental, LGBTQ+, and other movements challenged consensus theories and placed inequality, power, discrimination, and social justice at the center of sociological attention. These movements changed the questions scholars asked and encouraged closer relationships between research and political action. Mills’s critical sociology helped prepare this shift, while the subsequent radical and neo-Marxist turn gave conflict-oriented explanations greater influence within American sociology.
  • A changing profession. As universities became more accessible, women, racial and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ scholars, and members of other previously excluded groups entered sociology in greater numbers. Their experiences raised questions that established departments had often overlooked and expanded research on race, gender, sexuality, identity, and intersecting inequalities. This greater inclusion broadened the discipline’s knowledge while also increasing support for scholarship that connected the study of inequality with demands for recognition, reform, and social justice.
  • Public and critical sociology. Sociologists increasingly questioned whether producing academic knowledge was enough. Michael Burawoy’s model of professional, policy, critical, and public sociology called on scholars to examine their discipline’s assumptions, engage organized publics, and support collective action and social-movement mobilization. His election as president of the American Sociological Association in 2004, followed by an annual meeting centered on public sociology, signaled that this activist orientation had moved from the profession’s margins toward its institutional center and was becoming dominant within the ASA.
  • An institutionalized “sacred project.” A moral vision centered on emancipation, equality, personal autonomy, and opposition to oppression, exclusion, and domination became embedded in the discipline. It shaped graduate training, hiring, professional rewards, research agendas, and judgments about which questions and explanations appeared important or legitimate. These commitments increasingly operated as an institutional culture rather than merely as the preferences of individual scholars, making it easier to present a contested moral project as if it followed directly from social science itself.
  • The rise of symbolic capitalists. As social-justice language became influential and trendy, fluency in that language began to function as cultural capital: it could communicate moral commitments while also bringing professional benefits, prestige, and status. Identifying with marginalized groups, mastering specialized concepts, and challenging insufficiently progressive peers could help scholars claim authority and distinguish themselves. Their commitments need not be insincere; moral conviction and professional incentives can become blended and consequently reinforce one another within academic life.

Turner regards these developments as evidence that a justice-oriented moral project is displacing scientific sociology. He warns that advocacy can encourage ideological conformity, discourage inconvenient findings, alienate politically diverse audiences, and weaken sociology’s ability to solve problems for a wide range of publics and organizations. Sociologist Stephen Cole offers a parallel diagnosis.

Stephen Cole on sociology’s disciplinary paradigm

Cole writes:

“In sociology the disciplinary paradigm is closer to an ideology than to a set of commonly held theories, methods, and exemplars. The disciplinary paradigm in sociology is a type of victimology in which poor people are seen as the victims of capitalism and the capitalist class, women are seen as the victims of men, Afro-Americans and other ethnic minorities are seen as the victims of whites, and homosexuals are seen as the victims of homophobes. Evidence which does not support the dominant victimology is ignored or rejected; statements which support the dominant victimology are accepted without the benefit of empirical support.

…

The disciplinary paradigm in sociology also includes a preference for structural explanations as opposed to cultural explanations. Structural explanations concern how the society is organized and acts to influence the fate of individuals. … Cultural explanations concern anything which is in the individual’s head. Attitudes, values, goals, norms, all of these would be part of culture… Cultural explanations are usually seen as a form of blaming the victim argument and therefore are politically incorrect.”

Value-conscious objectivity

A productive middle position is to treat objectivity as a disciplined practice rather than the absence of all values. Sociologists can pursue questions they consider morally important and still protect inquiry from becoming predetermined advocacy. Doing so requires them to:

  1. State the question and relevant commitments clearly. Readers should be able to distinguish the researcher’s motivation from the empirical claim being tested.
  2. Separate evidence from evaluation. Explain what the evidence supports before arguing what people or governments should do.
  3. Use transparent and appropriate methods. Define categories, document decisions, disclose limitations, and make it possible for others to evaluate the analysis.
  4. Seek disconfirming evidence and rival explanations. A conclusion is more credible when it survives serious attempts to show that it is wrong.
  5. Remain answerable to criticism from multiple perspectives. Inclusion should broaden the questions and evidence considered without making political agreement a condition of participation.

This approach does not eliminate the tension between scientific and activist sociology. It makes the tension visible and manageable. Sociology can investigate inequality, suffering, institutions, solidarity, organizational problems, or any other subject while maintaining the same obligation: conclusions must be earned through evidence and reasoning rather than guaranteed by the moral attractiveness of the cause.

Further reading on the debate: Max Weber, “The Meaning of Ethical Neutrality in Sociology and Economics”; William F. Ogburn, “Folkways of a Scientific Sociology”; Lester F. Ward, Dynamic Sociology; Stephen Cole, ed., What’s Wrong with Sociology?; Christian Smith, The Sacred Project of American Sociology; Musa al-Gharbi, We Have Never Been Woke; Michael Burawoy, “For Public Sociology”; and Jonathan H. Turner, “The More American Sociology Seeks to Become a Politically-Relevant Discipline, the More Irrelevant It Becomes to Solving Societal Problems”. Turner’s article provides a forceful scientific-sociology critique; it should be read as a position within the controversy and compared with arguments for public, critical, feminist, and anti-racist sociology.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Social context connects individuals to institutions, relationships, history, and material conditions.
  • Sociology explains patterned social life rather than treating every outcome as purely individual.
  • Industrialization, urbanization, social change, and scientific inquiry helped establish sociology as a discipline.
  • The value-neutrality debate asks how sociologists can acknowledge moral commitments while preventing those commitments from predetermining empirical conclusions.
  • Values can enter research through topic selection, concept construction, operationalization, interpretation, explanatory emphasis, and the movement from findings to recommendations.
  • Sociological reflexivity makes the assumptions behind measures visible and distinguishes observations from causal, conceptual, and moral interpretations.
  • C. Wright Mills connected personal troubles to public issues and biography to history.
  • Cohort and life-course analysis show why the same historical event can affect groups differently depending on timing.
  • Durkheim’s Suicide shows how social integration and regulation can help explain patterned differences in individual outcomes.
