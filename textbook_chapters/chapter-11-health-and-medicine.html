Textbook · Chapter 12

Health, Illness, and Medicine

How social conditions shape chronic and infectious disease, and how to evaluate health-care cost, performance, and access.

A work schedule can shape sleep, a bus route can shape access to a clinic, and housing conditions can shape exposure to pollution. The sociology of health asks how these everyday arrangements affect who gets sick and who can get care.

Week 13 starts with the social determinants of health and uses them to explain two patterns: the rise of chronic disease as a leading cause of death and the reemergence of infectious disease. It then evaluates the U.S. health-care system along three dimensions: cost, performance, and access.

Social determinants of health

The social determinants of health are the nonmedical conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, along with the institutions and policies that shape those conditions. At the local level, job schedules, family resources, housing, and transportation affect daily exposures and choices. At the broader level, labor markets, public policy, economic inequality, and social norms help determine who faces those conditions.

Embodiment

Nancy Krieger uses embodiment to describe how the social and material conditions of life become incorporated into the body over time. For example, housing policy can shape where families can afford to live, which can affect repeated exposure to air pollution and, in turn, respiratory health.

The epidemiological transition

Compare the leading causes of death in the United States around 1900 with those in recent mortality records. Infectious diseases such as tuberculosis were among the leading causes around 1900. In 2024, heart disease and cancer were the two leading causes. This shift toward noncommunicable chronic diseases is the epidemiological transition.

Sanitation, safer water, vaccination, and other public health measures helped reduce deaths from many infections. As more people survived childhood and lived to older ages, chronic conditions accounted for a larger share of deaths. Work, transportation, and food environments also changed. A list of leading causes tells us which causes account for the most deaths in a particular population and period. Infectious diseases can still cause outbreaks and substantial harm.

Why chronic disease follows social patterns

Stress process theory

Leonard Pearlin’s stress process theory asks how social positions shape exposure to stressors and the resources available to cope. Stress proliferation occurs when a strain in one area creates strains in others. A worker who loses a job may fall behind on rent, lose health insurance, and experience conflict at home. Paid leave, savings, and social support can change how far these effects spread.

The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis help translate perceived threats into physiological responses. These responses help the body meet short-term demands. When threats such as job or housing insecurity repeatedly activate these systems, the resulting physiological wear can accumulate. This cumulative burden is called allostatic load.

Fundamental cause theory

Bruce Link and Jo Phelan argue that socioeconomic advantage helps protect health across many diseases because it provides flexible resources, including money, knowledge, power, and social connections. When a new screening test becomes available, people with reliable transportation, flexible schedules, and access to medical information can often use it sooner. The technology has changed, but the resources that help people benefit from it are still unequally distributed.

How a daily routine becomes the default

Imagine a neighborhood where most errands require a car, sidewalks end before reaching stores, and a worker finishing a late shift can readily buy a prepared meal but has little time to cook. The built environment, work schedule, and food market make some choices easier to repeat. The lecture’s “default American lifestyle” also asks how a care system oriented toward managing symptoms can leave the conditions shaping daily routines in place. Extending grocery hours or connecting sidewalks changes what people can realistically do each day.

Why infectious diseases reemerge

Ulrich Beck’s risk society theory asks how technological and economic development creates unintended hazards. For example, widespread antibiotic use in medicine and agriculture can contribute to antimicrobial resistance. The hazard can spread across jurisdictions, while the institutions responsible for monitoring and responding to it often have limited reach.

Mobility transition theory asks how modernization changes patterns of movement. Industrialization increases rural-to-urban migration. Later, frequent commuting, long-distance travel, and movement between cities create more connections among places. An outbreak can reach distant communities through those connections. Risk society theory directs attention to how modern institutions generate hazards. Mobility transition theory directs attention to how changing patterns of movement can carry infections between places.

The U.S. health-care system

Cost, performance, and access answer different questions about a health-care system. An advanced hospital can deliver excellent care to the people who reach it while patients elsewhere have difficulty obtaining routine treatment. Each dimension needs its own evidence.

Cost

National health expenditures reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person and 18.0 percent of gross domestic product. This measure includes spending across payers. The lecture’s 2024 estimate of about $1,630 per person in out-of-pocket spending refers to what households paid directly for care and excludes insurance premiums. For a childbirth bill, the provider’s listed charge, the amount an insurer negotiates, and the patient’s share can all differ. Comparing costs requires checking which amount is being reported and which services it includes.

Performance

Performance looks at what care achieves, from outcomes for patients receiving a particular treatment to outcomes for a population. The United States has advanced equipment and specialized expertise alongside uneven population outcomes. Life expectancy summarizes mortality across a population and reflects living conditions as well as medical care. Avoidable mortality draws attention to deaths that effective prevention or timely treatment could avert. Comparing neighborhoods, as in the lecture’s Baltimore example, can reveal differences hidden by a national average.

Access

Access asks whether people can obtain appropriate care when they need it. In 2024, 92.0 percent of people in the United States had health insurance for some or all of the year, and employment-based coverage was the largest single type. Coverage helps pay for care. A person with insurance may still delay a visit because of a deductible, an out-of-network specialist, a long wait, or lack of transportation.

A patchwork of payers and providers

People may get insurance through an employer, buy an individual plan, qualify for a public program such as Medicare or Medicaid, or pay directly when uninsured. Insurers and public programs finance care, while clinicians, hospitals, pharmacies, and other providers deliver it. Much of the system operates through private organizations. Providers can set charges, and insurers negotiate or otherwise establish what they pay. As a result, two people who use the same hospital can face different prices or costs depending on who pays and what their coverage includes.

Why costs rise

Several mechanisms can operate together. Fee-for-service rewards the volume of billable services; fragmented insurance creates administrative work; hospital and physician-practice consolidation can increase bargaining power; patent rules and market concentration affect drug and device prices; defensive practices and patient expectations may increase utilization; and new technologies often expand what can be treated rather than simply replacing older spending. At the same time, insufficient primary care, prevention, housing, and social support can allow manageable problems to become expensive crises.

A purely consumer-market analogy is incomplete because patients often lack information, face urgent needs, rely on professional judgment, and cannot predict future illness. Kenneth Arrow used these uncertainties to explain why trust, licensure, insurance, and professional norms arise in medical markets. Yet regulation and professionalism create their own questions of accountability and exclusion.

Eliot Freidson: professional dominance

Freidson argued that medicine gained authority not only through technical knowledge but through jurisdiction: organized physicians helped define legitimate illness, control entry into practice, direct other occupations, and shape institutions. This autonomy can protect clinical judgment from commercial or political pressure, but it can also insulate professionals from patients and competing forms of knowledge.

Later movements toward informed consent, patient rights, shared decision-making, nursing autonomy, and online health information weakened a simple model of physician dominance. Power has not disappeared; it has become distributed among professions, hospital systems, insurers, pharmaceutical firms, regulators, technology companies, and organized patients.

Three ways to organize care

Health systems mix markets and states in different proportions. A market-oriented model relies heavily on private insurance and providers. A social-insurance model uses mandatory, broadly pooled insurance while care may remain privately delivered. A national health service finances care through taxation and gives government a larger role in provision. No country is a pure type, and each model must make choices about budgets, waiting, prices, coverage, innovation, and patient autonomy.

The normative disagreement is equally important. If health care is primarily a commodity, price and consumer choice coordinate scarce resources, although purchasing power produces unequal access. If it is a social right, access should depend primarily on need, although collective financing requires explicit limits and priorities. Sociology cannot settle the moral question with data alone, but it can reveal who benefits, who pays, and which tradeoffs are hidden by each vocabulary.

The social construction of illness

This supplementary section examines how diagnoses and social expectations shape everyday life.

Illness has biological, subjective, and social dimensions. A condition can have a biological cause, a personal experience of symptoms, and a cultural interpretation that determines whether it is recognized, stigmatized, excused, treated, or ignored.

It is useful to distinguish disease, a professionally identified bodily or mental pathology; illness, the person’s lived experience of symptoms and suffering; and sickness, the social status and expectations attached to a recognized condition. The three can diverge. A screening test may identify disease before a person feels ill. A person may experience disabling pain without receiving a diagnosis that others accept. Institutions may recognize some conditions for insurance or leave while denying the same status to others.

Calling illness socially constructed does not mean that bodies or suffering are imaginary. Construction concerns the categories, thresholds, meanings, and responses through which biological variation becomes socially intelligible. Blood pressure exists materially, but the cutoff defining hypertension, the procedures used to measure it, and the consequences of the label are organized through medicine, research, markets, and policy.

Talcott Parsons: the sick role

Parsons described illness as a form of social deviance because it interrupts ordinary responsibilities. The sick role grants temporary exemption from normal duties, but it also creates obligations to want recovery and seek competent help. The concept shows that being ill changes one’s relationships and expectations, not only one’s body.

Interactionists ask how sick roles differ across cultures and situations. Conflict theorists ask who has the authority to define sickness, whose suffering is recognized, and how medical categories can reflect power.

The model works best for an acute, temporary illness in which recovery is expected. It fits chronic illness, disability, contested conditions, mental illness, and terminal disease less well. People may be unable to recover, may manage symptoms outside professional medicine, or may encounter suspicion because their condition is invisible. Feminist and disability scholars also note that expectations about productivity, dependence, and caregiving are unequally distributed.

Medicalization, demedicalization, and stigma

Medicalization occurs when a behavior, condition, or life process comes to be defined and treated as a medical problem. Peter Conrad emphasizes that physicians are not the only drivers: advocacy groups may seek recognition, pharmaceutical firms may develop markets, insurers decide what qualifies for reimbursement, and patients may value a diagnosis that validates suffering. Childbirth, aging, addiction, attention difficulties, and variations in mood or body size have all been medicalized to different degrees.

Medicalization can reduce blame and connect people to effective care, yet it can also narrow attention to individual diagnosis and treatment while obscuring working conditions, poverty, discrimination, or environmental causes. Demedicalization moves in the other direction. Homosexuality’s removal from psychiatric diagnostic classifications illustrates how scientific evidence, professional conflict, and social movements can transform medical authority.

Erving Goffman’s concept of stigma explains how an attribute becomes discrediting within a relationship and setting. People may conceal a diagnosis, anticipate rejection, or absorb stereotypes; institutions may enact discrimination even when no individual expresses hostility. Stigma can therefore become a health mechanism by delaying care, reducing support, and adding chronic stress.

Disability: medical and social models

A medical model locates disability primarily in an individual impairment to be treated or rehabilitated. A social model distinguishes impairment from disability produced by inaccessible buildings, technologies, schedules, and norms. The perspectives need not be mutually exclusive: pain and bodily limitation may be real while social design determines how disabling they become.

Theoretical perspectives in conversation

Functionalism emphasizes the contribution of public health, medicine, professional standards, and the sick role to social coordination. Its weakness is a tendency to presume that an established arrangement is beneficial or consensual. Conflict theory examines how class, race, gender, professional closure, and corporate power distribute risks and care. Its weakness is a temptation to read every clinical practice as domination and understate genuine expertise or shared interests.

Symbolic interactionism follows diagnosis, stigma, identity, and negotiation in encounters among patients, families, and professionals. It reveals how categories acquire consequences but can lose sight of financing and population structure. Social constructionism studies how medical knowledge and boundaries are made, while critical realism reminds us that diseases can have causal properties independent of recognition. Feminist and intersectional approaches connect bodies and care work to institutions, expose exclusions from research and treatment, and ask how several inequalities operate together.

The perspectives answer different questions. For a rise in diabetes, a stress-process account might trace insecurity and coping; fundamental cause theory asks who can use new preventive knowledge; political economy examines food systems, work, and treatment markets; interactionism studies diagnosis and daily identity work; and a biomedical account identifies metabolic pathways. A strong sociological explanation connects these levels and identifies evidence that could challenge it.

Current evidence and sources

National averages change as final records replace provisional estimates, and categories such as cause of death or insurance coverage depend on stated definitions. The chapter follows the Week 13 Sociology of Health lecture. The dated U.S. figures draw on the CDC’s final 2024 mortality and life-expectancy report, the Census Bureau’s 2024 health-insurance estimates, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ national health-expenditure data. A source date should always accompany a claim that a health statistic is “current.”

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Social determinants organize exposures and resources. Embodiment describes how those conditions affect bodies over time.
  • The epidemiological transition describes a shift in leading causes of death toward chronic diseases. Infectious threats can still reemerge.
  • Stress process theory explains how strains spread and accumulate. Fundamental cause theory explains how flexible resources help preserve health advantages as risks and treatments change.
  • Risk society theory explains hazards created by modernization. Mobility transition theory connects changes in travel and migration to the spread of infection.
  • Cost measures spending, performance examines outcomes, and access asks whether people can obtain care.

Questions for study

  1. A plant closes and former workers report lost insurance, housing insecurity, and worsening sleep. Trace a pathway from job loss to embodiment. Where could an intervention interrupt stress proliferation?
  2. A clinic introduces a free screening test, but appointments are available only on weekday mornings. Use fundamental cause theory to explain who may benefit first. What change could reduce the advantage associated with flexible work hours?
  3. A region’s leading causes of death have shifted from infections to chronic diseases, yet a new infection spreads between its cities. How can the epidemiological transition and mobility transition explain these two observations together?
  4. Two patients receive the same procedure at the same hospital but owe different amounts. What information would you need to explain the difference? What additional evidence would tell you whether people who need the procedure can obtain it?
