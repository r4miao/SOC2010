Health, Illness, and Medicine

How social conditions become embodied, how illness is defined, and how health-care institutions distribute cost, access, and performance.

Health is not produced only inside the body. It is also produced through housing, work, education, income, discrimination, stress, policy, culture, and access to care.

Two patients can carry the same diagnosis yet live very different illnesses. One has paid leave, stable housing, transportation, nearby specialists, and someone to help; the other risks losing wages, delays treatment, encounters an inaccessible clinic, and must interpret instructions in a second language. Biology matters in both cases. Sociology explains how exposure, diagnosis, treatment, recovery, and consequences are organized around the body. This chapter moves from population patterns to the social meaning of illness and then to the institutions that deliver and finance care.

Social determinants of health

The social determinants of health are the nonmedical conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age. They include class position, education, occupation, neighborhood, political institutions, environmental exposure, family resources, discrimination, and social support.

Social determinants do not replace genes, pathogens, physiology, or individual behavior. They shape who encounters hazards, which behaviors are feasible, how quickly a condition is noticed, and which resources can prevent damage. A behavior such as diet or exercise is simultaneously an individual action and a patterned response to work schedules, prices, safety, transportation, marketing, stress, and local environments.

Ulrich Beck’s concept of risk society adds a useful contemporary perspective. Modern institutions do not only distribute wealth and opportunity; they also produce new risks through industrial chemicals, technologies, global supply chains, environmental change, and complex health systems. These risks can be difficult for individuals to see or control, and they are distributed unequally across social locations.

Concept: social epidemiology

The study of how social conditions and social organization influence the distribution of health, illness, disability, and mortality across populations.

The epidemiologic transition from infectious disease toward noncommunicable chronic disease was connected to industrialization, urbanization, technological change, food processing, work, leisure, and mass media. New risks emerged even as older risks declined.

The transition is not a completed replacement. Vaccination, sanitation, nutrition, antibiotics, and public-health institutions reduced many infectious threats, while longer lives increased exposure to chronic disease. Global mobility, ecological change, antimicrobial resistance, unequal vaccination, and state capacity can produce reemerging infection. Chronic and infectious disease also interact, as the COVID-19 pandemic made clear.

Current U.S. health snapshot

Final CDC data place U.S. life expectancy at 79.0 years in 2024, up from 78.4 in 2023. Heart disease, cancer, and unintentional injuries remained the three leading causes of death. The improvement is real, but one national average conceals large differences by place, race and ethnicity, sex, income, and cause. Describing health requires trends and distributions, not a single ranking.

John Cassel and the social environment

Social epidemiology treats the social environment as a cause of health rather than merely a background variable. Social relationships, status, work, neighborhood conditions, and stress can influence exposure to risks and access to resources.

The concept of embodiment describes how the social world becomes incorporated into the body over time. It connects institutions and inequalities to biological processes without reducing health to either biology or society alone.

How social conditions become embodied

Leonard Pearlin: the stress process

The stress-process model links social position to exposure, interpretation, resources, and health. Chronic strains such as insecurity, caregiving, discrimination, unsafe work, or neighborhood disorder are not randomly distributed. Stress proliferation occurs when a problem in one domain creates problems in others: job loss can produce debt, housing instability, relationship conflict, and loss of insurance.

Social support, mastery, money, and coping practices can mediate or buffer effects, but access to these resources is also socially patterned. Interpreting an event as threatening matters, yet this does not imply that stress is merely subjective; institutional conditions shape both the situation and plausible interpretations.

Allostatic load and weathering

Allostasis is the body’s adjustment to challenge. Repeated or prolonged activation can create allostatic load, cumulative strain across regulatory systems. Arline Geronimus’s weathering hypothesis proposes that persistent social and economic adversity can accelerate health deterioration among marginalized populations, helping explain why inequalities may widen with age.

These ideas provide pathways from structured experience to physiology without assuming that every group difference has one cause. Researchers must still measure exposures, coping, environments, discrimination, medical care, selection, and biological processes.

Nancy Krieger: ecosocial theory

Krieger asks how people literally embody social and ecological arrangements across the life course and historical generation. Pathways include material deprivation, hazardous work, environmental toxins, discrimination, inadequate care, and socially inflicted trauma. Bodies bear histories, but people remain active agents rather than passive vessels.

An ecosocial explanation specifies who is exposed, which institutions organize exposure, when in the life course it occurs, and which biological process links it to an outcome. This protects “social determinants” from becoming a list without causation.

Link and Phelan: fundamental causes

Bruce Link and Jo Phelan call socioeconomic resources a fundamental cause when they affect multiple diseases through multiple pathways and allow people to avoid risk or minimize consequences. Money, knowledge, power, prestige, and useful social connections are flexible resources: when the disease landscape or medical technology changes, advantaged groups can deploy them in new ways.

The theory predicts that a health innovation may initially widen inequality because informed and resourced people adopt it first. Universal and easily delivered interventions can narrow the gap by reducing dependence on personal resources.

Place, race, gender, and intersection

Neighborhoods cluster housing quality, pollution, food retail, violence, transport, health facilities, schools, and political influence. Racial disparities can arise through residential segregation, labor markets, wealth, discrimination, and unequal treatment, while gender shapes work, care, exposure, diagnosis, and help-seeking. Intersectional analysis asks how these mechanisms combine rather than treating categories as independent biological risks.

Maternal mortality illustrates the need for careful mechanism. CDC recorded 649 maternal deaths in 2024, a rate of 17.9 per 100,000 live births. The rate for non-Hispanic Black women was 44.8, compared with 14.2 for non-Hispanic White women and 12.1 for Hispanic women. Age mattered strongly as well. The disparity does not identify one cause; it motivates analysis of cardiovascular risk, quality and continuity of care, hospitals, insurance, stress, discrimination, geography, and measurement.

The social construction of illness

Illness has biological, subjective, and social dimensions. A condition can have a biological cause, a personal experience of symptoms, and a cultural interpretation that determines whether it is recognized, stigmatized, excused, treated, or ignored.

It is useful to distinguish disease, a professionally identified bodily or mental pathology; illness, the person’s lived experience of symptoms and suffering; and sickness, the social status and expectations attached to a recognized condition. The three can diverge. A screening test may identify disease before a person feels ill. A person may experience disabling pain without receiving a diagnosis that others accept. Institutions may recognize some conditions for insurance or leave while denying the same status to others.

Calling illness socially constructed does not mean that bodies or suffering are imaginary. Construction concerns the categories, thresholds, meanings, and responses through which biological variation becomes socially intelligible. Blood pressure exists materially, but the cutoff defining hypertension, the procedures used to measure it, and the consequences of the label are organized through medicine, research, markets, and policy.

Talcott Parsons: the sick role

Parsons described illness as a form of social deviance because it interrupts ordinary responsibilities. The sick role grants temporary exemption from normal duties, but it also creates obligations to want recovery and seek competent help. The concept shows that being ill changes one’s relationships and expectations, not only one’s body.

Interactionists ask how sick roles differ across cultures and situations. Conflict theorists ask who has the authority to define sickness, whose suffering is recognized, and how medical categories can reflect power.

The model works best for an acute, temporary illness in which recovery is expected. It fits chronic illness, disability, contested conditions, mental illness, and terminal disease less well. People may be unable to recover, may manage symptoms outside professional medicine, or may encounter suspicion because their condition is invisible. Feminist and disability scholars also note that expectations about productivity, dependence, and caregiving are unequally distributed.

Medicalization, demedicalization, and stigma

Medicalization occurs when a behavior, condition, or life process comes to be defined and treated as a medical problem. Peter Conrad emphasizes that physicians are not the only drivers: advocacy groups may seek recognition, pharmaceutical firms may develop markets, insurers decide what qualifies for reimbursement, and patients may value a diagnosis that validates suffering. Childbirth, aging, addiction, attention difficulties, and variations in mood or body size have all been medicalized to different degrees.

Medicalization can reduce blame and connect people to effective care, yet it can also narrow attention to individual diagnosis and treatment while obscuring working conditions, poverty, discrimination, or environmental causes. Demedicalization moves in the other direction. Homosexuality’s removal from psychiatric diagnostic classifications illustrates how scientific evidence, professional conflict, and social movements can transform medical authority.

Erving Goffman’s concept of stigma explains how an attribute becomes discrediting within a relationship and setting. People may conceal a diagnosis, anticipate rejection, or absorb stereotypes; institutions may enact discrimination even when no individual expresses hostility. Stigma can therefore become a health mechanism by delaying care, reducing support, and adding chronic stress.

Disability: medical and social models

A medical model locates disability primarily in an individual impairment to be treated or rehabilitated. A social model distinguishes impairment from disability produced by inaccessible buildings, technologies, schedules, and norms. The perspectives need not be mutually exclusive: pain and bodily limitation may be real while social design determines how disabling they become.

The health-care system

The U.S. health-care system can be evaluated through cost, access, and performance. A fee-for-service, market-driven system may produce advanced technology and specialized care, while also producing high prices, unequal access, fragmented payment, administrative complexity, and incentives to provide profitable services.

Example: a costly treatment

A patient may have an effective treatment covered by insurance while another patient with the same condition cannot afford it. The difference is not simply medical need. It reflects employment, insurance design, public policy, provider networks, wealth, and bargaining power.

Fundamental cause theory argues that social class affects many diseases through flexible resources such as money, knowledge, social connections, and political influence. When one health risk changes, advantaged groups can use these resources to avoid or reduce the new risk. This helps explain why health inequality persists even when the specific mechanisms change.

A patchwork of payers and providers

The United States does not have one health-care market. Employer plans, individually purchased insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, military and veterans’ systems, safety-net institutions, and direct payment overlap. In 2024, 92.0 percent of people had health insurance for some or all of the year; private coverage was more common than public coverage, and employment-based insurance remained the largest single type. Coverage nevertheless varies with employment, age, income, disability status, state policy, and immigration status. Having a card also does not guarantee timely access when deductibles, narrow networks, transportation, language barriers, or provider shortages remain.

National health expenditures reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person and 18.0 percent of gross domestic product. High spending can purchase useful labor, medicines, facilities, and innovation, so cost alone is not a verdict. Sociological analysis asks who sets prices, who bears costs, which services receive investment, and what population health results follow.

Why costs rise

Several mechanisms can operate together. Fee-for-service rewards the volume of billable services; fragmented insurance creates administrative work; hospital and physician-practice consolidation can increase bargaining power; patent rules and market concentration affect drug and device prices; defensive practices and patient expectations may increase utilization; and new technologies often expand what can be treated rather than simply replacing older spending. At the same time, insufficient primary care, prevention, housing, and social support can allow manageable problems to become expensive crises.

A purely consumer-market analogy is incomplete because patients often lack information, face urgent needs, rely on professional judgment, and cannot predict future illness. Kenneth Arrow used these uncertainties to explain why trust, licensure, insurance, and professional norms arise in medical markets. Yet regulation and professionalism create their own questions of accountability and exclusion.

Eliot Freidson: professional dominance

Freidson argued that medicine gained authority not only through technical knowledge but through jurisdiction: organized physicians helped define legitimate illness, control entry into practice, direct other occupations, and shape institutions. This autonomy can protect clinical judgment from commercial or political pressure, but it can also insulate professionals from patients and competing forms of knowledge.

Later movements toward informed consent, patient rights, shared decision-making, nursing autonomy, and online health information weakened a simple model of physician dominance. Power has not disappeared; it has become distributed among professions, hospital systems, insurers, pharmaceutical firms, regulators, technology companies, and organized patients.

Three ways to organize care

Health systems mix markets and states in different proportions. A market-oriented model relies heavily on private insurance and providers. A social-insurance model uses mandatory, broadly pooled insurance while care may remain privately delivered. A national health service finances care through taxation and gives government a larger role in provision. No country is a pure type, and each model must make choices about budgets, waiting, prices, coverage, innovation, and patient autonomy.

The normative disagreement is equally important. If health care is primarily a commodity, price and consumer choice coordinate scarce resources, although purchasing power produces unequal access. If it is a social right, access should depend primarily on need, although collective financing requires explicit limits and priorities. Sociology cannot settle the moral question with data alone, but it can reveal who benefits, who pays, and which tradeoffs are hidden by each vocabulary.

Theoretical perspectives in conversation

Functionalism emphasizes the contribution of public health, medicine, professional standards, and the sick role to social coordination. Its weakness is a tendency to presume that an established arrangement is beneficial or consensual. Conflict theory examines how class, race, gender, professional closure, and corporate power distribute risks and care. Its weakness is a temptation to read every clinical practice as domination and understate genuine expertise or shared interests.

Symbolic interactionism follows diagnosis, stigma, identity, and negotiation in encounters among patients, families, and professionals. It reveals how categories acquire consequences but can lose sight of financing and population structure. Social constructionism studies how medical knowledge and boundaries are made, while critical realism reminds us that diseases can have causal properties independent of recognition. Feminist and intersectional approaches connect bodies and care work to institutions, expose exclusions from research and treatment, and ask how several inequalities operate together.

The perspectives answer different questions. For a rise in diabetes, a stress-process account might trace insecurity and coping; fundamental cause theory asks who can use new preventive knowledge; political economy examines food systems, work, and treatment markets; interactionism studies diagnosis and daily identity work; and a biomedical account identifies metabolic pathways. A strong sociological explanation connects these levels and identifies evidence that could challenge it.

Current evidence and sources

National averages change as final records replace provisional estimates, and categories such as maternal death or insurance coverage depend on stated definitions. Current figures in this chapter draw on the CDC’s final 2024 mortality and life-expectancy report and 2024 maternal-mortality report, the Census Bureau’s 2024 health-insurance estimates, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ national health-expenditure data. A source date should always accompany a claim that a health statistic is “current.”

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Health is shaped by social determinants as well as biology, behavior, and medical care.
  • Stress, weathering, ecosocial theory, and fundamental cause theory specify different mechanisms of embodiment.
  • Disease, illness, and sickness distinguish pathology, lived experience, and social recognition.
  • The sick role, medicalization, stigma, and disability models show how meanings and institutions organize suffering.
  • Cost, access, and performance reflect payer arrangements, professional and corporate power, incentives, and moral choices.
  • Theoretical perspectives are most useful when their different questions are connected rather than treated as interchangeable slogans.

Questions for study

  1. How can the same biological condition have different social consequences?
  2. Why might a new medical innovation initially widen a health inequality?
  3. When does medicalization reduce blame, and when can it conceal a social cause?
  4. How would functionalist, interactionist, and conflict perspectives analyze the sick role?
  5. What tradeoffs appear when health care is described as a commodity or as a social right?
