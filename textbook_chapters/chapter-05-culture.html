Culture

How shared meanings, material objects, values, norms, and language organize social life.

Culture is not simply art, food, or tradition. It is the shared system of meanings through which people interpret the world, coordinate action, evaluate behavior, and imagine what is possible.

Imagine arriving at a dinner where you do not know whether to remove your shoes, where to sit, when to begin eating, or whether refusing food is polite. Nothing physical prevents you from acting, yet every choice seems socially consequential. Other guests move easily because they possess cultural knowledge that has become habitual. This chapter begins with that experience of disorientation, identifies the elements of culture, and then asks a deeper causal question: do material conditions and power produce culture, does culture independently shape institutions and conduct, or do the two processes continually interact?

Entering an unfamiliar culture

Cultural shock is the disorientation and confusion people may feel when they encounter a culture whose meanings, routines, language, and expectations differ from those they have learned. Cultural shock is not evidence that one culture is irrational. It is evidence that everyday life depends on taken-for-granted knowledge.

Callout: a crisis of meaning

A greeting, gesture, meal, or form of dress may feel obvious to one person and confusing to another. The interaction becomes a “crisis situation” when participants assume that their own definition is universal. Sociological analysis slows the reaction down and asks how each person learned the meaning.

A system of shared meaning

Anthropologist Clifford Geertz described culture as a system of inherited conceptions expressed in symbolic forms through which people communicate, preserve, and develop knowledge and attitudes toward life. In simpler terms, culture is a group’s shared way of thinking and doing. It is learned, social, patterned, and changeable.

Concept: culture

A system of meanings, symbols, values, norms, practices, and material objects shared by a group and used to interpret and organize social life.

“Shared” does not mean uniform or consensual. People can recognize the same symbol while interpreting or evaluating it differently. Knowledge is also distributed: a physician, patient, insurer, and hospital administrator participate in the same medical culture but know different rules and possess unequal authority to define what counts as appropriate care. Culture exists through patterned overlap among people, organizations, and artifacts, not because an entire nation carries one identical set of ideas.

Culture emerges from action and then organizes action

People create slang, rituals, fashions, classifications, and rules as they interact. Repetition makes some innovations familiar; organizations record them in policies and technologies; socialization passes them to newcomers. Once institutionalized, these cultural forms confront later actors as inherited expectations. Culture is therefore neither a script imposed on passive people nor a purely private invention. It is an emergent social product that becomes a resource and constraint for subsequent action.

Two traditions for explaining culture

Sociologists have developed two influential lines of argument about how culture matters. The Marxist and neo-Marxist tradition asks how economic relations, institutions, and unequal power shape cultural ideas and make some meanings appear natural. The cultural-autonomy tradition asks how symbols, moral codes, classifications, and narratives can shape action and institutions in their own right. The traditions overlap in important ways, but they emphasize different directions of influence.

The disagreement is sometimes framed as materialism versus idealism. A strongly materialist explanation treats ideas as responses to technologies, economic organization, or group interests. A strongly idealist explanation treats beliefs and moral visions as primary causes of social organization. Marx’s analysis of capitalism is commonly placed near the first pole, while Weber’s account of how Protestant ideas helped organize disciplined economic conduct supplies a famous challenge to one-way economic determinism. Neither scholar reduced history to a single cause: Marx analyzed consciousness and political organization, and Weber repeatedly examined material interests and institutions. The productive question is therefore not “matter or meaning?” but which mechanisms connect them in a particular case.

Comparing explanations: why did credentialing expand?

A materialist account might emphasize employers’ need to sort a growing labor force and professional groups’ interest in restricting entry. A cultural account might emphasize public faith in expertise, merit, and education as signs of deservingness. An institutional account might show schools, states, and occupations copying established credential rules. The explanations can compete over causal priority while also describing mutually reinforcing processes.

Key idea: relative autonomy

To say that culture has relative autonomy is not to say that it floats free from history, inequality, or institutions. Culture is produced and transmitted in social conditions. Once meanings, categories, and stories become established, however, they can also constrain and enable action, organize perception, and reshape the institutions that helped produce them.

Classical foundations

Karl Marx analyzed commodities, consumption, and the way economic relations can become hidden behind the cultural meanings attached to things. This line of thought directs attention to the material conditions and power relations behind apparently ordinary cultural ideas.

Émile Durkheim emphasized the symbolic and ritual dimensions of collective life: shared sacred symbols can create solidarity and represent the group itself. Max Weber treated culture as central to human action. He examined how religious ideas, especially ideas about salvation and vocation, shaped motivation, institutions, and economic conduct. Anthropologist Clifford Geertz later described culture as a system of symbolic meanings through which people interpret experience.

These approaches differ, but all reject the idea that culture is decoration added to an otherwise economic or political world. Culture helps organize motivation, solidarity, conflict, and power.

Material and nonmaterial culture

Material culture includes the physical things people create and use: buildings, clothing, food, machines, art, tools, technologies, and landscapes. Nonmaterial culture includes language, gestures, values, beliefs, norms, and categories of thought. Material objects carry meanings, while meanings guide how objects are designed, used, and valued.

Callout: material objects are cultural texts

A garment can communicate modesty, profession, nationality, religious commitment, fashion, or resistance depending on the setting. Food can express hospitality, purity, class, or national identity. Architecture can embody ideas about privacy, authority, family, nature, or modernity. The physical object is only one part of its social meaning.

Landscapes are material culture as well. Roads, parks, farms, suburbs, shopping districts, and monuments reflect decisions about whose history is remembered, whose movement is prioritized, and what kinds of activities are encouraged. Material arrangements make some cultural practices easier and others more difficult.

Language and the organization of perception

Language and the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis

Language is a symbolic system that allows people to communicate abstract thought. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis draws attention to the way language both reflects and shapes cultural experience. Languages do not imprison thought, but the categories and distinctions made available through language can make some features of the world more noticeable and easier to discuss.

Words for family, gender, emotion, time, or social status can organize attention differently. A language may distinguish relationships or experiences that another language groups together. Learning a language therefore involves learning a patterned way of perceiving and relating to other people.

It is useful to distinguish a strong and a weak version of linguistic relativity. The strong version—linguistic determinism—would claim that language fixes what speakers are capable of thinking. Evidence that people can learn new categories, translate imperfectly, and notice distinctions for which they lack a convenient word weighs against that claim. The weaker version proposes that habitual language guides attention, memory, and classification without making alternative thought impossible. Most contemporary research asks when and how such effects occur rather than treating language as an inescapable prison.

Callout: naming and grouping

Words do not merely label a world that is already divided into natural categories. Calling several foods “nuts,” for example, groups them together for practical purposes. Terms for family members, occupations, gender, or nationality can make some relationships visible and place people into social categories with consequences.

Values, norms, and socialization

Values are collective ideas about what is good, desirable, proper, or wrong. Norms are expected behaviors or dominant practices based on values. Norms can be formal, such as laws and school regulations, or informal, such as expectations about greeting, dress, eye contact, personal space, and when to speak. Sanctions—rewards and punishments, formal and informal—encourage conformity and mark violations.

Socialization is the process through which people internalize cultural expectations and learn to participate in society. Families, schools, peer groups, workplaces, religious organizations, and media all socialize people. Socialization does not produce identical individuals; people interpret, negotiate, resist, and revise what they learn.

Concept: norm

An expected rule or practice that guides behavior. Folkways are ordinary conventions, mores carry strong moral significance, and laws are formally enforced norms.

Sociologists also distinguish ideal culture—what a group says people should do—from real culture—the recurrent practices researchers observe. The gap is analytically valuable. A university may celebrate open inquiry while students learn to avoid unpopular questions; a workplace may praise work-life balance while rewarding constant availability. The mismatch can expose competing values, unequal sanctions, or organizational conditions that make the official ideal difficult to enact.

Unity, subculture, and cultural boundaries

Some cultural patterns are widespread because all societies must address recurring problems such as communication, care of children, death, cooperation, and conflict. Calling these cultural universals does not mean that societies solve them in the same way. Families, rituals, exchange, and moral rules appear widely, but their forms and meanings vary historically.

A subculture is a group whose distinctive meanings and practices coexist with broader cultural patterns; occupations, music scenes, religious communities, military units, and online communities may all develop specialized vocabularies and norms. A counterculture not only differs but rejects important values or institutions of a dominant culture. Boundaries are porous: ideas move between groups, and what begins as opposition may be commercialized or incorporated into mainstream institutions.

Mary Douglas: classification, purity, and danger

Anthropologist Mary Douglas showed that classifications of clean and dirty, pure and polluted, orderly and dangerous are social as well as practical. Things perceived as “out of place” can disturb an established scheme of classification. Her approach helps explain why disputes about food, bodies, dress, sacred spaces, or social identities may evoke moral intensity out of proportion to immediate physical harm: the disputed object challenges a boundary through which a community imagines order.

Cultural variation and worldviews

Ronald Inglehart and Christian Welzel: cultural value dimensions

Research based on the World Values Survey organizes cross-national cultural variation along two broad dimensions. The first runs from traditional values to secular-rational values. Traditional values emphasize religion, parent-child ties, deference to authority, traditional family norms, and national pride. Secular-rational values place less emphasis on religious authority, traditional family expectations, and obedience.

The second dimension runs from survival values to self-expression values. Survival values prioritize economic and physical security, while self-expression values give greater weight to autonomy, participation, tolerance, gender equality, environmental protection, and voice in public decisions. Inglehart and Welzel link these shifts to changing levels of existential security associated with industrialization and postindustrial development.

The map is a comparative tool, not a ranking of cultures or a claim that every person in a country shares the same outlook. Societies contain class, regional, generational, religious, and political differences. The value of the theory is that it makes cultural patterns measurable and helps sociologists connect values to social change.

Callout: using cultural subtypes carefully

Two people may both value family but disagree about authority, gender roles, or individual choice. A society may show strong traditional values in religious life and self-expression values in environmental politics. Cultural subtypes identify tendencies and combinations; they do not replace the need to study people in context.

Culture as a relatively autonomous social force

The cultural-autonomy tradition treats culture as more than an outcome of class position, organizational interest, or political power. It studies the symbolic structures through which people sort experience into categories such as pure and impure, sacred and profane, civilized and backward, authentic and fake, or deserving and undeserving. Such categories can guide feeling, judgment, and action even when people do not explicitly recognize them.

From meaning to action: Geertz, Swidler, and Sewell

Geertz emphasized that social action is meaningful because people act within shared “webs of significance.” Ann Swidler described culture as a tool kit of habits, skills, stories, styles, and symbols from which people construct strategies of action. Culture does not dictate a single response; it makes some responses easier to imagine, justify, and carry out than others.

William H. Sewell Jr. connected cultural schemas—generalizable rules and assumptions for interpreting the world—to material resources. Schemas guide how people use resources, while resources can reinforce or transform schemas. This approach explains why cultural meanings have real effects without treating them as separate from material life.

Jeffrey C. Alexander and Philip Smith: cultural sociology

Jeffrey C. Alexander and Philip Smith argue for a “strong program” in cultural sociology. Rather than treating culture only as something explained by the economy, institutions, or interests, they analyze culture as a relatively autonomous system of codes, narratives, symbols, and moral binaries. These symbolic structures help shape identities, political performances, public debates, and institutional legitimacy.

For example, a public controversy may be organized through oppositions such as innocent/guilty, pure/corrupt, democratic/authoritarian, or ordinary/elite. Material interests still matter, but cultural codes give those interests their moral force and make some claims persuasive while making others seem suspicious or unthinkable.

The functionalist perspective on culture

Functionalists ask how culture contributes to the order, continuity, and integration of society. Shared values make cooperation possible because people can anticipate one another’s conduct. Norms and sanctions coordinate behavior, while rituals and symbols create a sense of belonging. Culture can also provide identity, meaning, and predictability during periods of uncertainty.

Functionalists do not have to assume that every cultural practice is beneficial. A practice can be dysfunctional when it prevents adaptation, creates conflict, or normalizes behavior that harms members of a society. Cultural systems may also contain contradictions: a society may praise individual freedom while rewarding conformity in schools and workplaces.

Durkheim and the integrative power of culture

Émile Durkheim’s analysis of ritual is a classic functionalist approach. When people gather around sacred symbols, they experience collective belonging and reaffirm moral commitments. The symbol represents more than an object; it represents the group and its shared life.

Example: school rituals

Orientation ceremonies, academic regalia, honor rolls, athletic events, and graduation rituals do more than communicate information. They create membership, mark transitions, recognize achievement, and connect individual accomplishments to a larger institution.

The conflict perspective on culture

Conflict theorists ask whose meanings become dominant, whose practices are treated as normal, and how culture legitimates unequal power. This perspective develops the Marxist and neo-Marxist concern that culture can be shaped by material inequality and used to stabilize domination. A school curriculum, media image, language standard, dress code, or national symbol may appear neutral while reflecting the history and interests of powerful groups.

Karl Mannheim: knowledge and social location

Karl Mannheim’s sociology of knowledge is often read alongside neo-Marxist approaches because it rejects the idea that ideas arise outside social life. He argued that people’s social locations, historical experiences, and group interests shape what they notice, regard as plausible, and call common sense. Groups can therefore interpret the same social problem in different ways.

Mannheim did not claim that a belief is false simply because it has a social origin. His point was that knowledge is situated: understanding an idea requires asking who holds it, in what historical setting, and in relation to which social experiences. He distinguished ideology, which can help sustain an existing order, from utopia, which imagines and seeks a transformed one.

Antonio Gramsci and cultural hegemony

Antonio Gramsci used hegemony to describe the way powerful groups maintain dominance partly by making their ideas appear natural, universal, or common sense. Cultural power does not depend only on force. It also operates through schools, media, language, and everyday assumptions.

Hegemony is never total. People can reinterpret symbols, create countercultures, organize movements, and challenge what has been presented as common sense. Conflict theory therefore studies both domination and resistance.

Pierre Bourdieu: cultural capital and distinction

Pierre Bourdieu argued that cultural resources can function like capital. Familiarity with valued language, tastes, manners, credentials, and styles can help people gain recognition in schools, workplaces, and elite institutions. Cultural capital is not simply personal refinement; it becomes an advantage when institutions reward some cultural forms more than others.

Example: the hidden curriculum

A school teaches official subjects, but it also teaches punctuality, competition, obedience, self-presentation, and expectations about authority. These informal lessons can prepare students for organizational life while also rewarding students whose families already know how institutions work.

The symbolic interactionist perspective on culture

Symbolic interactionists study culture as something people accomplish in everyday interaction. Meanings are not fixed objects stored inside a society. People interpret symbols, negotiate definitions, repair misunderstandings, and push cultural boundaries. Cultural conflict can arise because groups attach different meanings to the same practice, not only because one group is simply “oppressor” and the other “oppressed.”

This perspective directs attention to cultural competence: the practical knowledge people use to know how to behave in a situation. It also examines how identities are performed, how labels become attached to people, and how repeated interactions make a cultural rule feel natural.

Interactionists also explain cultural change from the bottom up. An innovation may begin as an improvised definition in a small group. If others adopt it, repeat it, attach sanctions to it, and carry it across social networks, the innovation can become a convention. Organizations and media can then stabilize and diffuse it on a much larger scale. Macro-level culture is thus reproduced through micro-level encounters, although actors with greater visibility and institutional authority have more power to circulate their definitions.

Example: a changing meaning

A national flag can be experienced as a symbol of unity, military sacrifice, exclusion, protest, or state authority. The material object has not changed, but its meaning changes across audiences, historical moments, and interactional settings.

Culture, power, and social change

Culture changes through several mechanisms. Innovation introduces a new object or practice; diffusion carries cultural elements between groups; hybridization recombines forms from different settings; and institutionalization embeds a meaning in routines, laws, curricula, markets, or technologies. Global circulation rarely produces simple copying. Local actors translate imported forms, creating what scholars sometimes call glocalization.

William F. Ogburn: cultural lag

Ogburn used cultural lag to describe a mismatch in the rate of change among parts of culture. Technologies may change faster than laws, moral expectations, organizational routines, or everyday skills. Artificial intelligence, reproductive technologies, and social media, for example, create capabilities before institutions settle questions of privacy, authorship, responsibility, and appropriate use. The concept identifies a temporal gap; it does not assume that every technological innovation should be accepted or that nonmaterial culture will eventually adjust in only one direction.

The Chinese Cultural Revolution provides a dramatic example of an attempt to transform culture through political power. The “Four Olds”—old ideas, old culture, old habits, and old customs—were targeted as remnants of oppressive class relations. Monuments, historical texts, religious objects, books, art, and traditions became political targets. The case shows that cultural change can involve education and persuasion, but it can also involve coercion, destruction, and the authority of the state.

Callout: culture can be both resource and battleground

A monument can be experienced as heritage by one group and as a symbol of domination by another. Removing, preserving, renaming, or surrounding it with new information changes the public meaning of the site. Cultural politics is therefore also a struggle over memory, identity, and whose interpretation becomes visible.

Contemporary debates also distinguish between people who see the world from “Anywheres” and people who see it from “Somewheres.” The contrast describes a cultural and political divide between highly mobile, educated, professional people who identify with broad institutions and people whose identities are more rooted in local places, communities, and inherited belonging. It should not be treated as a complete classification of people, but it helps explain why national debates can involve competing experiences of mobility, home, authority, and change.

Zygmunt Bauman: liquid modernity and culture

Zygmunt Bauman described contemporary social life as liquid modernity: a condition in which institutions, identities, careers, relationships, and cultural standards change rapidly and provide less durable guidance. The metaphor of liquidity does not mean that nothing is structured. It means that structures are more flexible, temporary, and difficult to rely on for a lifetime.

Bauman connects liquid modernity to individualization and consumer culture. People are encouraged to treat identity as a personal project, continually updating the self through choices, experiences, relationships, and commodities. This can expand freedom, but it can also produce insecurity, rootlessness, and responsibility for problems that are socially organized.

Bauman’s argument extends the lecture’s discussion of postmodern culture: individuals may be freed from older obligations while becoming more exposed to uncertainty, market pressure, and the demand to remain adaptable.

Postmodern critiques emphasize the burden of individualization. As religion, tradition, the state, and stable work provide less shared identity, people may experience freedom from old constraints together with rootlessness, uncertainty, and pressure to construct an “authentic” self. Consumerism can turn self-expression into a market project in which identity is communicated through goods and lifestyles.

The culture-war thesis

James Davison Hunter: culture wars

In Culture Wars (1991), sociologist James Davison Hunter argued that American public life was increasingly organized around a struggle between competing moral visions. He described a conflict between traditionalist and progressivist orientations over issues such as family, sexuality, religion, education, law, art, and public authority.

The culture-war thesis is not simply the claim that people disagree. It argues that disagreements across several issues can align into broader moral camps, and that institutions become arenas in which groups compete to define the meaning of the society. Political parties, churches, schools, courts, media, and advocacy organizations can amplify these symbolic conflicts.

The thesis is debated. Some sociologists argue that “culture war” language exaggerates polarization, hides economic interests, or treats diverse groups as more unified than they are. Used carefully, however, the thesis helps explain why conflicts over symbols and values can become conflicts over law, institutional control, and national identity.

Callout: when a symbolic dispute becomes institutional

A disagreement over a school book, public monument, curriculum, or religious display may begin as a dispute over meaning. It becomes a broader institutional conflict when groups seek to change policy, control funding, influence curriculum, or define who belongs in the public community.

Cultural difference is not automatically cultural conflict. Ethnocentrism judges other ways of life by the standards of one’s own group. Cultural relativism seeks to understand practices in their own cultural context. Cultural relativism is an analytical tool, not a command to approve every practice; it asks us to understand before judging.

How to construct a cultural explanation

A convincing cultural explanation does more than observe that groups have different values. It identifies the meanings at stake, shows how people acquire and use them, and demonstrates a mechanism linking meaning to action. It also asks which alternatives were materially possible, who could authorize a definition, and whether institutions rewarded some cultural competencies over others. Without those checks, “culture” can become a vague label or a way of blaming disadvantaged groups for conditions they did not create.

A four-part test

When explaining a cultural pattern, ask: (1) What symbols, classifications, narratives, values, or habits are involved? (2) Through which families, organizations, networks, rituals, or media are they transmitted? (3) How do they alter perception, motivation, coordination, or justification? (4) How do resources, sanctions, and power reinforce or challenge them? This test keeps meaning in the explanation without isolating it from social structure.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Culture is a learned, shared, symbolic, patterned, and changeable system of meanings and practices; shared culture can still be contested and unevenly distributed.
  • Culture emerges through social action and, once institutionalized, constrains and enables later action.
  • Material objects and nonmaterial meanings are connected.
  • Language helps organize communication and perception, but linguistic relativity does not require linguistic determinism.
  • Values guide judgments; norms guide expected behavior; socialization transmits cultural expectations.
  • Ideal and real culture may diverge, revealing contradictions between official principles and recurrent practices.
  • Inglehart and Welzel identify traditional/secular-rational and survival/self-expression value dimensions.
  • Marxist and neo-Marxist approaches examine how power and material inequality shape cultural meanings, while the cultural-autonomy tradition examines how meanings can also shape action and institutions.
  • Culture has relative autonomy: it is socially produced but can become a force that organizes perception, identity, and collective action.
  • Functionalists emphasize integration, conflict theorists emphasize power, and interactionists emphasize meaning-making.
  • Hegemony shows how cultural meanings can reproduce power while remaining open to challenge.
  • Innovation, diffusion, hybridization, glocalization, institutionalization, and cultural lag explain different aspects of cultural change.
  • Hunter’s culture-war thesis explains how moral visions can become institutional and political conflicts.
  • Ethnocentrism and cultural relativism provide contrasting approaches to cultural difference.
