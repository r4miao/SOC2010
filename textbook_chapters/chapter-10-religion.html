Textbook · Chapter 10

Religion

What counts as religion, why modern societies secularize, and how sacred life takes new forms.

A congregation sings together without worshipping a god. A political leader becomes the center of pilgrimage and devotion. A reader treats a fictional character as a spiritual guide. Where does religion begin, and where does it end?

These cases connect two questions that organize this chapter: How should we define religion? And what happens to religion as societies modernize? If religion means membership in established churches, its decline may seem straightforward. If it includes sacred commitments, collective rituals, and sources of ultimate meaning, the same society may appear to be transforming its religious life. We begin, as the lecture does, by separating what people believe, what they do, and where they belong. We then examine competing definitions, the secularization debate, and modern transformations of religion, returning at the end to the boundary between fandom and faith.

Start with the survey: belief, practice, and affiliation

The lecture’s opening survey asks about religious preference or identification. A response such as Catholic, Protestant, Muslim, Buddhist, Jewish, Hindu, Orthodox, another religion, or no religion measures affiliation. It does not tell us how strongly someone believes, how often they practice, or how important religion is in daily life. Nor does “none” necessarily mean atheist.

A person might attend for family and community while doubting doctrine. Another might believe in a spiritual power and pray privately while rejecting organized religion. A third might identify with a tradition mainly through ancestry, holidays, or cultural belonging. These combinations are part of the phenomenon to explain, rather than mistakes in the survey.

Three dimensions that a single religious label cannot capture
DimensionA possible survey questionWhat it reveals
BeliefDo you believe in God or a spiritual power? How certain are you?Conviction, doubt, and understandings of reality.
BehaviorHow often do you attend services, pray, or take part in rituals?Public participation and private practice.
BelongingWhat religion, if any, do you identify with?Affiliation, identity, and community ties.

The lecture’s historical Pew charts compare 2007 and 2014. They show a decline in absolute certainty about God and in weekly attendance. Those are dated comparisons, and they measure different dimensions. For a later reference point, Pew’s 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, published in 2025, found that 62% of U.S. adults identified as Christian, 29% were unaffiliated, and 7% belonged to other religions. Christian identification was down from 78% in 2007, although recent surveys showed a plateau beginning around 2019. In the same 2023–24 study, 83% believed in God or a universal spirit, while 33% attended services in person at least monthly. Affiliation, belief, and attendance therefore cannot substitute for one another.

Compare like with like

A classroom poll describes the students who answer it; it is not a representative sample of U.S. adults. Before interpreting differences from a national survey, compare ages, question wording, response choices, survey year, and whether attendance includes online services. A class with many unaffiliated students may still contain substantial belief and private religious practice.

Pew also cautions that changes in survey mode and attendance questions make the 2014 and 2023–24 attendance estimates unsuitable for a direct trend comparison.

Boundary cases: what counts as religion?

Familiar examples make religion seem easy to recognize. Harder cases reveal the assumptions behind that recognition. Sunday Assembly describes itself as a secular congregation: people gather for songs, talks, reflection, social connection, and service without shared supernatural doctrine. Its participants can gain some of the benefits associated with religious congregations while understanding their gathering as nonreligious. Should a sociologist adopt that self-description, or classify the gathering by the functions it performs?

Official communist atheism presents another boundary case when juxtaposed with devotion to Mao Zedong. Portraits, collective recitation, ritualized loyalty, pilgrimage, and reverence for a preserved body invite comparison with religious practices. The comparison is especially striking when Mao’s mausoleum is placed beside a saint’s shrine. Yet similar appearances alone do not establish identical beliefs or social relationships. A visitor might be a devotee, a tourist, a patriot, or someone complying with political expectations. Researchers must investigate participants’ meanings, the organization of the practice, and the conditions under which people participate.

Who gets to recognize a religion?

The lecture’s Church of Body Modification case makes classification consequential. In 2010, the ACLU challenged a North Carolina school’s treatment of Ariana Iacono, a 14-year-old suspended for a nose stud that she and her mother understood as religious expression. For sociology, the case raises a question about power: whose practices look sufficiently familiar to be recognized as religious? Institutional recognition can affect access, discipline, and belonging. An unfamiliar practice requires investigation rather than an automatic assumption that it is merely fashion.

Two approaches organize the disagreement. A functional definition asks what religion does: does it organize sacred commitments, create meaning, and sustain a moral community? A substantive definition asks what religion is about: do its beliefs and practices concern superhuman powers or realities beyond ordinary human life? These definitions draw different boundaries around the same cases.

Durkheim: sacred things and moral communities

Émile Durkheim: a functional approach

In The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), Durkheim connected beliefs and practices concerning sacred things with the formation of a moral community. His central question was how people experience a shared world as binding and larger than themselves. Religion matters because it helps produce the collective life through which individuals acquire identities, obligations, and ideals.

A useful way to summarize this approach is through the three Bs: belief, behavior, and belonging. Beliefs identify and explain sacred things. Behaviors, especially rituals, regulate how people approach them. Belonging places participants within a community that recognizes and renews those commitments. Durkheim’s use of “church” refers to a moral community, rather than requiring a Christian institution or a church building.

Concept: sacred and profane

The sacred consists of things set apart and surrounded by special rules, prohibitions, or reverence. The profane is the ordinary world of everyday activity. In this distinction, profane does not mean evil, and sacred does not simply mean morally good.

An object’s material properties do not determine whether it is sacred. A piece of cloth may become a revered flag; an ordinary location may become a place of pilgrimage. What matters is the significance people collectively assign and the treatment they demand. Restrictions on touching, displaying, consuming, or criticizing an object can reveal its sacred status. Disputes over desecration reveal how strongly those boundaries are defended.

Ritual brings these meanings into social action. Through coordinated movement, speech, music, silence, or mourning, participants can experience collective effervescence: a heightened emotional energy generated by gathering together. The group’s power is experienced through its symbols and can renew a sense of duty and solidarity. That solidarity may support care and cooperation, but it can also intensify exclusion and conformity. The same mechanism does not guarantee the same moral outcome.

Must the sacred be supernatural?

A Durkheimian approach allows the sacred to be located in humanity, a nation, a political cause, or a collective ideal. Transcendence points beyond ordinary human or natural existence; immanence locates sacred presence or ultimate significance within the world. Religious traditions can combine the two. A personal creator god is therefore neither the only possible sacred reality nor a necessary feature of every religious tradition.

Religion also helps construct a meaningful world: it connects individual lives to a larger moral order. This world-building role explains why collective commitments can retain religious forms even when they reject conventional theology. It also creates the central difficulty of a broad definition: many nonreligious communities generate meaning, emotion, and obligation.

Civil religion and political sacredness

In “Civil Religion in America” (1967), Robert Bellah argued that American public life contains a religious dimension distinct from particular churches. National ideals can be expressed through sacred histories, founding documents, revered leaders, commemorations, and public rituals. Inaugurations, memorial ceremonies, and invocations of God can present the nation as accountable to a larger moral mission.

The Declaration of Independence and Constitution function as authoritative texts; founders and fallen leaders become exemplary figures; national holidays and commemorative places organize collective memory. Bellah’s argument also allows national conduct to be judged against higher ideals. Civil religion can legitimate state power, but its language can also support criticism of a nation that fails its professed commitments.

The comparison with devotion to Mao asks whether explicitly atheist politics can also acquire sacred forms. A movement can present a comprehensive account of history, identify a path to collective salvation in this world, revere authoritative texts, and organize recurring rituals of commitment. These are functional similarities. They do not establish that communism and Christianity share a theology, or that all political participation is religious. The stronger analytical question is which elements are comparable, for whom, and with what effects.

Apply the three Bs to a political ritual

Choose an inauguration, memorial ceremony, or leader-centered gathering. Identify its beliefs about the community, its repeated actions, and its rules of membership. Then ask what evidence would distinguish sacred devotion from ordinary respect, entertainment, or enforced compliance. A photograph can suggest a comparison; it cannot settle those questions.

Substantive definitions and a workable boundary

Substantive definitions preserve distinctions that broad functional comparisons can blur. In the formulations discussed in the lecture, David Martin emphasizes reference beyond observable reality; Rodney Stark and Roger Finke emphasize supernatural assumptions and relations of exchange; Christian Smith emphasizes practices premised on superhuman powers, personal or impersonal, through which people pursue human goods and avoid harms. The formulations differ, but each asks about the content of a practice rather than its social benefits alone.

Smith’s emphasis on superhuman powers makes room for more than belief in a single personal deity. It also directs attention to practices: people pray, make offerings, meditate, or perform rituals because of what they understand these powers to make possible. By this approach, a group does not become a religion simply because its members find friendship or inspiration there.

Two definitions, two ways of drawing the boundary
ApproachMain strengthMain difficulty
FunctionalRecognizes sacred meaning, ritual, and community across familiar and unfamiliar settings.Can include so much that religion becomes difficult to distinguish from politics, fandom, or other commitments.
SubstantiveDistinguishes religion by reference to superhuman powers or a reality beyond ordinary observation.Can exclude practices that people experience as religious if the definition is too closely modeled on familiar theistic traditions.

A useful compromise distinguishes religion in a substantive sense from quasi-religious or functionally religious forms. The second category identifies similarities without erasing differences. Sunday Assembly may perform congregational functions while explicitly rejecting supernatural content. Civil religion may combine national ritual with references to a higher authority. Each case requires evidence rather than classification by intensity alone.

This distinction will matter throughout the rest of the chapter. A decline in substantive religion can occur alongside the persistence of sacred commitments in other settings. Conversely, calling a new commitment “religion-like” does not demonstrate that it replaces everything an older religion provided.

Secularization: why might modernization weaken religion?

Secularization concerns the declining significance or authority of religion in particular areas of life. Using a substantive understanding of religion, the argument is that modernization may weaken reliance on supernatural explanations, religious institutions, and inherited authority. Several processes must be distinguished because one can occur without the others.

Three processes of secularization

Rationalization and disenchantment expand scientific, technical, and bureaucratic ways of explaining and managing the world. Institutional differentiation separates domains such as medicine, education, law, and government from direct religious control. Privatization makes religion more a matter of personal conviction and voluntary association than a binding framework for the whole society.

These processes suggest mechanisms, rather than a single law of inevitable decline. Medical treatment can reduce reliance on a religious explanation of illness without eliminating prayer. Public welfare can assume tasks once organized by congregations without preventing religious charity. A government can operate through secular law while its citizens remain devout. We should ask which authority or practice is changing and through what institutional process.

Existential security

Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart: security and religious demand

The existential-security argument links religious commitment to vulnerability. When survival, income, health, and family life are insecure, religion can offer reassurance and a framework for coping with uncertainty. Reliable livelihoods, lower mortality, and dependable social protection may reduce some of that demand. The argument concerns experienced security and the conditions of socialization, rather than wealth alone.

Scientific and technological capacities can also increase a sense of human control over life. Meanwhile, humanism, Romanticism, and individualism offer ways to locate meaning or fulfillment within human life and experience. In this account, religion faces alternatives both in practical problem solving and in the search for a meaningful life. Such alternatives can reduce religious authority even among people who continue to believe.

The lecture uses World Values Survey comparisons to illustrate declining religiosity in many settings during roughly 2007–2019. Inglehart’s analysis and the amendment archived by the WVS should be read as a particular study with specified countries, territories, periods, and indicators. Its chart measures change in the importance of God; it does not directly measure every form of ritual, belonging, or public religious power. A trend can support a secularization argument while leaving its causes open to investigation.

Political backlash and value change offer additional explanations. If religious institutions become closely identified with conservative political projects, some people may distance themselves from religion as they reject those projects. Greater emphasis on self-expression and gender equality can also create tension with inherited authority. These processes may operate alongside economic security; observing a decline by itself does not tell us which mechanism produced it.

Three challenges to a simple decline story

1. Does a Western trajectory explain the world?

A theory developed around Western European history may mistake one regional path for a universal sequence. The lecture’s examples of religious revival and public mobilization outside that setting challenge the assumption that modernity necessarily makes religion private or politically irrelevant. Nation building, migration, state policy, and political competition can strengthen religious identities and institutions. The appropriate comparison is among historical trajectories, rather than between societies assumed to occupy earlier and later stages of one path.

2. Is prosperity sufficient?

The lecture uses the United Arab Emirates to question a mechanical equation between wealth and religious disappearance. High development can coexist with public religious institutions and commitments. National income alone does not establish how secure different groups feel, how religion is governed, or how families transmit belief. This challenge limits a deterministic claim that prosperity is sufficient for secularization; it does not by itself disprove a broader statistical association between security and religious change.

3. Does the definition hide transformation?

If we measure only conventional affiliation or supernatural belief, we may miss new ways of organizing sacred commitments. A Durkheimian perspective asks whether meaning, ritual, moral community, and collective devotion have moved into other settings. Political causes, national identities, and consumer cultures may become candidates for comparison. They must still be examined case by case: strong commitment alone does not make every activist, consumer, or atheist religious.

Decline and persistence can occur together

Imagine a society where fewer people join churches, religious organizations lose control over schools, and national commemorations become more emotionally intense. The first two observations support particular secularization claims; the third raises a question about civil religion. The observations concern different objects, so they need not contradict one another.

Religion under conditions of choice

Modern religious change involves more than disappearance or revival. When people encounter multiple worldviews, inherited religion becomes one possibility among alternatives. Pluralism changes the conditions of belief: an institution must increasingly explain why its teachings deserve commitment from people who could choose differently. Even a person who remains in a family tradition may now experience that membership as a choice.

Peter Berger’s account of pluralism highlights this movement from taken-for-granted certainty toward competing claims. Stark and Finke’s religious-market perspective emphasizes a different possibility: competition can encourage organizations to innovate, recruit, and meet varied preferences. Pluralism may therefore weaken inherited authority while encouraging new organizational activity. Neither outcome is guaranteed; family ties, regulation, and unequal resources shape the available choices.

Four overlapping transformations help explain religion in this environment. They describe shifts in emphasis and authority, rather than four stages through which every religion must pass. Ethical concern, healing, sensory experience, and economic exchange have long existed in religious life. The question is how they are reorganized and how much authority they now carry.

Ethicalization: religion as a way of living well

Ethicalization places greater emphasis on moral character, compassion, service, and social good relative to ritual correctness or doctrinal precision. A person may justify religious participation primarily by saying that it makes people kinder or improves the community. In a pluralistic setting, such claims can be communicated to people who do not share the tradition’s theology.

This emphasis can adapt religion to universal moral reasoning and concerns about life in this world. It may also accompany demythologization, in which inherited stories are interpreted more symbolically and less as literal accounts. Ethicalization does not require the complete abandonment of supernatural belief. The analytical issue is which standard becomes decisive when doctrinal authority, ritual obligation, and moral judgment appear to conflict.

Therapeuticalization: religion and the psychological self

Therapeuticalization, or psychologization, makes psychological well-being a central measure of religious value. People may evaluate a teaching or community by whether it supports self-worth, reduces anxiety, helps them cope, or enables personal growth. The shift concerns the criterion of authority: a practice is increasingly justified by how it makes the individual feel and flourish.

Philip Rieff and therapeutic culture

In The Triumph of the Therapeutic (1966), Rieff contrasted a religious orientation toward salvation and binding moral demands with a psychological orientation toward personal satisfaction. His critique helps us ask what happens when institutions are expected to accommodate the self rather than form the self through obligations. This is an argument about cultural authority, rather than a claim that traditional religions offered no comfort or healing.

Sheilaism: the individual as an authority

In Habits of the Heart (1985), Robert Bellah and his coauthors described a woman, given the name Sheila Larson, who called her personal faith “Sheilaism.” She emphasized an inner guiding voice, gentleness toward herself, and care for others rather than regular church participation. The case illustrates religious individualism: the person selects and authorizes a spiritual path. Ask what enables that freedom, and what happens to shared obligations when each person becomes the final judge of religious truth and practice.

Aestheticization: feeling the sacred

Aestheticization makes sensory and emotional experience increasingly important in how religion is presented and evaluated. Music, lighting, architecture, images, bodily movement, and atmosphere can make an event feel moving, beautiful, or uplifting. Participants may judge a gathering by the experience it produces, sometimes giving less weight to doctrinal understanding or ongoing duty.

Religions have always engaged the senses. The proposed transformation is therefore a change in emphasis: an experience may become a central reason for choosing, returning to, or leaving a community. A service can be both aesthetically compelling and doctrinally demanding. The sociological question is whether sensory appeal supports other commitments or becomes the primary standard by which they are judged.

Commodification: sacred life in the marketplace

Commodification organizes religious goods, symbols, practices, experiences, or forms of access as products for exchange. Books, objects, retreats, apps, and online content can be packaged, priced, branded, and selected among competitors. Religious organizations may address people increasingly as consumers whose attention and continued participation must be won.

Economic exchange itself is not new in religion. The analytical issue is how market relationships reshape authority and participation. An illustrative wellness subscription might encourage a convenient daily practice while making access depend on payment. A retreat might offer a powerful experience while detaching selected practices from the obligations of a wider tradition. Who controls interpretation, who receives revenue, and who can afford participation become questions about religious power as well as consumer choice.

These transformations can reinforce one another. A program can promise ethical improvement, emotional relief, and an inspiring atmosphere while selling individualized access. That combination might sustain substantive religious belief, translate a tradition for new audiences, or produce a religion-like practice detached from its original theology. We need to investigate the resulting beliefs, practices, and communities.

Return to the boundary: Harry Potter and Snapeism

The lecture ends with the Harry Potter franchise and a small online community studied by Zoe Alderton in “‘Snapewives’ and ‘Snapeism’” (2014). The participants she studied understood Severus Snape as a spiritual being who existed beyond the novels and could guide their lives. Alderton described claims of channeling, spiritual relationships, and a shared theology. Her article treats a historically specific group, already disbanded by the time of her conclusion, rather than a representative sample of Harry Potter fans.

This case differs from enjoying a story or attending a fan convention. If participants claim an independently existing superhuman being, the group may meet a substantive definition as well as a functional one. The disagreement therefore cannot be resolved simply by saying that fandom provides belonging. We must ask what participants believe exists and how that belief organizes their lives.

The broader franchise also brings the chapter’s themes together. Commercial media distribute stories and symbols; audiences interpret them, create communities, and sometimes invest them with sacred significance. A purchased cultural product can become a resource for commitments its producers do not control. That possibility connects commodification with individual agency, rather than assuming that consumers merely absorb meanings provided by a brand.

Make a classification argument

Compare three cases: an ordinary fan convention, Sunday Assembly, and the Snapeism community Alderton studied. For each, identify evidence of belief, behavior, and belonging. Then apply both a functional and a substantive definition. State what further evidence would change your classification. The task is to explain the boundary consistently, including when a practice seems unfamiliar.

Studying decline and transformation together

The chapter begins and ends with the same problem: our definition shapes what we can see. Durkheim directs attention to sacred boundaries, ritual, and moral community. Substantive definitions preserve the importance of superhuman referents. Secularization theories explain changes in religious authority through rationalization, differentiation, security, and privatization. The transformations discussed above show how religion may also adapt to pluralism, individual choice, and markets.

A sociological explanation identifies the population, institution, period, and dimension of religion being studied. It then asks which mechanisms best explain the evidence. Declining affiliation does not establish that every sacred commitment is disappearing, and a new source of collective enthusiasm does not establish that religion has fully returned.

Researchers can study these processes while bracketing the truth of supernatural claims. This means investigating how beliefs are learned, practiced, authorized, and experienced without asking sociology to settle metaphysical questions beyond its methods. The approach applies to established traditions and unfamiliar communities alike.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Belief, behavior, and belonging are connected dimensions of religion that must be measured separately.
  • Functional definitions emphasize sacred meanings, rituals, and moral communities; substantive definitions emphasize superhuman powers or realities.
  • Durkheim explains how sacred boundaries and collective rituals make shared moral life possible.
  • Civil religion and devotion to political leaders test the boundary between religion and functionally similar forms.
  • Rationalization, differentiation, privatization, and existential security offer distinct explanations of secularization.
  • Regional variation, affluent religious societies, and changing definitions challenge an inevitable, universal decline story.
  • Pluralism makes inherited authority less taken for granted and creates conditions for choice and competition.
  • Ethicalization, therapeuticalization, aestheticization, and commodification describe overlapping transformations of religious life.
  • Sheilaism illustrates personal spiritual authority; Snapeism shows why unfamiliar claims require consistent sociological analysis.
  • Decline in one dimension can coexist with persistence or transformation in another.

Questions for study

  1. How could someone believe without belonging, or belong without strong belief? What would a survey need to ask?
  2. How would functional and substantive definitions classify Sunday Assembly differently?
  3. What distinguishes the sacred from the profane? Why does sacredness not require a personal god?
  4. Use the three Bs to analyze civil religion or devotion to Mao. Where does the comparison with a conventional religion become limited?
  5. Why is recognizing an unfamiliar religion a question about institutional power as well as definition?
  6. Explain how rationalization, differentiation, privatization, and existential security could produce different kinds of change.
  7. What does the UAE example challenge? What would it take to test existential-security theory more fully?
  8. How can pluralism both weaken inherited certainty and encourage religious innovation?
  9. Distinguish ethicalization, therapeuticalization, aestheticization, and commodification using examples. How might they overlap?
  10. What does Sheilaism suggest about the relationship between individual choice and shared obligations?
  11. Why might Snapeism meet a substantive definition when ordinary fandom does not? What evidence matters?
  12. How can a sociologist investigate religious change without deciding whether a supernatural belief is true?
