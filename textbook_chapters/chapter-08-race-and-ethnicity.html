Race, Ethnicity, and Racism

What these categories mean, how they acquire social power, and why sociologists debate how to interpret them.

Race and ethnicity are not merely personal identities. They are social classifications whose meanings and consequences are produced through history, institutions, culture, and interaction. This chapter first defines the concepts in the same sequence as the Week 9 lecture, then places major arguments about race and ethnicity in conversation with one another.

Imagine siblings whose ancestry is identical but who are classified differently because observers read their skin tone differently, or a person counted as White under one census rule and Middle Eastern or North African under another. Their genes did not change when the form changed. Yet the category can affect political representation, civil-rights enforcement, research, identity, and how others interpret them. The sociological task is to explain both halves of this fact: racial and ethnic boundaries are historically made, and classifications that are made can still have durable material and psychological effects.

What are race and ethnicity?

Race is a category based on beliefs about shared ancestry and perceived biological or physical traits, especially visible traits such as skin color. Sociologically, race is a social construction: what matters is not only what a trait is, but what people and institutions perceive it to mean. Racial categories are not fixed biological divisions, but racial classifications are real in their effects because they shape treatment, opportunity, belonging, and exclusion.

Calling race socially constructed does not deny human biological variation. Populations can differ statistically in the frequency of some inherited traits, and ancestry can be estimated probabilistically. The sociological point is that variation is overlapping and often gradual rather than naturally divided into the few bounded, hierarchical races societies have named. Institutions select certain visible differences, group them, attach ancestry stories and moral meanings, and use them to allocate membership and resources. Racialization is the process through which a population, practice, religion, or nationality comes to be interpreted through racial meanings.

Ethnicity refers to a shared cultural heritage or origin. It may involve language, religion, national origin, history, traditions, food, family ties, and a sense of common identity. Unlike race, ethnicity emphasizes culture and belonging rather than presumed biological difference. In practice, however, race and ethnicity often overlap, and the same group may be classified differently across time and place.

Concept: social construction

The process through which people create, interpret, institutionalize, and change the meanings attached to categories, identities, and social realities. A socially constructed category is not imaginary; it can organize institutions and produce very material consequences.

Race has a history

Human beings have always categorized one another, but the modern idea of “race” is relatively recent. Earlier societies were more likely to classify people through religion, language, lineage, or geographic location. From the era of European exploration onward, racial classifications such as “Caucasoid,” “Mongoloid,” “Negroid,” and “Australoid” were used to sort populations. These labels were never neutral: they often expressed a hierarchy of superiority and inferiority connected to political power, economic resources, and technological dominance. Racial differentiation therefore became a form of racial stratification.

The history of the U.S. census makes the constructed character of race visible. Census categories have changed repeatedly: “white,” “slave,” “free colored,” “black,” “mulatto,” “Mexican,” “Chinese,” “Japanese,” “Hindu,” “Korean,” “Filipino,” “American Indian,” and other categories have been added, removed, renamed, or redefined. The 2020 census explains that its racial categories reflect a social definition of race, not an attempt to define race biologically, anthropologically, or genetically. People may also report more than one race, and Hispanic or Latino origin may be reported by people of any race.

Federal standards changed again in 2024. The Office of Management and Budget replaced the older separate race and Hispanic-origin questions with a combined race-and-ethnicity format that permits multiple selections and added Middle Eastern or North African (MENA) as a minimum category. The Census Bureau plans to use the combined format in the American Community Survey and the 2030 Census. This change will improve recognition for some respondents but also complicates comparisons with older data. Classification systems do not merely describe populations; they shape which populations become statistically visible.

Why categories matter

If a government changes who counts as “white,” “Mexican,” “Asian,” or “American Indian,” it is not merely changing vocabulary. It is changing how people are counted, represented, governed, and sometimes granted or denied rights. Categories are made by institutions, but they can also be challenged and remade by social movements.

Ethnic boundaries, symbolic ethnicity, and group position

Ethnic traditions matter, but groups are not simply containers of ancient cultural traits. Fredrik Barth emphasized the boundary that distinguishes members from nonmembers. Language, dress, religion, ancestry, or food may mark the boundary, and the markers can change while a sense of group continuity remains. States, migration, intermarriage, schools, and political organizations can harden, blur, or redraw such boundaries.

Herbert Gans: symbolic ethnicity

Gans used symbolic ethnicity to describe a voluntary, intermittent identification with an ancestral heritage among later-generation descendants of European immigrants in the United States. A person might celebrate a holiday, cook a family dish, or claim an ancestry without organizing everyday work, residence, or marriage around the group. This relatively optional form contrasts with identities imposed by outsiders or backed by discrimination. Whether an identity can be switched off is therefore itself a form of social advantage.

A sociological minority group is not defined only by numerical size. It is a category whose members experience subordinate power or unequal treatment; a numerical majority may be politically subordinate, and a small group may be dominant. Racial and ethnic identities also intersect with class, religion, gender, nationality, color, and generation, producing substantial variation within every broad census category.

Racial formation and racial projects

Ethnic boundaries and unequal group positions raise a further question: how do classifications become part of the organization of a society? A census names populations, people accept or challenge those names, and institutions act on the classifications. Michael Omi and Howard Winant call the larger historical process racial formation: the making, inhabiting, transformation, and destruction of racial categories. Their approach connects representations of difference with the organization of institutions and resources.

Categorization, signification, and structure

Working definition: racial project

A racial project involves categorizing people on the basis of perceived biological traits, assigning meanings to and developing explanations for those distinctions, and organizing or reorganizing the distribution of resources along those categorical lines.

This classroom formulation makes the initial construction of race explicit. A project can help create a classification rather than merely interpret a race that already exists. Perceived bodily traits become the basis of categories; stories about ancestry, character, ability, or belonging make those distinctions meaningful; and institutions connect membership to rights, opportunities, labor, land, or protection. Not every participant must consciously design all three elements. An organization can inherit a classification and reproduce its consequences through ordinary routines.

Signification concerns what a racial distinction means: how bodies are categorized, what explanations accompany the categories, and which identities appear legitimate or threatening. Structure concerns who gets what: how laws, markets, organizations, and political arrangements distribute resources and power. These are connected dimensions, rather than mutually exclusive types of project. Rules can strengthen a belief in racial difference, while racial meanings can justify rules.

Historical construction: slavery, ancestry, and racial hierarchy

European conquest and Atlantic slavery connected classifications of human difference to control over land, labor, and citizenship. Colonial institutions did not simply discover populations already divided into the modern races. They helped make ancestry an inherited condition with unequal consequences. Virginia's 1662 law, for example, made a child's enslaved or free status follow the mother's condition. Law helped reproduce a labor hierarchy across generations.

Religious justifications and claims of natural inferiority accompanied these arrangements; later racial science supplied further rationalizations. The chronology matters: Atlantic slavery and colonial racial laws preceded nineteenth-century Darwinian theories. Scientific racism did not originate all of these institutions. It participated in their later explanation and legitimation.

The Nazi racial project against Jews

Nazi ideology and the Nuremberg race laws classified Jewish ancestry as an inherited racial threat, including people whose religious practice or self-identification did not fit that label. This signification was joined to exclusion from citizenship and occupations, forced dispossession, deportation, and genocide. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's account of “Aryanization” documents the transfer of Jewish-owned businesses and property to non-Jewish owners. The case connects imposed classification, dehumanizing meaning, and organized material domination. Economic theft was one component of persecution; it does not fully explain the Holocaust.

Omi and Winant's racial-formation framework uses “racial project” more broadly than this working definition, encompassing projects that interpret race while seeking to change its institutional significance. The distinction becomes important when discussing colorblindness below.

The “race problem”: who or what needs to change?

Racial projects also define what counts as the problem to be solved. In hierarchical framings, racialized populations themselves became “problems”: supposed inferiority, racial mixing, or an alleged threat to the dominant population supplied reasons for exclusion. The proposed solutions followed from that diagnosis. If difference supposedly meant natural rank or contamination, domination and separation could appear justified to those who accepted the premise.

W. E. B. Du Bois: changing the question

In The Philadelphia Negro (1899), Du Bois investigated what contemporaries called “the Negro problem” through an empirical study of social conditions. He examined employment, housing, community life, and the effects of prejudice and exclusion. This shifted attention toward the relationships and institutions constraining Black Americans. The question became how historically produced conditions and unequal treatment created social problems, rather than how an allegedly inferior population should be managed.

The study illustrates why framing matters. Once inequality and discrimination become the problem, changing institutions and expanding equal citizenship become possible remedies. The older framing did not simply disappear, but equality and justice supplied a different basis for corrective racial projects.

A battleground of competing projects concerning race

Du Bois's work helps mark a change in the question: from managing an allegedly inferior population to investigating the conditions that obstruct equal citizenship. That shift did not settle how equality should be pursued. Contemporary disagreement concerns both how to understand racial inequality and which institutions should do what about it.

Progressive racial projects: redefining meaning and changing conditions

Progressive anti-racist projects challenge racial domination. At the level of signification, they may reclaim a stigmatized identity or describe an outcome as systemic rather than the fault of isolated individuals. At the level of structure, they may seek equal-treatment enforcement, school access, changes in policing, investment in underserved communities, or racial preferences in selection. These are different interventions. A campaign against discriminatory hiring and a rule that uses racial membership to allocate a scarce position do not pose identical questions.

Their shared concern is that repealing discriminatory law may leave unequal assets, residential patterns, networks, and treatment in place. The end of a formal restriction does not automatically reverse everything it produced. Whether a particular legacy persists, through what mechanism, and what remedy would address it remain empirical questions.

Neoconservative colorblind projects: equal treatment and limits on classification

The colorblind approach emphasizes equal individual treatment and opposes allocating opportunities by racial membership. It can accept enforcement against discrimination, acknowledge historical injustice, and support assistance based on need while contesting racial preferences. Some versions also minimize the continuing importance of racism; others recognize it but reject preferences on legal or moral grounds. Those arguments should be examined separately.

Under our narrower definition, colorblind or non-racial project identifies an effort to remove race from allocation rather than organize allocation through racial categories. The umbrella phrase “competing projects concerning race” accommodates both approaches. In Omi and Winant's broader usage, colorblindness can itself be a racial project: it interprets the significance of race and proposes an institutional arrangement. The vocabulary differs because the definitions differ.

The additional burden of justifying racial allocation

Enforcing a rule against unequal treatment and introducing a preference by racial membership require different arguments. Historical exclusion can establish a reason to investigate continuing disadvantage. It does not alone establish that a present racial preference is necessary, well targeted, proportionate, or fair to other individuals. A defender must connect the proposed remedy to the relevant barrier and compare alternatives. A critic must also explain how the preferred alternative would address continuing discriminatory conduct or inherited obstacles. Neither position is established simply by pointing to a disparity or to the repeal of an old law.

Descriptive detection and prescriptive engineering

Two uses of categories

Descriptive detection: using racial categories to identify and measure unequal conditions or discriminatory treatment.

Prescriptive engineering: using racial categories to determine the institutional allocation of opportunities, rights, or resources.

Purpose and method are separate dimensions. A project may seek to preserve hierarchy or correct inequality, and it may emphasize classification and explanation or rules governing allocation. Detection belongs mainly to signification and engineering mainly to structure, but both can link meanings to institutional consequences. Scientific racism presented itself as description while legitimizing domination; a corrective policy can change resources while reinforcing a classification.

Detection: making unequal treatment visible

In Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan's 2004 résumé experiment, researchers sent fictitious applications to employers in Boston and Chicago, randomly assigning names perceived as White or African American. Applications with White-sounding names received about 50 percent more callbacks. Random assignment supported an inference about employers' responses to the name signals, rather than an uncontrolled comparison of actual applicants. The result concerns perceived race in that labor-market setting, not a complete explanation of racial employment gaps.

This example shows why rejecting racial preferences need not require abandoning racial research. Researchers can measure discriminatory responses without assigning real employment opportunities by race. Conversely, a descriptive group difference without such a research design does not automatically establish discrimination.

Engineering: choosing a remedy and a decision rule

A racial preference requires decisions about which categories count, who qualifies, how much weight membership carries, and whose opportunity changes. Evidence of unequal treatment can justify intervention without settling those design choices. The question is whether this rule addresses this mechanism better than feasible alternatives and at an acceptable cost.

One institution, four different choices

A hypothetical university could collect voluntary demographic data to investigate unequal access, recruit in underserved schools, offer aid based on financial need, or give racial membership weight in selection. An evaluator should identify the specific practice rather than treating all four as interchangeable meanings of “affirmative action.”

The paradox of progressive racial projects

A progressive project can seek to dismantle racial inequality while repeatedly invoking the categories inherited from racial stratification. Counting, comparing, representing, and allocating by category can expose exclusion and expand access. Those same practices may make the categories seem more natural, internally uniform, or permanent. Reification means treating a human-made abstraction as if it were a fixed thing.

The paradox

Progressive racial projects may challenge the unequal consequences of racial classification while reproducing the boundaries through which people understand and organize racial difference.

This is a possible tension, not a finding that every corrective project fails. Distinguish a material outcome, such as degree completion, from a categorical outcome, such as increased pressure to prove group membership. A program could improve one while worsening the other. Showing that racial labels are repeated is also different from showing that participants increasingly regard them as biological or permanent.

Anthony Giddens's idea of structuration helps explain the mechanism: social structures are conditions of action and are reproduced or changed through action. People use existing classifications to make claims and obtain remedies; those actions can renew the authority of the classifications. They can also alter meanings, open boundaries, or make a category less relevant. The outcome depends on the practice, its setting, and how people respond.

One paradox, three families of perspectives

The lecture groups responses into defending, auditing, and retiring. These are families of questions, not established schools with identical policy conclusions. Omi and Winant offer a theory of formation; Bleemer evaluates a policy change; the SFFA dissents develop legal arguments. Their evidence does different work. Likewise, an auditor may favor reform, broader redistribution, or repeal.

Family A: the defending perspective

The defending family argues that formal equality can coexist with inherited barriers and continuing discrimination. It asks whether institutions must attend to race to understand and counter those barriers. The four strands below develop different parts of that case: a framework for institutions and meanings, research on legitimating explanations, an estimate of policy effects, and a legal account of equal opportunity.

Omi and Winant: racial formation and institutional consequences

Approach. In Racial Formation in the United States (first published in 1986), Omi and Winant connect historical changes in racial meaning to political struggles and institutional arrangements. This is a historical-theoretical framework, rather than an experiment estimating one remedy's effect. It directs attention to how apparently ordinary rules acquire racial consequences and how competing projects remake them.

Contribution to the paradox. Rejecting racial language is itself consequential if institutions continue to operate on unequal conditions. The framework therefore supports investigating what a declaration of neutrality actually changes. Limit. It does not, by itself, establish that a particular preference is necessary or effective. Explaining how race becomes consequential and justifying a specific allocation rule are separate tasks. See their account of racial formation.

Eduardo Bonilla-Silva: ideology after explicit exclusion

Study and method. Bonilla-Silva and Tyrone Forman's “I Am Not a Racist But...” (2000) compared survey answers and interviews with White students at three universities. The interviews revealed qualifications and explanations that closed-ended responses often concealed. Participants could endorse equality in general while describing particular inequalities or remedies in ways that discounted racial barriers.

Bonilla-Silva, Amanda Lewis, and David Embrick's 2004 study of racial storylines and testimonies used college-student and Detroit-area interview material to examine recurring accounts, including stories in which White people presented themselves as the victims of racial preferences. The object of study was how explanations make an unequal social order intelligible and defensible.

Contribution and limit. The broader argument in Racism without Racists is that inequality can be normalized without openly asserting biological inferiority. Researchers should examine what explanations obscure as well as what they state. These qualitative findings do not establish that every objection to a preference disguises prejudice, or that preferences are the best remedy. Arguments about individual rights and policy effects still require direct assessment.

Zachary Bleemer: evaluating California's Proposition 209

Study and method. Bleemer's “Affirmative Action, Mismatch, and Economic Mobility After California's Proposition 209” (2022) linked University of California applicants from 1994–2002 to later educational and earnings records. The ban on race-conscious admissions took effect in 1998. A difference-in-differences design compared changes for underrepresented minority applicants with changes for other applicants across the policy transition.

Findings. The ban shifted underrepresented minority applicants toward less selective institutions and reduced degree attainment and later earnings. The results challenge the claim that, in this setting, ending preferences would generally help affected applicants by placing them in institutions where they would perform better. They give the defending family evidence about educational and material consequences, beyond a change in campus representation.

Limit. This is an observational policy evaluation whose causal interpretation depends on the comparison and assumptions about what would have happened without the ban. It concerns a particular system, period, and applicant population. Applicant gains are not a measure of benefits to every racial-group member, nor do they settle fairness, categorical costs, or the performance of every feasible alternative.

The SFFA dissents: equal opportunity as a legal argument

Case and approach. Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA) challenged admissions at Harvard and the University of North Carolina. In the 2023 opinions, Justice Sonia Sotomayor defended limited consideration of race through precedent, educational opportunity, and a substantive understanding of equality. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson emphasized how historical exclusion can continue through family wealth and opportunity. Jackson participated in the UNC case only; she recused herself from Harvard's case.

Contribution to the paradox. The dissents argue that attention to race can help institutions evaluate opportunity in its actual social context. Using a category can serve an equality-promoting purpose rather than assume that all members possess the same character or outlook.

Limit. These are legal and normative arguments, not experimental estimates of admissions outcomes. They were dissents, not the controlling ruling. A commitment to meaningful opportunity still requires explaining which uses of race are permissible, how individual applicants are treated, and when a remedy should change or end.

Defending: insights and limits

The family's strongest insight is that ending an explicitly discriminatory rule need not eliminate the disadvantages or ongoing practices connected to it. Its policy-evaluation strand also shows why effectiveness should be investigated rather than assumed absent. But the defense becomes stronger when it states the additional burdens of justification directly:

  • Diagnosis and fit. A disparity does not by itself identify discrimination, a causal mechanism, or the remedy that addresses it.
  • Classification and internal diversity. Broad, changing categories may combine people with very different wealth, migration histories, preparation, and needs. Membership is an imperfect proxy for disadvantage.
  • Fairness and categorical costs. Benefits to selected recipients do not alone justify racial allocation. Assess effects on other applicants, individual equal treatment, and the reinforcement of boundaries.
  • Reach. Access to an elite institution for a small number of recipients may coexist with little improvement for the wider population.
  • Alternatives. Compare feasible outreach, equal-treatment enforcement, and assistance based on need or place. Whether these can achieve the relevant objective is a question for evidence; they should not be described categorically as untested.
  • Objectives and endpoints. Specify what success means, how progress will be measured, and what would trigger continuation, revision, or termination.

Several concerns overlap with the SFFA majority's objections. Applying equal-protection requirements to UNC and Title VI to Harvard, the Court found the challenged programs lacked sufficiently measurable objectives, used problematic racial categories, risked stereotyping and negative treatment of other applicants, and lacked meaningful endpoints. The Congressional Research Service's account of the ruling explains the legal framework. These objections address the justification and design of the admissions programs; the ruling does not establish that racial inequality has disappeared.

Family B: the auditing perspective

The auditing family asks who actually gains from a project, who bears its costs, and what changes beyond its stated intention. The lecture brings together opinion research, class analysis, theories of elite capture and commodification, and comparative policy criticism. Their shared concern is distribution and institutional incentives. Their explanations and political conclusions differ.

Hidden Tribes: profiling an activist segment

Study and method. More in Common's Hidden Tribes: A Study of America's Polarized Landscape (2018) used a weighted online survey of about 8,000 U.S. adults and grouped respondents by patterns of underlying beliefs. Its seven segments were analytical profiles, rather than existing organizations. “Progressive Activists” comprised about 8 percent of the sample.

Finding relevant to the audit. The segment profiles report that Progressive Activists were 80 percent White and 59 percent college educated, compared with 69 and 29 percent respectively in the overall sample. Their relatively advantaged profile raises a question about the relationship between highly engaged advocates and the populations in whose name they speak.

Limit. The 80 percent figure refers to racial identification; it does not mean 80 percent were simultaneously White, wealthy, and highly educated. The study does not demonstrate that activists avoid policy costs or that their preferred policies fail. A demographic profile is a starting point for investigating representation, not a causal test of elite capture, sincerity, or effectiveness.

Adolph Reed Jr. and Walter Benn Michaels: diversity and class inequality

Work and approach. Reed and Michaels's essays, collected in No Politics but Class Politics (2023), develop a political-economic critique of treating representative diversity as a sufficient measure of justice. This is an argument about the organization of inequality, rather than a single statistical estimate of a preference program.

Core distinction. Changing the racial distribution of people in highly paid positions can leave the number of low-paid positions, the power of employers, and the distance between rich and poor largely unchanged. In a hypothetical corporation, diversifying executives and raising workers' wages answer different distributive questions. The authors emphasize labor politics and broad material provision as alternatives to making elite representation the main horizon of reform.

Contribution and limit. Their critique asks whether a project's measure of success matches the hardship it invokes. Greater diversity at the top cannot stand in for improvements throughout a population. However, this distinction does not show that discriminatory barriers within a class are unimportant, or that broad redistribution automatically removes them. Representation, equal treatment, and economic redistribution may be pursued together; their relative effects require evidence.

Olúfẹ́mi O. Táíwò: elite capture and constructive politics

Work and mechanism. In “Identity Politics and Elite Capture” (2020) and Elite Capture (2022), Táíwò examines how people already positioned to control institutions shape political agendas and resources. Capture is more than a person having selfish motives. Unequal access to decision-making allows the priorities of a relatively privileged subset to become the apparent needs of a much larger population.

He contrasts narrow elite agendas with the coalition-building ambition associated with the Combahee River Collective. Identity politics can begin from concrete experiences of oppression and build shared material demands; it need not culminate in competition over which elite individual represents a category. This separates his argument from a simple claim that class should replace race in every analysis.

Contribution and limit. For the paradox, the question is whether naming a group opens political participation or authorizes a few gatekeepers to speak for it. His constructive response emphasizes changing institutions and expanding people's capacity to act together. In applying the theory, researchers must identify the relevant decision-makers, excluded constituencies, and diverted resources. Calling an outcome “capture” without that evidence risks turning a mechanism into a general accusation.

Barbara and John Ehrenreich: the professional-managerial class

Work and approach. In “The Professional-Managerial Class” (1977), the Ehrenreichs describe salaried mental workers whose occupational roles help manage production and reproduce capitalist culture and class relations. Expertise, credentials, and organizational authority distinguish this class location from both ownership and much manual labor. Their analysis concerns an occupational formation; it is not an original study of contemporary diversity programs.

Application to the audit. Extending this framework to progressive racial projects suggests questions about who obtains professional jurisdiction. Does a reform redistribute resources to affected people, expand administrative work and expert authority, or do both? For example, a hypothetical institution might fund new compliance positions while leaving poorly paid employees' conditions largely unchanged. That is an illustration of a question to investigate, not an empirical finding from the 1977 essay.

Limit. Professional authority can also supply useful services, organize workers, and enforce rights. Professionals differ greatly in income, security, and power. The framework cannot establish that a particular reform is ineffective merely because it creates professional jobs.

Nancy Leong: deriving value from another person's racial identity

Work and approach. Leong's “Racial Capitalism” (2013) uses legal analysis and institutional examples to examine the extraction of social or economic value from another person's racial identity, particularly value that predominantly White institutions obtain from non-White identity. This is her specific use of the term; it should not be treated as a summary of every theory called racial capitalism.

Mechanism. An institution can acquire prestige by displaying diversity while treating the people who supply it as instruments of institutional reputation. In a hypothetical university, promotional images might signal inclusion without changing students' access to support or influence. The problem is not simply that an organization benefits from diversity. It is whether that value is extracted on unequal terms and substitutes for substantive change.

Contribution and limit. Leong links classification to the production of institutional value: a marked identity becomes an asset others can use. Her argument identifies risks of commodification and shallow compliance. It does not establish that every diversity initiative exploits participants or produces no gains. An audit needs evidence about terms of participation, resources, bargaining power, and actual outcomes.

Musa al-Gharbi: symbolic capital and elite status competition

Work and approach. In We Have Never Been Woke: The Cultural Contradictions of a New Elite (2024), al-Gharbi examines “symbolic capitalists”: people whose position depends heavily on knowledge, credentials, networks, and cultural authority. He connects social-justice discourse to competition over status, institutional influence, and resources within this social stratum.

Core claim. Social-justice discourse can advance symbolic capitalists' elite status while delivering limited benefits to disadvantaged racial minorities. The audit asks whether recognition, professional advancement, or institutional prestige translates into material improvements for those invoked in the argument. This overlaps with Leong's concern about value extraction, but its focus is the social position and competition of a professional elite rather than the valuation of another person's identity alone.

Limit. Al-Gharbi explicitly allows sincere conviction to coexist with self-interest. His argument should not be reduced to exposing hypocrisy. Nor does elite advantage prove that beneficiaries outside the elite receive nothing. Testing the claim requires identifying the population, period, rewards, and material outcomes rather than treating every moral commitment as evidence of status seeking.

Thomas Sowell: a comparative audit of group preferences

Work and method. In Affirmative Action Around the World (2004), summarized in his adapted book essay, Sowell compares group-preference policies across societies, including India, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, and the United States. His historical-comparative approach examines stated objectives, recipients, political incentives, and unintended effects.

Claims relevant to the audit. He argues that preferences often benefit relatively advantaged members of eligible groups, that temporary arrangements can become politically entrenched, and that competition over eligibility can intensify group conflict. He also challenges inferring discrimination solely from unequal group outcomes. His questions connect program design to within-group distribution and to the incentives created by categorical eligibility.

Placement and limit. Sowell fits the auditing family by method; his policy conclusions favor ending preferences. That conclusion is distinct from abandoning racial data or research. Cross-national comparisons are useful for identifying recurring problems, but countries differ in histories, institutions, and policy designs. Such comparisons do not by themselves isolate a policy's causal effect or establish that every preference fails. His conclusions should be compared with focused evaluations such as Bleemer's, asking why contexts or findings differ.

Auditing: insights and limits

  • Follow the distribution. Distinguish disadvantaged recipients, already advantaged group members, intermediaries, institutions, and people who lose an opportunity. “The group benefits” may conceal very different distributions.
  • Separate mechanisms. Activist demographics, elite control of decisions, professional job creation, identity commodification, and policy failure are different claims. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others.
  • Allow mixed outcomes. A program can serve an institution's interests and improve recipients' lives. Personal benefit neither proves insincerity nor cancels a useful effect.
  • Make capture testable. State whose resources or authority increased, through what process, relative to which alternative. Identify findings that would count against the explanation.
  • Evaluate replacements. An audit can justify repair or repeal. Broader redistribution, voluntary action, need-based aid, and equal-treatment enforcement each need credible comparisons rather than assumed superiority.

The family therefore does more than ask whether an advocate is privileged. Its strongest form connects an identifiable institutional process to a measurable distribution of gains and costs.

Family C: the retiring perspective

The retiring family asks which uses of racial categories should be reduced, reshaped, or discontinued. Its concern is that classifications can acquire authority beyond the purpose for which they were introduced. The authors below offer related critiques of groupism, race-thinking, fixed identity, and explanations that naturalize inequality. They do not constitute one school or recommend identical policies.

Rogers Brubaker: categories are not necessarily groups

Work and approach. In “Ethnicity without Groups” (2002), Brubaker criticizes groupism: treating ethnic or racial populations as bounded, internally homogeneous groups that act with a shared interest. His conceptual analysis asks researchers to investigate categorization, organizations, political entrepreneurs, and moments of collective mobilization rather than assuming cohesive groups from the outset.

Mechanism. A category can matter without everyone classified under it sharing an identity or agenda. An organization may attempt to produce a sense of common membership, and a crisis may intensify solidarity. “Groupness” is therefore variable and contingent, rather than a permanent property of a census label.

Contribution and limit. A progressive project can reify a population when it treats one leader's claim as the voice of everyone classified within it. Brubaker's alternative is to study how such representative claims are made and accepted. This is an analytical warning, not a demonstration that collective organization is illegitimate or that demographic data should disappear. The researcher must still explain when solidarity and common exposure to exclusion are real and consequential.

Paul Gilroy: beyond raciology and racial sorting

Work and approach. In Against Race: Imagining Political Culture Beyond the Color Line (2000), Gilroy challenges raciology, the habits of thought that organize humanity into supposedly fundamental racial kinds. His 2001 keynote on racial identity and political culture develops the critique through cultural and political analysis, including the relationship between racial imagery, consumer culture, and corporate power.

Core concern. A politics of liberation can reproduce racial sorting when authenticity, solidarity, or authority depends on policing inherited membership. Marketable images of racial pride can also become commercial spectacles without transforming the conditions of those represented. Gilroy's alternative seeks forms of human solidarity that do not depend on racial essence.

Contribution and limit. This is an anti-racist aspiration, not a claim that racism has already disappeared. Gilroy explicitly recognizes continuing racialized danger and the limits of conceptual change alone. Applied to a policy, the aspiration leaves practical questions: how can people organize against exclusion, preserve valuable solidarities, and document unequal treatment while reducing the authority of racial boundaries?

Kwame Anthony Appiah: identity and the Medusa Syndrome

Work and approach. Appiah distinguishes the rejection of biological racial essences from recognition that racialized social identities affect people's lives. Color Conscious (1996, with Amy Gutmann) develops questions of race and political morality. The Ethics of Identity (2005) examines the relation between collective identity and individual freedom; its discussion of the Medusa Syndrome belongs to this latter book. These are philosophical arguments, rather than empirical tests of an admissions policy. See the excerpt from The Ethics of Identity.

Mechanism and application. The Medusa image points to the risk that official recognition will freeze an identity that is actually changing and contested. Applied to progressive projects, an institution might recognize a population to remedy exclusion but then require fixed membership or treat a selected interpretation as authentic. This is an application of the concern to our paradox, not a claim that Appiah regards every use of identity as mistaken.

Contribution and limit. Identities can support dignity and solidarity while also imposing expectations on individuals. The task is to preserve room for disagreement and self-development. Critiquing fixed categories does not supply a complete substitute for remedies or show that every recognition policy has the same effects.

Karen E. Fields and Barbara J. Fields: racism produces belief in race

Work and approach. Racecraft: The Soul of Inequality in American Life (2012) develops a historical and conceptual account of how racist practices generate seemingly natural racial explanations. In their explanation of race, racism, and racecraft, the Fieldses distinguish human variation from doctrines of inherently different, ranked populations. They define racism through ancestry-based double standards in social practice, not simply through hostile feelings.

Mechanism. Racecraft turns something people do into an apparent property of those to whom it is done. Saying that people were segregated “because of their race” can hide the decisions and institutions that imposed segregation. The comparison with witchcraft concerns how collective practices sustain an explanatory belief; it does not deny the material reality of racist harm.

Contribution and limit. For the paradox, the warning is to explain discriminatory practices rather than make “race” itself the cause of an outcome. A corrective project can reproduce the confusion if it treats inherited categories as natural causal agents. The practical challenge is to trace those practices while retaining ways to measure whom they target. Rejecting racial naturalism does not make unequal treatment disappear.

Retiring: insights and limits

  • Question the authority of a label. A statistical category is not automatically a cohesive group; a public representative is not automatically its collective voice.
  • Specify what would be retired. Racial preferences, official records, statistical measures, personal identities, and political organizations are different objects. Ending one use does not require ending every other use.
  • Preserve detection. Removing racial data can make discriminatory patterns harder to identify. Measurement can distinguish self-identification, perceived race, ancestry, and treatment rather than assuming they coincide.
  • Account for persistence. Other people may continue to impose a racial identity after an individual or government stops using the label.
  • Explain the transition. Specify replacement measures, equal-treatment protections, and checks for unintended consequences. A hope that boundaries will weaken needs an account of how institutional change would produce that result.

The family's strongest contribution is to make the reproduction of categories an outcome to investigate. Its unresolved practical question is how to reduce categorical authority while preserving the capacity to recognize and remedy harm directed at people through those categories.

Investigating racism: beliefs, treatment, and accumulated inequality

Contemporary discussions often focus on inequality, discrimination, and injustice. To investigate these problems, sociologists distinguish what people believe, how they evaluate others, what they do, and how institutions operate.

Three related terms should remain distinct. A stereotype is an oversimplified set of beliefs about a group. Prejudice is a negative belief, attitude, or judgment about individuals or groups. Discrimination is behavior or action that treats individuals or groups unfairly. They often reinforce one another, but a stereotype is not itself an action, and an action can have discriminatory effects even when the actor denies prejudiced intent.

Racism at interpersonal, structural, and cultural levels

Racism is not limited to openly hostile individuals. It can operate through cultural meanings, interpersonal actions, and institutions. Cultural racism installs ideas of inferiority through language, imagery, symbols, stereotypes, unconscious assumptions, and the internalization of racial beliefs. Interpersonal discrimination is an intentional action by an individual or small group that unfairly harms members of a racial or ethnic group. Institutional or structural racism occurs when laws, policies, organizational routines, or social arrangements systematically advantage some racial groups and disadvantage others, even when no single person openly expresses racist intent.

Slavery and Jim Crow are clear examples of institutionalized racial domination. Contemporary structural racism is often less visible. Housing segregation, redlining, unequal investment, racial profiling, and zoning practices can reproduce unequal outcomes through many ordinary decisions and institutional routines. In this sense, racism can persist even when explicitly discriminatory rules are formally prohibited and even when the individuals participating in a system do not understand themselves as racists.

Intent, process, and outcome should also be separated. A policy can produce unequal outcomes because it treats similar cases differently, because a formally uniform rule interacts with historically unequal conditions, or because relevant needs genuinely differ. A disparity is evidence requiring explanation, not by itself a complete proof of discrimination. Researchers compare similarly situated cases, trace decision rules, conduct audit experiments, study historical pathways, and test alternative mechanisms.

How housing inequality accumulates

Federal underwriting and local practices once restricted mortgage access in many Black neighborhoods. Segregation then shaped property appreciation, school tax bases, exposure to hazards, commute time, and family wealth. Later formally race-neutral decisions occur on that inherited landscape: buyers use accumulated down payments, lenders evaluate wealth and credit histories, and municipalities defend zoning arrangements. No present actor must have designed the whole outcome for earlier rules and current incentives to reproduce it. At the same time, identifying this mechanism requires evidence about policies, markets, preferences, income, and neighborhood change rather than assuming that every residential difference has one cause.

Current evidence: wealth as an accumulated outcome

The Federal Reserve’s 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances illustrates both large group disparities and important within-group variation. Median family wealth was approximately $285,000 among White families, $44,900 among Black families, $61,600 among Hispanic families, and $536,000 among Asian families. The Asian estimate became separately reportable because of improved oversampling, while the remaining combined category contained several very different populations. These figures do not identify one cause, and group medians do not describe every family. They direct analysis toward inheritance, age, earnings, homeownership and appreciation, debt, family structure, migration selectivity, business ownership, discrimination, and historical access to assets.

Du Bois: double consciousness, standpoint, and intersectionality

W. E. B. Du Bois treated race as a historical and institutional relationship rather than a natural hierarchy. His account of double consciousness described the experience of seeing oneself both through one’s own perspective and through the judgment of a dominant society. This “two-ness” can be painful, but it can also provide insight into how social power works. Du Bois connected racial identity to labor, citizenship, education, migration, urban life, and political power.

Later standpoint theory develops a related insight: knowledge is shaped by social location, and people positioned at the margins may notice forms of power that are invisible from the center. Standpoint theory does not mean that every member of a group has identical knowledge or that personal experience automatically proves a claim. It means that biography and position are relevant to what people can see, what questions they ask, and whose accounts have historically been treated as authoritative.

Later scholars developed this insight into intersectionality, especially Kimberlé Crenshaw’s analysis of how race, gender, class, and other statuses can operate together. Intersectionality argues that a person cannot always be understood by examining one category at a time. For example, the experiences of Black women may not be captured by a theory focused only on racism or only on sexism. The framework asks how institutions and identities combine to create distinctive patterns of advantage and disadvantage.

From this perspective, racial inequality is reproduced at multiple levels: cultural narratives shape expectations; interpersonal encounters communicate stereotypes and prejudice; and institutions distribute safety, housing, education, employment, and political voice unevenly. Vigilant coping—the effort to anticipate discrimination by continuously monitoring and modifying one’s behavior or surroundings—illustrates how structural and cultural racism can become an everyday psychological burden. Du Bois’s double consciousness remains useful here because it connects inner experience to a social world that is looking back.

These examples bring the three families back to concrete mechanisms. Defending asks which barriers persist; auditing asks whether remedies reach the people affected; retiring asks whether the remedy strengthens unnecessary boundaries. A useful investigation can ask all three questions.

The tension and the middle ground

The choice need not be between every possible use of racial categories and complete abandonment of them. Outreach can broaden an applicant pool while keeping category membership out of the final selection rule. Programs based on economic need or neighborhood disadvantage can address barriers within as well as across racial groups. These approaches may still miss unequal treatment or barriers that do not closely track income or place.

Targeted universalism, associated with john a. powell and colleagues, sets a goal for everyone and develops different strategies to address the barriers different populations face in reaching it. A hypothetical school system might aim for every student to read proficiently, then provide language support, disability accommodations, or additional instruction according to the obstacles identified. The shared goal does not require identical assistance.

Each proposal needs evaluation. Do benefits reach the people facing the greatest barriers? Does the design address the mechanism producing inequality? Does it create incentives to police or strategically claim group membership? What would justify continuing, revising, or ending it? Measuring outcomes by race and allocating assistance by need can coexist, but their combination must be assessed rather than assumed to solve every problem.

Comparative applications: recognition, boundaries, and rights

The following cases extend the paradox to ethnic recognition and religious accommodation. They illustrate related questions about classification and institutional power; ethnicity, religion, and race remain distinct concepts. In each case, ask what problem the policy seeks to remedy, how it uses categories, who benefits, and which boundaries become more or less consequential.

Case study: when state recognition hardens ethnic boundaries

The Soviet Union. Beginning in the 1920s, the Soviet policy of korenizatsiia, or indigenization, promoted non-Russian languages, trained local officials and intellectuals, and organized union republics and autonomous territories around designated nationalities. Historian Terry Martin calls this an “affirmative action empire” because the state deliberately sought to correct Russian dominance and accelerate the development of minority elites. The policy expanded literacy, administration, and cultural production in many languages; it was not simply a scheme to provoke conflict.

Yet the same system also converted fluid identities into official categories, linked particular peoples to bounded territories, and elevated titular elites over minorities living inside those territories. Moscow later reversed many early protections through centralization, Russification, purges, and mass deportations. When central authority weakened in the late 1980s, republican institutions, official histories, territorial borders, and established elites supplied ready-made channels for ethnonational mobilization. Conflicts such as Nagorno-Karabakh cannot be attributed to affirmative action alone: coercive rule, contested borders, demographic change, historical violence, elite strategy, and state collapse all mattered. The lesson is narrower. A policy designed to recognize and develop groups can also make group boundaries more politically consequential.

China. The People’s Republic classified 56 official minzu, established autonomous jurisdictions, and granted minority populations various preferences, including preparatory programs and bonus points in the gaokao university examination. These policies sought to widen access for populations facing geographic, linguistic, and educational disadvantages. Studies have found genuine gains in college access, and some research disputes the claim that preferences generally produced academic failure or widespread interethnic hostility.

Other evidence identifies tradeoffs. James Leibold’s study of reactions to the 2009 Chongqing scandal—in which students were found to have falsified minority identity to obtain examination advantages—shows how benefits based on fixed ethnic categories can foster Han resentment and strategic identity claims. A 2026 study of the bonus-point system found that stronger preferences increased admission to colleges and elite institutions but were also associated with lower within-college rank, movement toward lower-earning majors, and lower college satisfaction among recipients. These findings concern policy design and average outcomes, not the worth or ability of minority students.

China’s ethnic tensions likewise cannot be reduced to preferential admissions. Regional inequality, Han migration, language policy, restrictions on religion and dissent, separatist violence, policing, and coercive assimilation are much larger parts of the history of Xinjiang, Tibet, and Inner Mongolia. Preferential policy can simultaneously improve access, create dependency or mismatch in some settings, and generate majority resentment. A more targeted design might consider socioeconomic and geographic disadvantage within ethnic groups, while continuing to protect language, equal citizenship, and freedom from discrimination.

Case study: Muslim integration, one civil law, and pluralism in Europe

European debates frequently join immigration, Islam, national identity, gender equality, public security, and secularism into one emotionally charged issue. The categories require care. Europe’s Muslims differ by national origin, class, religiosity, generation, and political outlook; many are citizens born in Europe rather than immigrants. Non-Muslim residents likewise range from pluralists to assimilationists and from principled secularists to anti-Muslim nationalists.

Sharia councils in England and Wales provide a concrete case. These councils do not have jurisdiction over British law, and their decisions are not legally binding. A 2018 independent government review found that nearly all users were women and that more than 90% were seeking an Islamic divorce, often because a religious marriage had never been civilly registered. The panel found both good practice and discriminatory practice. Supporters argue that voluntary religious mediation can help believers end a marriage recognized by their community. Critics worry that social pressure, unequal bargaining power, or lack of civil registration can leave women without the protections of family law.

The disagreement is therefore not accurately described as “British law versus an approaching Muslim legal takeover.” It concerns how a liberal state protects religious association while ensuring that one civil law, individual consent, gender equality, and access to courts remain supreme. Similar European disputes over religious clothing, school curricula, gender separation, speech, and accommodation ask where private religious practice ends and public civic obligation begins. Some Islamist demands and practices genuinely conflict with liberal rights; identifying such conflicts is not inherently prejudiced. Treating all Muslims as responsible for them, however, substitutes collective suspicion for evidence.

Exclusion is part of the same integration problem. The EU Agency for Fundamental Rights reported in 2024 that 47% of Muslim respondents in thirteen EU countries had experienced racial discrimination during the preceding five years, up from 39% in the comparable 2016 survey; job and housing discrimination were especially common. Discrimination can weaken trust and belonging, while segregated networks and rejection of shared civic rules can weaken contact and reciprocity. Durable integration therefore asks responsibilities of both institutions and individuals: enforce a common rights-protecting law, oppose anti-Muslim discrimination, require peaceful democratic conduct, support language and economic participation, and allow religious difference that does not violate others’ rights.

Sources for the comparative cases: See Terry Martin’s The Affirmative Action Empire; James Leibold’s study of Han reactions to minority education preferences; a 2026 study of China’s college bonus-point policy; the UK government’s independent review of Sharia councils; and the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights report Being Muslim in the EU.

Comparative takeaway

Recognition and accommodation can correct exclusion, but they may also harden categories or empower internal gatekeepers. Universal rules can protect equal citizenship, but they may also conceal unequal starting points or become a language for majority domination. Good policy asks who benefits, who bears costs, whether individual rights remain enforceable, and whether group boundaries become easier or harder to cross.

Additional sociological explanations

Theories of race and ethnicity disagree less about whether categories matter than about the mechanisms that make them consequential. A strong explanation specifies actors, institutions, incentives, meanings, and historical sequences rather than attributing every pattern either to prejudice or to culture.

Functionalism and assimilation theory

Functionalists examine how common institutions, communication, and norms support coordination in diverse societies. Classical assimilation theory expected differences associated with immigration to decline across generations as people entered shared schools, labor markets, neighborhoods, and civic institutions. Later segmented assimilation theory argues that incorporation follows multiple paths depending on reception, discrimination, family resources, community networks, and the structure of the economy.

The perspective highlights integration and shared membership, but it can treat dominant norms as neutral and underestimate coercion, unequal power, or the persistence of racialized boundaries after cultural assimilation. The relevant empirical question is which differences fade, which persist, and under what institutional conditions.

Conflict theory, split labor markets, and group threat

Conflict approaches examine competition over labor, land, political authority, and social status. Edna Bonacich’s split labor market theory explains how employers, higher-paid workers, and lower-paid workers can have different interests: employers may seek cheaper labor, while established workers may support exclusion to reduce competition. Racial boundaries can divide workers who might otherwise share economic interests.

Group-threat theories propose that prejudice and exclusion can intensify when a dominant group perceives another group as challenging jobs, political influence, safety, or cultural authority. Competition alone does not mechanically produce hostility; leaders, media, organizations, and histories frame which population is blamed and whether cooperation or exclusion seems legitimate.

Symbolic interactionism, labeling, and boundary work

Interactionists study how people learn categories, read bodies and names, manage identities, and negotiate ambiguous encounters. Repeated expectations can create stereotype threat, vigilant coping, or self-fulfilling prophecies. Everyday encounters also reproduce institutions when gatekeepers repeatedly interpret the same conduct differently across groups.

This perspective does not imply that race exists only in face-to-face perception. Institutional categories enter interaction through forms, records, professional routines, neighborhood reputations, and algorithms. Micro encounters enact macro histories, while changing interaction can slowly alter boundaries.

Social psychology: social identity and contact

Social identity theory explains how even minimal group distinctions can produce in-group favoritism when belonging supplies meaning and esteem. Gordon Allport’s contact hypothesis offers a contrasting mechanism: interaction can reduce prejudice when participants have relatively equal status in the setting, cooperate toward common goals, receive institutional support, and have opportunities for meaningful acquaintance. Mere proximity can fail or worsen conflict when contact is competitive, unequal, involuntary, or segregated within the same institution.

Patterns of intergroup relations

Societies organize diversity through several ideal-typical patterns. Assimilation occurs when group distinctions diminish as members adopt institutions and practices of a larger society. It may be voluntary and reciprocal, or coercive and one-sided. Pluralism combines shared political institutions with continuing group differences. Amalgamation creates new populations and identities through intermarriage and cultural blending. Segregation maintains spatial or institutional separation; expulsion removes a population; and genocide attempts its physical destruction. Real histories can contain several patterns at once.

Assimilation is multidimensional

A family may learn English and enter mainstream schools and occupations while retaining religion, food, kinship practices, or a transnational identity. It may be structurally integrated yet encounter racial exclusion, or culturally distinct while enjoying equal citizenship. Asking whether a group “has assimilated” is therefore less useful than specifying language, networks, residence, marriage, economic mobility, identification, and acceptance.

Putting the three responses in dialogue

The defending perspective asks whether a proposed remedy addresses barriers that neutral rules leave in place. The auditing perspective asks who receives its benefits and what incentives sustain it. The retiring perspective asks whether the remedy increases dependence on the categories it ultimately seeks to make less consequential. Together these questions direct attention to both material outcomes and the political effects of classification.

Agreement on a goal does not settle these questions. Two people can oppose discrimination, accept the same evidence of unequal treatment, and disagree about which remedy is effective or justified. A sociological argument should identify the disagreement, compare mechanisms and evidence, and explain what would count against its preferred approach.

How to evaluate a claim about racism

Controversies often become unproductive because participants answer different questions. One person asks whether an actor intended harm; another asks whether a rule generates a disparate outcome; a third asks whether a historical institution created unequal starting conditions; and a fourth asks whether an alternative explanation fits the evidence better. Sociological inquiry separates these claims and investigates each.

  1. Define the outcome and comparison. Is the claim about attitudes, treatment, representation, income, wealth, health, punishment, or belonging? Which cases are meaningfully comparable?
  2. Specify the level. Is the proposed mechanism cultural, interpersonal, organizational, legal, spatial, or political?
  3. Establish timing. Did the proposed cause precede the outcome, and through which historical sequence could its effects persist?
  4. Test alternatives. Examine class, age, geography, family structure, selection, institutional rules, measurement, and other plausible processes without assuming that controlling for a variable makes its own history irrelevant.
  5. Look for converging evidence. Surveys, interviews, audit studies, administrative records, experiments, and historical documents reveal different parts of the process.
  6. Keep moral and empirical judgments distinct but connected. Evidence can show what a policy does; principles of equality, freedom, remedy, and social cohesion shape what people believe should be done.

Core idea: construction, constraint, and change

People create racial and ethnic meanings through classification and interaction. Institutions stabilize those meanings in boundaries, records, neighborhoods, and resource distributions. Those structures then constrain later action, but people can organize, intermarry, migrate, reinterpret identities, challenge rules, and build new coalitions. Race and ethnicity emerge from action and become conditions for action.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Race and ethnicity are socially constructed classifications with real consequences. Race emphasizes perceived physical traits and ancestry; ethnicity emphasizes cultural heritage and belonging.
  • Ethnic boundaries can change. Symbolic ethnicity is more optional for some people than identities imposed through exclusion.
  • Racial formation describes the historical making and remaking of racial categories. Our working definition of racial projects links categorization by perceived biological traits, assigned meanings and explanations, and the organization of resources along those distinctions.
  • A project’s purpose and its method are separate questions. Corrective intentions require evaluation of actual effects.
  • Framings of the “race problem” have shifted from treating populations as threats or inferiors toward examining inequality, discrimination, and injustice. Du Bois helped develop this empirical alternative.
  • Stereotypes are beliefs, prejudice concerns attitudes and judgments, and discrimination concerns treatment. Racism can operate through interpersonal conduct, institutions, and cultural meanings.
  • The paradox of progressive racial projects is that efforts to reduce racial inequality can also reproduce or reify racial categories. This is an outcome to investigate, rather than an assumption that every remedy fails.
  • The defending perspective emphasizes continuing barriers; the auditing perspective examines beneficiaries and incentives; the retiring perspective questions the continuing authority of categories.
  • Descriptive detection uses categories to investigate conditions or treatment. Prescriptive engineering uses them to govern allocation. Supporting one does not automatically require supporting the other.
  • Outreach, assistance based on economic need or place, and targeted universalism offer different designs whose benefits and limitations require evidence.
  • Comparative cases show how recognition can widen access while making boundaries more politically consequential. Universal rules also require scrutiny of their effects.
