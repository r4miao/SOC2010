Race, Ethnicity, and Racism

What these categories mean, how they acquire social power, and why sociologists debate how to interpret them.

Race and ethnicity are not merely personal identities. They are social classifications whose meanings and consequences are produced through history, institutions, culture, and interaction. This chapter first defines the concepts in the same sequence as the Week 9 lecture, then places major arguments about race and ethnicity in conversation with one another.

Imagine siblings whose ancestry is identical but who are classified differently because observers read their skin tone differently, or a person counted as White under one census rule and Middle Eastern or North African under another. Their genes did not change when the form changed. Yet the category can affect political representation, civil-rights enforcement, research, identity, and how others interpret them. The sociological task is to explain both halves of this fact: racial and ethnic boundaries are historically made, and classifications that are made can still have durable material and psychological effects.

What are race and ethnicity?

Race is a category based on beliefs about shared ancestry and perceived biological or physical traits, especially visible traits such as skin color. Sociologically, race is a social construction: what matters is not only what a trait is, but what people and institutions perceive it to mean. Racial categories are not fixed biological divisions, but racial classifications are real in their effects because they shape treatment, opportunity, belonging, and exclusion.

Calling race socially constructed does not deny human biological variation. Populations can differ statistically in the frequency of some inherited traits, and ancestry can be estimated probabilistically. The sociological point is that variation is overlapping and often gradual rather than naturally divided into the few bounded, hierarchical races societies have named. Institutions select certain visible differences, group them, attach ancestry stories and moral meanings, and use them to allocate membership and resources. Racialization is the process through which a population, practice, religion, or nationality comes to be interpreted through racial meanings.

Ethnicity refers to a shared cultural heritage or origin. It may involve language, religion, national origin, history, traditions, food, family ties, and a sense of common identity. Unlike race, ethnicity emphasizes culture and belonging rather than presumed biological difference. In practice, however, race and ethnicity often overlap, and the same group may be classified differently across time and place.

Concept: social construction

The process through which people create, interpret, institutionalize, and change the meanings attached to categories, identities, and social realities. A socially constructed category is not imaginary; it can organize institutions and produce very material consequences.

Race has a history

Human beings have always categorized one another, but the modern idea of “race” is relatively recent. Earlier societies were more likely to classify people through religion, language, lineage, or geographic location. From the era of European exploration onward, racial classifications such as “Caucasoid,” “Mongoloid,” “Negroid,” and “Australoid” were used to sort populations. These labels were never neutral: they often expressed a hierarchy of superiority and inferiority connected to political power, economic resources, and technological dominance. Racial differentiation therefore became a form of racial stratification.

The history of the U.S. census makes the constructed character of race visible. Census categories have changed repeatedly: “white,” “slave,” “free colored,” “black,” “mulatto,” “Mexican,” “Chinese,” “Japanese,” “Hindu,” “Korean,” “Filipino,” “American Indian,” and other categories have been added, removed, renamed, or redefined. The 2020 census explains that its racial categories reflect a social definition of race, not an attempt to define race biologically, anthropologically, or genetically. People may also report more than one race, and Hispanic or Latino origin may be reported by people of any race.

Federal standards changed again in 2024. The Office of Management and Budget replaced the older separate race and Hispanic-origin questions with a combined race-and-ethnicity format that permits multiple selections and added Middle Eastern or North African (MENA) as a minimum category. The Census Bureau plans to use the combined format in the American Community Survey and the 2030 Census. This change will improve recognition for some respondents but also complicates comparisons with older data. Classification systems do not merely describe populations; they shape which populations become statistically visible.

Why categories matter

If a government changes who counts as “white,” “Mexican,” “Asian,” or “American Indian,” it is not merely changing vocabulary. It is changing how people are counted, represented, governed, and sometimes granted or denied rights. Categories are made by institutions, but they can also be challenged and remade by social movements.

Michael Omi and Howard Winant: racial formation

Omi and Winant define racial formation as the sociohistorical process through which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed. A racial project connects a representation of racial difference with an effort to organize resources or institutions. Census rules, immigration law, school boundaries, popular entertainment, and civil-rights movements can all participate in racial formation, although they do so with very different purposes and power.

The framework links structure and agency. People inherit classifications and unequal institutions, but they also contest names, build identities, reinterpret history, and change policy. Race is neither a timeless essence nor a label that individuals can alter without constraint.

Ethnic boundaries, symbolic ethnicity, and group position

Ethnic traditions matter, but groups are not simply containers of ancient cultural traits. Fredrik Barth emphasized the boundary that distinguishes members from nonmembers. Language, dress, religion, ancestry, or food may mark the boundary, and the markers can change while a sense of group continuity remains. States, migration, intermarriage, schools, and political organizations can harden, blur, or redraw such boundaries.

Herbert Gans: symbolic ethnicity

Gans used symbolic ethnicity to describe a voluntary, intermittent identification with an ancestral heritage among later-generation descendants of European immigrants in the United States. A person might celebrate a holiday, cook a family dish, or claim an ancestry without organizing everyday work, residence, or marriage around the group. This relatively optional form contrasts with identities imposed by outsiders or backed by discrimination. Whether an identity can be switched off is therefore itself a form of social advantage.

A sociological minority group is not defined only by numerical size. It is a category whose members experience subordinate power or unequal treatment; a numerical majority may be politically subordinate, and a small group may be dominant. Racial and ethnic identities also intersect with class, religion, gender, nationality, color, and generation, producing substantial variation within every broad census category.

Patterns of intergroup relations

Societies organize diversity through several ideal-typical patterns. Assimilation occurs when group distinctions diminish as members adopt institutions and practices of a larger society. It may be voluntary and reciprocal, or coercive and one-sided. Pluralism combines shared political institutions with continuing group differences. Amalgamation creates new populations and identities through intermarriage and cultural blending. Segregation maintains spatial or institutional separation; expulsion removes a population; and genocide attempts its physical destruction. Real histories can contain several patterns at once.

Assimilation is multidimensional

A family may learn English and enter mainstream schools and occupations while retaining religion, food, kinship practices, or a transnational identity. It may be structurally integrated yet encounter racial exclusion, or culturally distinct while enjoying equal citizenship. Asking whether a group “has assimilated” is therefore less useful than specifying language, networks, residence, marriage, economic mobility, identification, and acceptance.

The problem of racism

Racism is not limited to openly hostile individuals. It can operate through cultural meanings, interpersonal actions, and institutions. Cultural racism installs ideas of inferiority through language, imagery, symbols, stereotypes, unconscious assumptions, and the internalization of racial beliefs. Interpersonal discrimination is an intentional action by an individual or small group that unfairly harms members of a racial or ethnic group. Institutional or structural racism occurs when laws, policies, organizational routines, or social arrangements systematically advantage some racial groups and disadvantage others, even when no single person openly expresses racist intent.

Slavery and Jim Crow are clear examples of institutionalized racial domination. Contemporary structural racism is often less visible. Housing segregation, redlining, unequal investment, racial profiling, and zoning practices can reproduce unequal outcomes through many ordinary decisions and institutional routines. In this sense, racism can persist even when explicitly discriminatory rules are formally prohibited and even when the individuals participating in a system do not understand themselves as racists.

Three related terms should remain distinct. A stereotype is an oversimplified set of beliefs about a group. Prejudice is a negative belief, attitude, or judgment about individuals or groups. Discrimination is behavior or action that treats individuals or groups unfairly. They often reinforce one another, but a stereotype is not itself an action, and an action can have discriminatory effects even when the actor denies prejudiced intent.

Intent, process, and outcome should also be separated. A policy can produce unequal outcomes because it treats similar cases differently, because a formally uniform rule interacts with historically unequal conditions, or because relevant needs genuinely differ. A disparity is evidence requiring explanation, not by itself a complete proof of discrimination. Researchers compare similarly situated cases, trace decision rules, conduct audit experiments, study historical pathways, and test alternative mechanisms.

How housing inequality accumulates

Federal underwriting and local practices once restricted mortgage access in many Black neighborhoods. Segregation then shaped property appreciation, school tax bases, exposure to hazards, commute time, and family wealth. Later formally race-neutral decisions occur on that inherited landscape: buyers use accumulated down payments, lenders evaluate wealth and credit histories, and municipalities defend zoning arrangements. No present actor must have designed the whole outcome for earlier rules and current incentives to reproduce it. At the same time, identifying this mechanism requires evidence about policies, markets, preferences, income, and neighborhood change rather than assuming that every residential difference has one cause.

Current evidence: wealth as an accumulated outcome

The Federal Reserve’s latest Survey of Consumer Finances, conducted in 2022, illustrates both large group disparities and important within-group variation. Median family wealth was approximately $285,000 among White families, $44,900 among Black families, $61,600 among Hispanic families, and $536,000 among Asian families. The Asian estimate became separately reportable because of improved oversampling, while the remaining combined category contained several very different populations. These figures do not identify one cause, and group medians do not describe every family. They direct analysis toward inheritance, age, earnings, homeownership and appreciation, debt, family structure, migration selectivity, business ownership, discrimination, and historical access to assets.

Major sociological explanations

Theories of race and ethnicity disagree less about whether categories matter than about the mechanisms that make them consequential. A strong explanation specifies actors, institutions, incentives, meanings, and historical sequences rather than attributing every pattern either to prejudice or to culture.

Functionalism and assimilation theory

Functionalists examine how common institutions, communication, and norms support coordination in diverse societies. Classical assimilation theory expected differences associated with immigration to decline across generations as people entered shared schools, labor markets, neighborhoods, and civic institutions. Later segmented assimilation theory argues that incorporation follows multiple paths depending on reception, discrimination, family resources, community networks, and the structure of the economy.

The perspective highlights integration and shared membership, but it can treat dominant norms as neutral and underestimate coercion, unequal power, or the persistence of racialized boundaries after cultural assimilation. The relevant empirical question is which differences fade, which persist, and under what institutional conditions.

Conflict theory, split labor markets, and group threat

Conflict approaches examine competition over labor, land, political authority, and social status. Edna Bonacich’s split labor market theory explains how employers, higher-paid workers, and lower-paid workers can have different interests: employers may seek cheaper labor, while established workers may support exclusion to reduce competition. Racial boundaries can divide workers who might otherwise share economic interests.

Group-threat theories propose that prejudice and exclusion can intensify when a dominant group perceives another group as challenging jobs, political influence, safety, or cultural authority. Competition alone does not mechanically produce hostility; leaders, media, organizations, and histories frame which population is blamed and whether cooperation or exclusion seems legitimate.

Symbolic interactionism, labeling, and boundary work

Interactionists study how people learn categories, read bodies and names, manage identities, and negotiate ambiguous encounters. Repeated expectations can create stereotype threat, vigilant coping, or self-fulfilling prophecies. Everyday encounters also reproduce institutions when gatekeepers repeatedly interpret the same conduct differently across groups.

This perspective does not imply that race exists only in face-to-face perception. Institutional categories enter interaction through forms, records, professional routines, neighborhood reputations, and algorithms. Micro encounters enact macro histories, while changing interaction can slowly alter boundaries.

Social psychology: social identity and contact

Social identity theory explains how even minimal group distinctions can produce in-group favoritism when belonging supplies meaning and esteem. Gordon Allport’s contact hypothesis offers a contrasting mechanism: interaction can reduce prejudice when participants have relatively equal status in the setting, cooperate toward common goals, receive institutional support, and have opportunities for meaningful acquaintance. Mere proximity can fail or worsen conflict when contact is competitive, unequal, involuntary, or segregated within the same institution.

Two debating perspectives on race and ethnicity

The following perspectives do not exhaust sociological scholarship, and they should not be confused with a simple political left-versus-right binary. They ask different questions about evidence, power, identity, and social cohesion. The first begins from the standpoint of people who experience racialized inequality and traces how overlapping systems of power shape their lives. The second challenges parts of that tradition and emphasizes universal standards, open inquiry, and the value of a shared civic culture.

Perspective 1: From Du Bois's standpoint to intersectionality

W. E. B. Du Bois treated race as a historical and institutional relationship rather than a natural hierarchy. His account of double consciousness described the experience of seeing oneself both through one’s own perspective and through the judgment of a dominant society. This “two-ness” can be painful, but it can also provide insight into how social power works. Du Bois connected racial identity to labor, citizenship, education, migration, urban life, and political power.

This approach is a form of standpoint theory: knowledge is shaped by social location, and people positioned at the margins may notice forms of power that are invisible from the center. Standpoint theory does not mean that every member of a group has identical knowledge or that personal experience automatically proves a claim. It means that biography and position are relevant to what people can see, what questions they ask, and whose accounts have historically been treated as authoritative.

Later scholars developed this insight into intersectionality, especially Kimberlé Crenshaw’s analysis of how race, gender, class, and other statuses can operate together. Intersectionality argues that a person cannot always be understood by examining one category at a time. For example, the experiences of Black women may not be captured by a theory focused only on racism or only on sexism. The framework asks how institutions and identities combine to create distinctive patterns of advantage and disadvantage.

From this perspective, racial inequality is reproduced at multiple levels: cultural narratives shape expectations; interpersonal encounters communicate stereotypes and prejudice; and institutions distribute safety, housing, education, employment, and political voice unevenly. Vigilant coping—the effort to anticipate discrimination by continuously monitoring and modifying one’s behavior or surroundings—illustrates how structural and cultural racism can become an everyday psychological burden. Du Bois’s double consciousness remains useful here because it connects inner experience to a social world that is looking back.

Perspective 2: A critique from Cynical Theories to Out of the Melting Pot, Into the Fire

A contrasting perspective accepts that racism and discrimination exist but questions some of the methods, vocabulary, and conclusions associated with contemporary identity-centered scholarship. In Cynical Theories, Helen Pluckrose and James Lindsay argue that strands of postmodernism and critical theory developed into activist approaches that treat race, gender, and identity as central political categories. Their criticism is that these approaches can make claims about power difficult to challenge, elevate subjective experience over shared standards of evidence, divide people into fixed oppressor and oppressed groups, and encourage identity-based conflict rather than liberal equality.

From this viewpoint, standpoint and intersectionality can be useful prompts to investigate neglected experiences, but they become problematic when group identity is treated as a source of automatic epistemic authority or when disagreement is interpreted as evidence of hidden prejudice. The preferred alternative is universalism: judge people as individuals, protect equal rights, use evidence that others can examine, and preserve the possibility of reasoned disagreement. This is a critique of a body of scholarship and activism, not proof that structural racism is unreal.

Jens Heycke’s Out of the Melting Pot, Into the Fire extends the critique to multiculturalism and national identity. Heycke distinguishes between a society that welcomes cultural contributions while developing a common civic identity and a form of multiculturalism that treats separate group identities as permanent political boundaries. Drawing on historical comparisons, he argues that social cohesion becomes more difficult when a country lacks shared language, norms, institutions, or public allegiance. The argument favors assimilation into a common civic culture while allowing private and cultural difference to continue.

The critical question for this perspective is whether emphasizing race and ethnicity reduces prejudice or unintentionally hardens the very categories it seeks to overcome. Its critics respond that a “melting pot” can conceal unequal power and pressure minority groups to abandon meaningful identities. The debate therefore concerns both inequality and integration: how can a society recognize historical and continuing disadvantages without turning racial and ethnic categories into permanent divisions?

Putting the perspectives in dialogue

The first perspective begins with the question, Whose experience and knowledge have been excluded, and how do institutions reproduce unequal outcomes? The second begins with questions such as, What standards allow claims to be challenged, and what shared identity allows a diverse society to remain politically united? Both perspectives can study the same case—such as racial profiling, affirmative action, school segregation, or multicultural education—but they will emphasize different mechanisms and risks.

A sociological analysis should make those disagreements visible without collapsing them. It should examine evidence of unequal treatment and institutional outcomes, take lived experience seriously without treating it as infallible, and ask whether policies reduce inequality while preserving equal citizenship and open inquiry. Race and ethnicity are socially constructed, but that does not mean they are socially unimportant. They are categories made by people and institutions, and therefore categories that people and institutions can contest, reshape, or use for very different political purposes.

How to evaluate a claim about racism

Controversies often become unproductive because participants answer different questions. One person asks whether an actor intended harm; another asks whether a rule generates a disparate outcome; a third asks whether a historical institution created unequal starting conditions; and a fourth asks whether an alternative explanation fits the evidence better. Sociological inquiry separates these claims and investigates each.

  1. Define the outcome and comparison. Is the claim about attitudes, treatment, representation, income, wealth, health, punishment, or belonging? Which cases are meaningfully comparable?
  2. Specify the level. Is the proposed mechanism cultural, interpersonal, organizational, legal, spatial, or political?
  3. Establish timing. Did the proposed cause precede the outcome, and through which historical sequence could its effects persist?
  4. Test alternatives. Examine class, age, geography, family structure, selection, institutional rules, measurement, and other plausible processes without assuming that controlling for a variable makes its own history irrelevant.
  5. Look for converging evidence. Surveys, interviews, audit studies, administrative records, experiments, and historical documents reveal different parts of the process.
  6. Keep moral and empirical judgments distinct but connected. Evidence can show what a policy does; principles of equality, freedom, remedy, and social cohesion shape what people believe should be done.

Core idea: construction, constraint, and change

People create racial and ethnic meanings through classification and interaction. Institutions stabilize those meanings in boundaries, records, neighborhoods, and resource distributions. Those structures then constrain later action, but people can organize, intermarry, migrate, reinterpret identities, challenge rules, and build new coalitions. Race and ethnicity emerge from action and become conditions for action.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Race is a socially constructed category based on perceived traits and beliefs about ancestry; ethnicity emphasizes shared cultural heritage and origin.
  • Social construction does not make a category imaginary, and it does not deny overlapping human biological variation.
  • Racial formation and ethnic-boundary theories explain how categories emerge, become institutionalized, and change.
  • Race has changed across history and census systems, while racial classifications have continued to organize power and stratification.
  • Assimilation, pluralism, amalgamation, segregation, expulsion, and genocide describe different patterns of intergroup relations.
  • Racism can operate through cultural meanings, interpersonal discrimination, and institutional or structural arrangements.
  • Disparity, discriminatory process, and prejudiced intent are related but analytically distinct claims.
  • Functionalist, conflict, interactionist, social-identity, and contact theories identify different mechanisms of cohesion, exclusion, and change.
  • Du Bois’s double consciousness and standpoint theory connect lived experience to social knowledge and social structure.
  • Intersectionality examines how race, gender, class, and other statuses operate together rather than separately.
  • Cynical Theories and Out of the Melting Pot, Into the Fire challenge aspects of identity-centered scholarship and multiculturalism, emphasizing universal standards and common civic identity.

Questions for study

  1. How can race be socially constructed while racial classification remains consequential?
  2. Why does the changing history of census categories support the sociological claim that race is socially constructed?
  3. How do racial formation and symbolic ethnicity describe different processes?
  4. Why is assimilation not a single yes-or-no outcome?
  5. How would conflict, interactionist, social-identity, and contact theories explain prejudice differently?
  6. How can institutional inequality persist without a centralized planner?
  7. How does Du Bois’s double consciousness connect personal experience to cultural and institutional racism?
  8. What does intersectionality add to an analysis that studies race, gender, or class separately?
  9. What are the strongest criticisms made by Cynical Theories of standpoint and intersectionality?
  10. How does the melting-pot argument differ from multiculturalism, and what are the possible benefits and risks of each?
  11. What evidence is needed to distinguish a disparity from a discriminatory mechanism?
  12. Can a society recognize racial and ethnic differences while also sustaining a shared civic identity? Explain using evidence.
