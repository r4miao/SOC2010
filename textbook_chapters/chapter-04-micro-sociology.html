Social Construction

How people create meaning, identity, and social reality through everyday interaction.

Chapter 3 explained how institutions and structures organize social life. This chapter asks how those structures become meaningful, habitual, and taken for granted—and how individuals, through interaction and repeated practices, participate in forming, reproducing, negotiating, and transforming them.

Imagine entering a room where everyone suddenly becomes silent. The silence is physical, but it does not tell you what is happening. Is this an exam, a memorial, a conflict, a performance, a prank, or a response to your arrival? Before you can act intelligently, you must interpret the situation, infer what others expect, and present yourself in a way they can recognize. Other people are simultaneously interpreting you. The encounter proceeds only if participants establish enough shared meaning to coordinate their actions.

Microsociology begins with such moments, but it does not end with them. Definitions of situations shape identities, labels, relationships, and opportunities. Repeated definitions become routines; routines become attached to roles and institutions; institutions then influence later encounters. The chapter therefore follows a cumulative path from meaning and selfhood to performance, recognition, inequality, and the recursive relationship between interaction and structure.

Berger and Luckmann: from interaction to social reality

To understand social construction, begin with the relationship between human action and the social structures that confront people as an already existing world. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann explain how ordinary activity can become institutionalized and how institutions can later be experienced as natural, objective, and unquestionable.

Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann: the social construction of reality

In The Social Construction of Reality (1966), Berger and Luckmann argued that people create social arrangements through interaction, but those arrangements can eventually appear objective, natural, and independent of the people who produced them.

The process can be understood through three connected moments. Through externalization, people express meanings and create recurring practices. Through objectivation, those practices become established as institutions, rules, categories, and “facts” that confront later individuals as an already existing world. Through internalization, people learn those arrangements through socialization and experience them as common sense.

This is not a simple one-way sequence. People inherit an objective social world, interpret it, and reproduce or revise it through action. A routine begins in interaction, becomes stabilized through repetition and institutionalization, and is passed on through language and socialization. Because people created the arrangement, it can also be revised—although revision is difficult when the arrangement is supported by institutions, authority, and shared taken-for-granted beliefs.

Concept: social construction of reality

The process through which people create shared meanings and recurring practices that become institutionalized, treated as objective reality, and learned by later members of society.

People define situations before they act in them. When participants share a definition, interaction feels routine. When their definitions conflict, a crisis situation can emerge. The classic Thomas theorem expresses the sociological insight: situations defined as real become real in their consequences.

Symbolic interaction and the dynamics of construction

W. I. Thomas and Robert K. Merton: definitions can create consequences

William Isaac Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas formulated the insight now called the Thomas theorem: definitions of situations become consequential because people act on them. A false rumor of a bank’s insolvency can prompt depositors to withdraw funds, producing the very collapse they feared. The belief is not made factually correct by being believed; action organized around the belief changes the situation.

Robert K. Merton developed this mechanism as the self-fulfilling prophecy. An initially false definition evokes behavior that makes the expected outcome more likely, and the resulting outcome is then treated as proof that the original belief was correct. The mechanism connects micro interpretation to institutional inequality when expectations guide policing, lending, hiring, tracking, diagnosis, or discipline.

What social construction does—and does not—claim

To call something socially constructed is not to call it imaginary, freely chosen, or easy to change. Money is socially constructed, yet a person without it faces material consequences. National borders are historically created, yet states enforce them with law and force. Medical categories depend on classification, yet illness can involve biological processes that do not disappear when terminology changes. Construction concerns the production and stabilization of meaning, categories, and institutions; it does not deny an external or material world.

Nor is construction a solo game. A person cannot ordinarily redefine a bank robbery as a friendly request, a failing grade as an honors credential, or a courtroom as a private conversation simply by wishing it so. Definitions require uptake from other participants and often institutional authorization. The ability to impose a definition is therefore a form of power. Judges, physicians, teachers, journalists, platform designers, employers, and state agencies possess unequal authority to classify situations and make their classifications consequential.

This point creates an important debate. Strong constructionist accounts emphasize how categories and realities arise through discourse and practice. Realist critics ask whether constructionism sometimes underestimates biological constraints, material scarcity, or causal processes that exist independently of interpretation. A productive sociology distinguishes the material process, the categories used to identify it, the institutional response organized around those categories, and the feedback through which the response changes later behavior.

Social construction is the process through which people create, negotiate, and stabilize meanings, identities, categories, and social realities. It is especially visible in small-scale social life: face-to-face encounters, conversations, gestures, identities, groups, and the meanings people use to coordinate action. This perspective does not claim that reality is imaginary or that material conditions do not matter. It asks how meanings become shared, institutionalized, and consequential. Microsociology provides one important route for studying these processes because institutions are enacted in everyday encounters, and repeated encounters can reproduce or change institutions.

Symbolic interactionism is the central perspective in this chapter. It views society as an ongoing accomplishment of interaction among people who use symbols and interpret one another. The perspective rests on three tenets:

  1. People act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them.
  2. Those meanings arise through interaction with other people.
  3. People interpret and revise meanings in particular contexts rather than responding automatically to stimuli.

George Herbert Mead and Herbert Blumer: the self as an interaction process

George Herbert Mead supplied the philosophical foundation for symbolic interactionism. He argued that mind and self develop through communication and role-taking—imagining a situation from another person’s standpoint. In childhood, people first take the roles of particular others and later learn the generalized other: the broader expectations of a community that allow them to evaluate their own conduct.

Mead distinguished the “I”, the spontaneous and not-yet-predicted response, from the “me,” the organized attitudes of others that a person has internalized. The self is the continuing relationship between these moments. Social expectations shape conduct, but the “I” ensures that action is never a mechanical copy of the past. Interaction can therefore reproduce convention while also introducing novelty.

Herbert Blumer coined the term symbolic interactionism and formulated its three central premises: action follows meaning, meaning arises through interaction, and meaning is interpreted in context. He opposed explanations that treated actors as passive products of structures or stimuli. People define one another’s actions and construct a joint course of conduct, although they do so with unequal resources and inside institutions inherited from prior interaction.

Concept: social construction

The process through which people create shared meanings and recurring practices that become socially consequential, institutionalized, and experienced as reality.

Concept: symbolic interactionism

A microsociological perspective that explains social life through symbols, meanings, interpretation, identity, and interaction.

Meaning and symbols

A symbol may be an object, word, gesture, person, or behavior to which people attach a shared meaning. Symbols allow people to communicate about things that are not physically present. A handshake, uniform, emoji, wedding ring, academic hood, or raised hand can communicate identity and expectations.

Concept: symbol

An object, word, gesture, person, or behavior to which people attach a shared meaning. Symbols allow people to communicate about things that are not physically present.

The same gesture can mean different things in different settings. Meaning is therefore not contained in an object alone; it is produced through social interpretation. A gesture of respect in one setting may be rude in another. A uniform can signal authority to one audience and oppression to another.

Charles Horton Cooley: the looking-glass self

Charles Horton Cooley argued that the self develops through an imagined social mirror. We imagine how we appear to others, imagine their judgment of that appearance, and develop feelings about ourselves in response. The self is therefore an interactive product rather than an isolated possession.

The looking-glass self has three movements: imagining our appearance to another person, imagining that person’s evaluation, and developing a feeling such as pride, shame, confidence, or anxiety. The process is interpretive; people may be mistaken about what others think, but those interpretations can still shape conduct.

The concept helps explain stereotyping, discrimination, labeling, social conformity, peer influence, stigma, self-esteem, and consumption. People may buy clothing, technology, or other goods partly because those objects help them present a desirable self to an imagined audience.

Example: the slow elevator

Tenants complain that an old elevator is slow and threaten to leave. The manager could replace the elevator at great cost, or redefine the situation by making the wait visible and meaningful—for example, by displaying art, information, or a small reading space. The material problem remains, but changing the definition of the situation can change how people experience it.

The slow-elevator example illustrates a broader principle: changing the definition of a situation can change the practical consequences of that situation. A manager might redefine the elevator space as a gallery, a place for neighborhood information, or a feature of the building’s character. The physical elevator remains, but people’s expectations and feelings about waiting can change.

Example: Joshua Bell and the social organization of recognition

In a 2007 Washington Post demonstration, concert violinist Joshua Bell played incognito during morning rush hour in a Washington Metro station. Of 1,097 passersby, only seven stopped for more than a minute and 27 contributed money, totaling $32.17. The performance did not arrive with the concert hall, ticket price, program, formal clothing, seated audience, and advance reputation that normally define it as an important cultural event.

This was a journalistic demonstration rather than a controlled experiment, so it cannot prove that context alone caused every response; commuters faced time pressure and many may not have preferred classical music. Sociologically, its value is comparative. Bell’s skill and instrument remained largely constant while the setting, audience expectations, and status signals changed. The case anticipates Bourdieu’s point that value requires socially organized recognition.

Redefining spaces and products

Social construction can also be used deliberately. A space designed for one purpose may be redefined through new symbols, routines, and expectations. A vacant lot can become a community garden; a school hallway can become an exhibition space; a waiting room can become a place for information and social connection. Redefinition does not eliminate material constraints, but it can open new interpretations and uses.

Callout: redefining a product

A product may fail when consumers interpret it differently from the producer’s intention. A company marketing a minivan as a family vehicle may find that consumers associate it with an unwanted identity. Repositioning the vehicle as flexible transportation, adventure equipment, or a status-bearing lifestyle product changes the definition of the situation surrounding the same object.

Definitions of situations can also conflict. A person wearing a religious garment may define the clothing as an expression of faith or identity, while an observer may define it through stereotypes about foreignness, gender, or danger. The resulting interaction is a crisis situation because the participants do not share the same meaning. Microsociology asks how those competing definitions are negotiated and whose definition carries more institutional power.

Two additional microsociological approaches

Symbolic interactionism is the chapter’s central perspective, but it is not the only way to analyze small-scale social life. Ethnomethodology asks how people produce the appearance of an orderly and objective world through background practices. Social exchange theory asks how rewards, costs, dependence, and alternatives organize relationships. Both approaches examine interaction, yet they disagree about which mechanism deserves priority.

Harold Garfinkel: ethnomethodology and the hidden work of order

Harold Garfinkel developed ethnomethodology, the study of the practical methods ordinary people use to make situations intelligible and accountable. Conversation depends on background assumptions about turn-taking, relevance, sincerity, identity, and context that usually remain unnoticed. Social order is not merely imposed from above; participants continuously produce it by treating actions as recognizable instances of familiar activities.

Garfinkel’s breaching experiments deliberately disrupted taken-for-granted expectations—for example, asking students to behave as formal boarders in their own family homes. Confusion, anger, and repair efforts exposed the normally invisible assumptions organizing the encounter. The method shows that routine interaction requires active maintenance.

Ethnomethodology differs subtly from standard symbolic interactionism. Interactionists often ask how meanings and selves develop; ethnomethodologists ask how participants make any scene appear coherent enough to count as a classroom, joke, queue, interview, or family dinner in the first place.

George Homans and Peter Blau: social exchange and dependence

Social exchange theory analyzes relationships through valued rewards, costs, reciprocity, expectations, and available alternatives. George Homans emphasized how past rewards shape recurring interaction. Peter Blau connected exchange to emergent power: when one person controls a valued resource and the other lacks alternatives, dependence gives the resource-holder leverage.

The theory offers a contrast to meaning-centered explanations. A friendship can be understood through shared identity and interpretation, but also through support, obligation, trust, and reciprocity. Critics argue that exchange language can make affection and morality sound like market calculation. Defenders reply that rewards need not be monetary or consciously calculated; approval, belonging, information, care, and relief from obligation can all organize interaction. The empirical question is whether patterns of dependence explain the observed relationship better than meanings alone.

Dramaturgy and impression management

Erving Goffman: everyday life as performance

Erving Goffman analyzed social life through the metaphor of drama. In the front stage, people perform for an audience and use clothing, language, settings, and props—what Goffman called sign-vehicles—to communicate an identity. In the back stage, people can prepare, relax, or step outside the performance.

Impression management is the effort to control how others perceive us. It is not necessarily dishonest. Social interaction requires people to coordinate performances, repair mistakes, and cooperate as teams. The identities people perform can eventually become roles they experience as part of themselves.

Goffman also emphasized the importance of teamwork. A restaurant staff coordinates a shared performance for customers; a family may cooperate to maintain a public image; students may help one another sustain a presentation in class. Team members protect one another from disruptive information and repair errors when a performance falters.

Goffman’s analysis includes several related concepts:

  • Front stage: the setting in which a performance is given to an audience.
  • Back stage: a region where people can prepare for, discuss, or step outside a performance.
  • Role performance: the visible enactment of a social role.
  • Sign-vehicles: clothing, speech, facial expression, setting, props, and other signals used to communicate identity.
  • Teamwork: cooperation among performers who help maintain a shared definition of the situation.
  • Impression management: efforts to guide how others perceive and respond to us.

Social media makes these processes especially visible. A profile, selfie, professional biography, or public comment is a presentation to an imagined audience. The audience may be multiple audiences at once, which creates the challenge of managing different identities in one space. Repeated performances can also become internalized: people may gradually become the roles they repeatedly play.

Callout: front stage and back stage

A classroom presentation is front stage: the student uses prepared language, visual aids, posture, and clothing to communicate competence. The hallway conversation afterward may be a partial back stage, where the student and classmates discuss what happened, repair awkward moments, and prepare for the next performance.

Beyond presentation: emotional labor and doing gender

Arlie Russell Hochschild extended dramaturgical analysis by showing that organizations can regulate not only visible conduct but also feeling. Emotional labor is the management of emotion and emotional display as part of paid work. Flight attendants, nurses, servers, teachers, debt collectors, and customer-service workers may be expected to produce reassurance, enthusiasm, deference, calm, or controlled irritation. Workers can use surface acting, displaying an emotion they do not feel, or deep acting, attempting to generate the required feeling itself.

Emotional labor connects micro performance to organizational power. A smile may look like an individual gesture, but its timing, intensity, and audience can be specified by training, supervision, ratings, or platform metrics. The costs are also distributed unequally when gendered and racialized expectations make some workers more responsible for soothing others or tolerating mistreatment.

Candace West and Don Zimmerman similarly described gender as an ongoing accomplishment. Doing gender does not mean pretending to have a gender. It means organizing conduct in ways that are accountable to culturally available expectations of femininity and masculinity. Dress, speech, posture, occupations, household tasks, and emotional displays become evidence through which others classify and evaluate a person. Because accountability is continuous, even resistance occurs in relation to recognizable expectations.

The interactionist analysis now leads to a question about unequal recognition. People can manage impressions, but audiences do not evaluate every performance equally. Some styles, credentials, objects, and ways of speaking are treated as more legitimate or prestigious than others. The next concepts connect everyday presentation to social status and power.

Conspicuous consumption and status display

Thorstein Veblen: conspicuous consumption

Thorstein Veblen argued that people may consume goods not only for their practical use but also to display wealth, status, taste, or membership. Conspicuous consumption is the purchase or public display of goods partly to communicate social standing. Conspicuous leisure is the display of time, activities, or lifestyles that signal freedom from ordinary labor.

Veblen’s argument does not mean that every purchase is status-seeking. It means that consumption is also symbolic. A car, vacation, watch, phone, meal, or style of home can become a sign-vehicle through which people present a social identity to others.

Callout: the audience for consumption

A person may choose clothing for comfort, but also for the way it will be read in a job interview, classroom, neighborhood, or online profile. The same item can communicate professionalism in one setting, rebellion in another, and ordinary taste in a third. Its meaning depends on the audience and the social context.

Symbolic capital and unequal recognition

Veblen helps explain why people use consumption as a status display. Pierre Bourdieu extends the argument by asking why some displays receive more recognition than others. A performance is not valuable simply because an individual intends it to be impressive. Its value depends on the social setting and on the audience’s capacity to recognize it.

Pierre Bourdieu: symbolic capital, distinction, and habitus

Symbolic capital is the prestige, honor, legitimacy, or recognition attached to a person, object, credential, style, or practice. A university degree, professional title, accent, artistic taste, family name, or institutional affiliation can carry symbolic capital when an audience treats it as evidence of worth or authority.

Bourdieu also described cultural capital: valued knowledge, manners, language, credentials, tastes, and dispositions that can be converted into educational or occupational advantage. Distinction refers to the ways groups use taste and lifestyle to mark social boundaries. Habitus refers to deeply learned dispositions that make some ways of speaking, dressing, judging, or moving through institutions feel natural.

These concepts extend Goffman’s analysis. Goffman asks how a person manages a performance; Bourdieu asks why one performance is recognized as sophisticated, professional, authentic, or authoritative while another is dismissed. Symbolic capital is therefore social recognition that can be converted into real opportunities.

Callout: the same performance, different recognition

The same vocabulary may be judged as “professional” in one setting and “pretentious” in another. A student who already knows how to address professors, request recommendations, and discuss academic interests may appear naturally confident. Bourdieu’s analysis asks how family background and prior institutional experience helped produce that ease.

Labels, stigma, and self-fulfilling expectations

The social meaning of a performance can also become attached to the person who performs it. Labeling is the process through which people or institutions define someone as a particular kind of person—honors student, troublemaker, gifted child, addict, outsider, or leader. A label can organize how others respond and how the person interprets future situations.

Stigma is a discrediting mark that changes how a person is treated or imagined. Erving Goffman showed that stigma is relational: a trait becomes stigmatizing in a setting where it violates expectations about what a normal or acceptable person should be. People may conceal, manage, challenge, or reclaim a stigmatized identity.

A self-fulfilling prophecy occurs when an expectation helps produce the behavior or outcome it anticipates. If teachers interpret one student as capable and another as disruptive, their different treatment may contribute to different opportunities and performances. This does not mean expectations determine people completely; it means interaction can help create the reality it claims merely to observe.

Howard Becker and Edwin Lemert: labeling, deviance, and power

Howard Becker argued that deviance is not a quality residing entirely in an act. Groups create rules, apply them selectively, and label some rule-breakers as outsiders. The same conduct may be ignored, celebrated, medicalized, or punished depending on who acts, who is harmed, who observes it, and which institution has jurisdiction. Labeling theory therefore shifts attention from “Why did this person deviate?” to “How was this behavior classified, by whom, and with what consequences?”

Edwin Lemert distinguished primary deviance, an initial act that may have little effect on identity, from secondary deviance, conduct reorganized around a deviant identity after social reaction. Exclusion from conventional opportunities, surveillance, stigma, and association with similarly labeled people can make the label consequential.

Critics note that labeling theory can underexplain the initial behavior and may minimize serious harm by focusing on social reaction. A fuller account separates four questions: what produced the conduct, how authorities classified it, whether classification was applied consistently, and how the response altered later identity and opportunity. The theory is strongest as an explanation of social reaction and feedback, not as a claim that all conduct is created by labels.

Callout: from label to opportunity

A student labeled “not college material” may receive less encouragement, fewer challenging assignments, and less institutional support. The label can become part of the student’s looking-glass self, while the institution’s response changes the student’s actual opportunities. Microsociology connects the meaning of a label to its structural consequences.

Applying multiple levels: why does marriage dissolve?

A functionalist explanation asks whether economic and cultural changes have reduced or redistributed functions once concentrated in marriage and whether new arrangements perform those functions differently. A conflict explanation examines bargaining power, gender inequality, property, paid and unpaid labor, and changes that make exit more possible. An interactionist explanation studies how partners define love, fairness, commitment, parenthood, and divorce and how repeated encounters stabilize or erode a shared marital reality.

The explanations can complement one another, but they should not be collapsed. Changes in law or women’s earnings may alter bargaining and exit options; those structural changes may also reshape the meanings partners attach to marriage. Daily interpretations then feed back into institutions as enough couples normalize new expectations. A complete account specifies which mechanism changed first, how it reached households, and why effects differed across groups and historical periods.

Giddens: structuration and the recursive making of social life

The chapter now returns to the relationship between structure and action. If Berger and Luckmann show how interaction becomes an objective social world, Anthony Giddens explains how that world is continually reproduced and revised through practice.

Anthony Giddens: structuration theory

Giddens developed structuration theory to explain how agency and structure are connected. People draw on rules and resources when they act, and their repeated actions reproduce or alter the structures that organize social life. Structure is therefore both a medium of action and an outcome of action.

People are knowledgeable about much of what they do, even when they cannot fully explain every routine. They monitor situations, adjust their conduct, and sometimes challenge the rules they are using. At the same time, action always takes place within conditions that individuals did not create alone. This is why structure constrains and enables action simultaneously.

Giddens’s theory provides an echoing finish to this chapter and a bridge back to Chapter 3. Social construction is not merely the creation of private meanings. Through recurring practices, meanings become institutions; through institutions, they shape later action; and through action, they can be reproduced or transformed.

Putting the microsociological mechanisms together

The chapter’s approaches describe different moments in a connected process. Mead and Cooley explain how interaction forms the self. Blumer explains how actors interpret and coordinate meaning. Garfinkel reveals the tacit methods that make situations appear orderly. Goffman and Hochschild show how people manage performances and feelings for audiences and organizations. Veblen and Bourdieu explain how performances receive unequal status recognition. Becker and Lemert show how institutional labels feed back into identity and opportunity. Berger, Luckmann, and Giddens explain how these repeated practices become objective structures that organize the next encounter.

Social exchange theory supplies a contrasting mechanism: meanings matter, but dependence and alternatives can organize action even when participants interpret the relationship differently. A strong microsociological explanation therefore asks four questions together: What does the situation mean? How do participants produce its order? What valued resources and alternatives shape dependence? Which institutions authorize one definition and carry its consequences into the future?

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Social construction explains how meanings, identities, categories, and practices become shared and consequential.
  • Microsociology studies small-scale interaction without separating it from institutions and social structure.
  • Symbolic interactionism explains action through meaning, interaction, and interpretation.
  • Mead’s “I,” “me,” role-taking, and generalized other explain how the self emerges through interaction; Cooley’s looking-glass self connects identity to imagined judgments by others.
  • Berger and Luckmann explain how interaction becomes institutionalized, objectivated, and internalized as social reality.
  • Construction does not imply imagination or unlimited choice: material constraints, collective agreement, institutional authority, and power limit which definitions become consequential.
  • The Thomas theorem and self-fulfilling prophecy show how definitions can alter the conditions they claim merely to describe.
  • Ethnomethodology reveals the background practices through which participants produce recognizable social order; exchange theory emphasizes rewards, costs, alternatives, and dependence.
  • Goffman’s dramaturgical analysis explains front stage, back stage, role performance, sign-vehicles, teamwork, and impression management.
  • Hochschild’s emotional labor and West and Zimmerman’s doing gender connect everyday performance to organizations and cultural accountability.
  • Veblen explains how consumption and leisure can display status.
  • Bourdieu’s symbolic capital explains why some identities and performances receive more recognition than others.
  • Labels, stigma, and self-fulfilling prophecies show how interaction can shape identity and opportunity; labeling theory is strongest when paired with an explanation of the initial conduct.
  • Giddens’s structuration theory explains how recurring action reproduces or transforms social structure.

Questions for study

  1. What are the three tenets of symbolic interactionism?
  2. How do Mead’s “I” and “me” explain both social continuity and novelty?
  3. How can the looking-glass self contribute to stigma, conformity, or consumption?
  4. How do externalization, objectivation, and internalization explain the social construction of reality?
  5. Why does describing something as socially constructed not mean it is imaginary or freely chosen?
  6. How do ethnomethodology and social exchange theory differ from symbolic interactionism?
  7. What is the difference between front stage and back stage, and how do emotional-labor requirements make performance organizational?
  8. How does conspicuous consumption communicate social status?
  9. How does symbolic capital extend Goffman’s analysis of impression management?
  10. How can a label become a self-fulfilling prophecy, and what does labeling theory leave unexplained?
  11. How does structuration connect individual action to the reproduction and transformation of social structure?
