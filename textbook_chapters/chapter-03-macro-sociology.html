Social Structure

How statuses, roles, groups, institutions, rationalization, and bureaucracy organize social life—and how social arrangements change over time.

A person never acts in an empty space. Every decision occurs inside a social structure: a patterned arrangement of statuses, roles, institutions, groups, classes, networks, and cultural meanings.

Consider a student deciding whether to miss class when a work shift changes unexpectedly. The decision is personal, but the conflict was organized before the student chose: the university established an attendance schedule, the employer controls shifts, a household may depend on the wages, transportation limits when the student can travel, and cultural expectations define what a “responsible” student or worker should do. The student interprets these demands and may negotiate, comply, resist, or seek help. Whatever happens can then reinforce or modify the expectations surrounding the next student.

This chapter moves outward through that process. Statuses locate people in relationships; roles translate positions into expectations; groups connect actors to one another; institutions stabilize recurring practices; and bureaucracies coordinate action through formal rules. These arrangements make complex cooperation possible while distributing resources and burdens unevenly. They endure because people repeatedly enact them, yet that same dependence on action makes change possible.

Seeing social structure

Social structure refers to the broad and relatively durable organization of social life. Sociologists ask how institutions and large-scale patterns organize the everyday world. A social structure is not a building or a rigid machine. It is the relatively durable arrangement of relationships and expectations that gives social life its shape. It surrounds individuals with possibilities and limits, often before they make a conscious choice.

Concept: social structure

The organized pattern of statuses, roles, groups, institutions, resources, and meanings that shapes social interaction and distributes opportunities.

The word structure does not mean that people are powerless. People reproduce structures by following routines, but they can also challenge, negotiate, and transform them. The sociological task is to understand both sides: how structures influence action and how action maintains or changes structures.

Structure across levels of analysis

Social structure can be studied at different levels. Macrosociology examines institutions, stratification, economies, political systems, and historical transformations. Microsociology examines face-to-face interaction, meaning-making, and everyday conduct. These are connected levels of analysis: institutions are enacted by people, and repeated interactions can stabilize or alter institutions.

Structural functionalism and conflict theory are often used as macro-level perspectives. Symbolic interactionism is commonly associated with micro-level analysis, although all three perspectives can be used to connect everyday experience to larger patterns.

The building blocks of structure

Statuses: positions in a social structure

A status is a recognized social position that carries expectations, rights, and obligations. A person can be a daughter, professor, tenant, voter, employee, caregiver, immigrant, neighbor, or friend. Statuses locate people in relation to others: the status of student implies teachers, classmates, institutions, assignments, and rules.

An ascribed social status is assigned or acquired with little or no individual choice, such as age, family background, racial classification, or nationality at birth. An achieved social status is earned, chosen, or entered through action, such as student, nurse, parent, employee, or elected official. The distinction is analytical rather than absolute. Achieved statuses are shaped by ascribed locations and by unequal access to opportunity.

Concept: status set

The collection of all the statuses a person occupies at a particular time. A person’s status set may include student, employee, sibling, friend, renter, citizen, and caregiver.

Status inconsistency occurs when a person’s statuses carry unequal levels of prestige, power, or economic reward. A highly educated worker in a low-paid job, or a wealthy person with little public prestige, may experience expectations that do not line up. Status inconsistency can affect how people are treated and how they understand themselves.

A status symbol is a visible sign that communicates a social position or claims an identity. A uniform, professional credential, wedding ring, luxury vehicle, work badge, religious garment, or university sweatshirt can tell others how to interpret the wearer. Status symbols are not always accurate indicators of wealth or authority; their sociological importance lies in the meanings people attach to them.

Some settings elevate one position into a master status—a status that becomes especially influential in how others classify a person and interpret nearly everything that person does. Occupation, disability, race, criminal record, celebrity, or parenthood may become a master status in particular contexts. No status is inherently masterful in every setting. Its dominance is produced when institutions, audiences, and repeated interactions make one classification difficult to escape.

Status categories also intersect. A person does not experience age, gender, class, race, citizenship, occupation, or disability one at a time. Their combination can produce expectations and opportunities that cannot be understood by simply adding separate effects. This is why achieved status should never be treated as achievement operating outside structure: the routes into valued positions are organized through prior resources, gatekeepers, networks, and institutional rules.

Callout: reading a status set

Imagine a person who is a first-generation college student, a full-time employee, a daughter, a renter, and a caregiver for a younger sibling. Each status carries obligations and resources. Understanding the person’s choices requires examining the entire status set, not treating the person as only a student.

Roles: performing a position

A role is the set of expected behaviors, obligations, and privileges attached to a status. A status is occupied; a role is performed. The role of a teacher includes preparing lessons, evaluating students, maintaining boundaries, and responding to institutional expectations. The role of a patient includes communicating symptoms, following a treatment plan, and negotiating with health-care workers.

A single status usually contains a role set: the different relationships and expectations attached to that position. A professor has a role set that includes relations with students, colleagues, administrators, families, professional associations, and the public. These audiences may want different things from the same person.

Concept: role strain

Tension among competing expectations within a single status. A student may experience role strain when one course requires extensive reading, another requires group work, and a third requires employment-related preparation.

Role conflict occurs when expectations attached to different statuses clash. A worker may be expected to stay late while also being expected to pick up a child. A student may need to attend class while a family member needs care. Role conflict is not simply poor time management; it can reflect incompatible institutional schedules.

Role overload occurs when the total number or intensity of role demands exceeds the time, energy, or resources available. Role overload can affect people who occupy many statuses at once, but it is also shaped by workplace policy, family support, transportation, income, and access to services.

Role captivity describes the feeling of being trapped in a role that one did not choose or wants to leave. A caregiver may love a family member yet feel confined by the expectation that caregiving must be constant. A worker may need a job for income while experiencing the position as identity-limiting or impossible to exit.

Roles are therefore neither fixed scripts nor purely personal inventions. Institutions supply expectations, sanctions, and resources, but people interpret the role, bargain over its boundaries, and perform it differently for different audiences. When enough actors alter a role in similar ways—when fathers take more daily caregiving responsibility, employees normalize remote work, or students challenge assumptions about disability—the institutional definition of the role can change. Role performance is one mechanism through which structure becomes action and action becomes structure again.

Callout: role conflict, strain, and overload

A parent who is also a student may face role conflict when an exam occurs during a child’s medical appointment. The same person may experience role strain when the student role demands both perfect attendance and extensive independent work. If several courses, work shifts, and caregiving duties accumulate beyond available time, the result is role overload.

Social groups

A social group is more than a collection of people in the same place. Groups involve interaction, a sense of membership or belonging, and shared expectations or norms. Groups provide identities, social support, information, opportunities, and standards against which people evaluate themselves.

Concept: social group

A set of people who interact, recognize some form of membership or connection, and share expectations that organize their relationship.

A primary group is relatively small and intimate. Family members and close friends often provide emotional support and help form the self. A secondary group is usually larger, more formal, and organized around a task or goal, such as a class, workplace, political organization, or professional association. The difference is not simply size. A small committee can be formal, while a large family network can remain intimate.

An in-group is a group with which a person identifies as “we.” An out-group is experienced as “they.” In-group boundaries can create solidarity, but they can also produce stereotyping, exclusion, and conflict. A reference group is a group people use as a standard for evaluating themselves, even if they are not members. Students may compare themselves with a professional community they hope to join; consumers may compare their lifestyles with an imagined peer group presented through media.

Groups differ in their internal structure. A dyad consists of two members and depends on both people continuing the relationship. A triad consists of three members and can form coalitions, mediate disagreements, or leave one member isolated. As groups grow, leadership, specialization, rules, and formal procedures become more important.

Callout: why groups matter

A workplace is a secondary group, but it may also contain primary relationships, informal cliques, in-groups, out-groups, and reference groups. The organization chart shows formal roles; the actual experience of work also depends on friendship, trust, status, and informal norms.

Georg Simmel: group size changes social possibilities

Georg Simmel treated group size as a structural property rather than a mere head count. A dyad is unusually intimate and fragile because each member relates directly to the other and either person can end the group by leaving. Adding a third person transforms the possibilities. The third member may mediate, exploit disagreement, or join one person in a coalition against the other.

As groups grow, no member can maintain equally intimate relations with everyone. Subgroups, representatives, formal leadership, specialized tasks, and rules become more likely. Size changes the form of interaction, which then changes what members can do. This is a compact example of a macro principle: the arrangement of relationships produces consequences that cannot be reduced to individual personalities.

Social networks: structure beyond group boundaries

A social network consists of actors, called nodes, connected through relationships, called ties. Unlike a formally bounded group, a network can stretch across workplaces, families, neighborhoods, online platforms, and organizations. Network analysis asks who is connected to whom, how closely, through what kinds of ties, and with what consequences for information, trust, influence, support, and opportunity.

Network position matters. A person with many connections may have access to more information, but the pattern and diversity of ties can be more important than the number. Dense networks can provide trust and rapid support while circulating redundant information and enforcing conformity. Connections that bridge otherwise separated circles can bring novel information and make the broker connecting them influential.

Mark Granovetter: the strength of weak ties

Mark Granovetter showed why close friends are not always the most useful source of new opportunities. Strong ties often know the same people and information that we do. Weak ties—acquaintances, former classmates, distant coworkers, and other less intimate contacts—more often connect us to social circles beyond our immediate group.

The argument does not claim that weak relationships provide more emotional support. It distinguishes kinds of resources. Strong ties are often crucial during crisis; weak and bridging ties can carry unfamiliar job information, ideas, and contacts. Network structure therefore helps explain why two equally skilled people can encounter different opportunities.

Callout: a student’s network as social structure

A student may receive encouragement from family, study support from close friends, internship information from an acquaintance, and a recommendation from a professor. These ties cross primary and secondary groups. Their effects depend not only on the student’s motivation but also on which institutions and opportunities the network reaches.

Social institutions: organized domains of life

A social institution is a durable, routinized pattern of practices organized around a major domain of social life. Institutions are not buildings or single organizations. A university is an organization within the broader institution of education; a courthouse is an organization within the institution of law; a particular church is an organization within the institution of religion.

Each institution contains interconnected values, norms, statuses, roles, groups, resources, and sanctions. Institutions tell people what is expected, distribute responsibilities, define legitimate authority, and provide procedures for handling recurring problems. They also shape what people imagine as normal. Because institutions are repeated across time, they can feel natural even though people created and maintain them.

Concept: institution versus organization

An institution is a broad pattern of norms and practices in a social domain. An organization is a concrete group or formal structure operating within that domain. Marriage is an institution; a particular household or wedding-planning company is an organization or group connected to it.

How practices become institutions

Institutionalization begins when repeated solutions to recurring problems become expected patterns. A convenient practice becomes a routine; a routine becomes attached to roles; organizations record and enforce it; laws, credentials, moral narratives, and professional knowledge legitimate it; and new participants learn it as the normal way to proceed. The result is more than repetition. An institution carries authority and can sanction those who depart from its expectations.

Once established, institutions often display path dependence: earlier decisions shape which later options are affordable, legitimate, or even imaginable. A city built around automobiles cannot adopt a transit-centered system simply by changing individual preferences; roads, zoning, housing, budgets, and daily routines have developed together. Yet path dependence is not destiny. Crisis, innovation, organized conflict, demographic change, or the gradual reinterpretation of roles can open a different path.

The institution of marriage and family

Marriage organizes socially recognized intimate relationships, expectations of commitment, property, inheritance, sexuality, caregiving, and kinship. The institution has changed across time and cultures. Who may marry, what rights spouses possess, how households are organized, and how gendered responsibilities are defined are all shaped by law, religion, economy, and culture.

Marriage is not identical to a particular married couple. A couple occupies statuses such as spouse, partner, parent, or in-law and performs roles shaped by both personal negotiation and institutional expectations. A conflict perspective asks who benefits from legal and economic arrangements. An interactionist perspective asks how partners define fairness, love, commitment, and family responsibility.

The institution of government and politics

Government organizes collective decision-making, authority, law, taxation, public services, and the legitimate use of force. Its statuses include citizen, voter, judge, legislator, civil servant, police officer, and elected executive. Its roles include representing constituents, enforcing rules, adjudicating disputes, and allocating public resources.

Government can coordinate action that individuals cannot accomplish alone, such as building infrastructure or responding to a disaster. It can also distribute burdens and benefits unequally. Political institutions therefore connect social structure to power: who gets heard, who is protected, who is monitored, and who has access to decision-makers.

The institution of education

Education transmits knowledge, skills, values, credentials, and cultural practices. It socializes people into roles, creates networks, and can support social integration. At the same time, schools sort students through grades, tracks, credentials, and disciplinary practices. Access to experienced teachers, stable housing, technology, transportation, and time for study is not equally distributed.

The institution of religion

Religion organizes sacred meanings, rituals, moral expectations, and communities. Religious institutions can create solidarity, provide care, support families, and give meaning during crisis. They can also define boundaries, legitimate authority, or become sites of conflict. Religious organizations are one part of the broader institution of religion.

The institution of the economy

The economy organizes production, work, exchange, consumption, and the distribution of resources. It assigns statuses such as employer, employee, consumer, owner, debtor, and investor. Economic institutions shape where people live, how much time they have, what education they can afford, and how vulnerable they are to illness or unemployment.

Callout: one life across institutions

Consider a student who works in a restaurant, attends a university, rents an apartment, votes in local elections, and supports a family member. The person moves through the economy, education, housing, government, and family institutions every day. A change in one domain—such as a rent increase or work-schedule change—can reorganize the rest of the status set.

Institutions can be analyzed through multiple perspectives. Functionalists ask what needs an institution fulfills and what dysfunctions it produces. Conflict theorists ask how the institution distributes power and advantage. Interactionists ask how people enact institutional roles and negotiate meanings in everyday settings. The perspectives are different, but together they show how large structures become part of lived experience.

Debate: what does education reproduce?

A functionalist account emphasizes knowledge, skill development, socialization, innovation, and the allocation of people to specialized roles. A conflict account agrees that schools perform tasks but asks whether funding, tracking, discipline, curricula, and credentials reproduce class and racial advantage. An interactionist account studies how teachers and students construct ability and belonging in daily encounters. Network theory adds a further mechanism: schools connect students to peers, mentors, and employers unevenly. The theories become most useful when they specify evidence that could distinguish genuine skill development from credential sorting, institutional selection, or unequal access to networks.

The transformation of social structure

Social structures are durable, but they are not permanent. Industrialization moved production from households and small workshops into factories. Urbanization brought people into rapidly growing cities. Wage labor, mass education, national governments, transportation, and communication connected people to organizations much larger than the local community. These changes altered family life, work, neighborhoods, politics, and the experience of time.

The transformation was not a simple march from “traditional” to “modern.” Older relationships did not vanish, and modern societies still depend on family, friendship, religion, and local belonging. The sociological question is how different forms of association coexist and how people move among them. A person may spend the day in a formal bureaucracy, return to an intimate household, and participate in an online network at night.

Theories of modernization disagree about what holds the newer order together. Tönnies foregrounded a shift from inherited community to calculated association. Durkheim instead asked how increasing specialization could create a new form of solidarity. Weber emphasized the expansion of rational calculation and bureaucracy. Marx emphasized dispossession, wage labor, class power, and conflict. These are not interchangeable descriptions of one inevitable transition; they identify different mechanisms and different costs.

Émile Durkheim: mechanical and organic solidarity

Durkheim distinguished mechanical solidarity, cohesion grounded in similarity and a strong shared moral order, from organic solidarity, cohesion grounded in specialization and interdependence. The terms do not mean primitive machinery versus natural life. They describe different bases of social integration.

As the division of labor expands, people may share fewer occupations and daily experiences, yet depend more heavily on strangers who perform specialized tasks. Organic solidarity therefore makes individual difference possible while increasing systemic dependence. Its failure can produce anomie—weak or disrupted regulation in which expectations no longer provide reliable direction. Durkheim’s account contrasts with the idea that modernization only dissolves community: it may replace one form of cohesion with another, although the transition can be unequal and unstable.

Anthony Giddens: structuration and modernity

Anthony Giddens developed structuration theory to explain how agency and structure are connected. People draw on rules and resources when they act, and their repeated actions reproduce or alter the structures that organize social life. Structure is therefore both a medium of action and an outcome of action.

Giddens also emphasized the distinctive conditions of modernity: institutions stretch social relationships across time and space, expert systems organize everyday life, and people increasingly monitor and revise their identities and practices. Modern institutions can expand choice while also creating dependence on systems that ordinary people cannot fully control.

This perspective provides a bridge to microsociology. Everyday interaction matters because it is where people enact rules, use resources, interpret institutions, and sometimes change what those institutions mean.

Ferdinand Tönnies: Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft

Ferdinand Tönnies described a historical shift from Gemeinschaft, or community, to Gesellschaft, or association. Gemeinschaft relationships are intimate, personal, and guided by tradition, kinship, and shared belonging. Gesellschaft relationships are more formal, impersonal, calculated, and oriented toward specific interests or contracts.

Tönnies’ distinction helps explain why modernization can produce both opportunity and loss. Formal associations make large-scale cooperation possible, but people may also experience weaker local ties, greater anonymity, and a stronger need to manage relationships through rules and contracts.

Charles Horton Cooley: primary and secondary groups

Charles Horton Cooley used the idea of the primary group to describe intimate, face-to-face associations that help form the social self. Families, childhood play groups, and close friends provide emotional support and early socialization. Secondary groups are larger, more formal, and often organized around a task or goal, such as a class, company, political party, or labor union.

The distinction shows that social change is also a change in the settings through which people become known, evaluated, and supported. It also reminds us that a large organization can contain small primary relationships, while a family can be governed by formal rules and unequal power.

Weber: rationalization and the iron cage

Max Weber used rationalization to describe the historical expansion of calculation, efficiency, predictability, technical expertise, and formal control. Rationalization does not mean that every person becomes perfectly logical. It means that institutions increasingly organize action through procedures that compare means and ends, measure performance, classify cases, calculate costs, and make outcomes predictable.

Concept: rationalization

The historical process through which social action becomes increasingly organized around calculation, efficiency, predictability, technical knowledge, and formal rules.

Weber distinguished several forms of rationality. Practical rationality involves calculating how to manage everyday problems and reach immediate goals. Theoretical rationality seeks to explain the world through abstract concepts and systematic knowledge. Substantive rationality evaluates action according to values or ultimate ends, such as justice, religious duty, equality, or human dignity. Formal rationality evaluates action through general rules, procedures, numbers, and calculable results, often without asking whether the goal itself is morally worthwhile.

These forms can overlap, but they can also conflict. A hospital may use formal rationality to schedule beds, measure productivity, and control costs. A physician may use practical rationality to solve a patient’s immediate problem. A family may use substantive rationality to insist that care should be available because human life has value, even when the treatment is not profitable. Sociology asks which kind of rationality an institution privileges and whose values become secondary.

Callout: when the metric becomes the goal

A university may begin by using graduation rates, student evaluations, and employment outcomes as tools for understanding education. Over time, the measures can become targets that shape teaching, advising, and admissions. Formal rationality makes comparison possible, but it can also encourage people to pursue what is measurable rather than what is educationally meaningful.

Weber believed rationalization brought important gains: reliable administration, specialized expertise, technical innovation, consistent legal procedures, and coordination across large populations. Yet he also warned that rationalized institutions could trap people inside systems of rules and calculation. The metaphor of the iron cage describes a condition in which people are enclosed by the procedures, obligations, credentials, schedules, and economic pressures of formally rational institutions.

The iron cage is not a literal prison. It is a way of describing how a system built for efficiency can become difficult to escape or question. A worker may need credentials to obtain a job, performance metrics to keep it, and constant availability to advance. A citizen may need to navigate forms, eligibility rules, databases, and automated decisions to receive public help. The system may be impersonal rather than intentionally cruel, yet its rules can still narrow the range of meaningful choices.

Weber’s argument is not that rationality is always bad or that tradition is always good. Formal rules can protect people from arbitrary personal favoritism. Calculation can expose waste and improve safety. The sociological problem arises when formal rationality becomes the only legitimate way to decide, when institutions treat people as cases rather than persons, or when efficiency overrides values that cannot be measured easily.

Bureaucracy as rationalized organization

Bureaucracy is a rationalized form of organization characterized by hierarchy, division of labor, specialized expertise, written rules, written records, impersonality, and replaceability. Bureaucracies make large-scale coordination possible because decisions do not depend entirely on personal relationships. A person can apply for a passport, enroll in a university, or file a tax return through standardized procedures.

At the same time, bureaucratic rules can create red tape, delay, inflexibility, and a feeling that no individual is responsible for the outcome. The same impersonality that limits favoritism can make an institution difficult to navigate when a person’s situation does not fit a standard category.

Callout: applying for help

A person seeking financial aid, medical care, or immigration relief may meet many employees who perform specialized tasks. The system may be fairer when rules are transparent, but the person can also experience the institution as confusing or indifferent. A structural analysis asks how bureaucratic organization produces that experience.

Why bureaucracies depart from their own ideals

Weber described bureaucracy as an ideal type: an analytical model that highlights defining features, not a claim that actual organizations operate perfectly. Later sociologists examined recurrent departures from the model. Robert Merton showed how strict conformity to rules can produce trained incapacity: procedures learned as reliable means become ends in themselves, leaving officials unable to respond intelligently to unusual cases. Goal displacement occurs when protecting the procedure, budget, metric, or organization replaces the purpose for which it was created.

Informal organization also grows inside formal hierarchy. Employees exchange favors, develop workarounds, protect colleagues, and rely on tacit knowledge that no manual captures. These relations can humanize a rigid system and keep it functioning, but they can also create favoritism or conceal mistakes. Bureaucracy therefore never eliminates personal relationships; it changes where and how they operate.

Robert Michels: the iron law of oligarchy

Robert Michels argued that even organizations committed to democracy tend to concentrate authority in a small leadership group. Large organizations require coordination, expertise, records, communication systems, and full-time officials. Leaders gain control over these resources, while ordinary members face higher costs of sustained participation.

Michels called this tendency the iron law of oligarchy. It is a structural argument, not a claim that every leader is corrupt. Organization itself can generate unequal information and influence. Critics note that leadership can be checked by elections, transparency, competing centers of authority, unions, professional norms, and active membership. The debate asks when organizational necessities produce durable elite control and which institutions can hold leaders accountable.

Digital bureaucracy and automated decisions

Contemporary bureaucracies increasingly translate rules into databases, ranking systems, risk scores, and automated workflows. Digitization can speed routine decisions and document consistency, but it may also conceal judgment inside technical systems. A person denied a benefit, loan, interview, or account may not know which data produced the result or which official has authority to reconsider it. The iron cage can therefore operate through code as well as paperwork.

This development sharpens the chapter’s recurring question about agency and structure. Designers, managers, regulators, and users create automated systems through decisions, but once embedded across institutions those systems organize later choices at a scale no individual actor controls. Reform requires more than changing one employee’s attitude; it may require altering data definitions, appeal procedures, incentives, procurement rules, and legal accountability together.

Three ways to analyze structure

Three sociological perspectives on social structure
PerspectiveCentral questionTypical emphasis
Structural functionalismHow do parts of society contribute to order and continuity?Functions, dysfunctions, integration, and social needs
Conflict theoryWho benefits from the existing arrangement?Power, inequality, competing interests, and institutional advantage
Symbolic interactionismHow do people create shared meanings in interaction?Symbols, definitions of situations, identities, and everyday conduct

These perspectives are not interchangeable, but their differences are useful. A school can transmit knowledge and integrate students; it can also reproduce class and racial inequality; and it is experienced through classroom interactions in which students and teachers interpret one another. A full sociological explanation can move among these levels.

Other approaches supply mechanisms that cut across the table. Network analysis explains how the pattern of ties channels resources among people and organizations. Structuration theory explains how actors draw on rules and resources while reproducing or altering them. Durkheim and Tönnies offer competing accounts of the transformation of solidarity, while Weber and Marx emphasize rationalization and class power. A coherent explanation should identify which process carries the causal weight rather than merely naming several perspectives.

The central lesson is therefore reciprocal: structure is more than the sum of personal intentions, but it has no life apart from patterned action. A rule constrains because organizations enforce it, people anticipate it, records preserve it, and resources reward compliance. Those same mechanisms identify points of change. Actors can reinterpret the rule, build a coalition, create a bridging network, change an incentive, establish an appeal, or organize a rival institution.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Social structure organizes broad patterns of social life while shaping and being shaped by action.
  • Statuses, roles, groups, networks, and institutions form key components of social structure.
  • Ascribed and achieved statuses combine in a status set and can produce status inconsistency; institutions and audiences can elevate one classification into a master status.
  • Role sets create role strain; competing statuses can create role conflict, role overload, and role captivity.
  • Groups provide membership, identity, support, reference points, and boundaries; group size changes the possibilities for coalition, mediation, and formal organization.
  • Networks channel information, trust, influence, and opportunity through strong, weak, dense, and bridging ties.
  • Institutions organize major domains such as family, government, education, religion, and the economy and become durable through institutionalization and path dependence.
  • Modernization transformed patterns of association without eliminating intimate relationships; Tönnies and Durkheim offered contrasting accounts of that transformation.
  • Weber’s rationalization describes the expansion of calculation, predictability, expertise, and formal control.
  • Formal rationality can improve coordination while producing the iron cage of impersonal rules and obligations.
  • Bureaucracies make large-scale coordination possible but can produce trained incapacity, goal displacement, oligarchy, and opaque automated decisions.
  • Functionalist, conflict, interactionist, network, and structuration approaches identify different mechanisms within the same social arrangement.

Questions for study

  1. How can a social structure constrain and enable action at the same time?
  2. What is the difference between an ascribed status, an achieved status, a master status, and a status symbol?
  3. How do role strain, role conflict, role overload, and role captivity differ?
  4. What makes a social group different from an aggregate, a category, or a network?
  5. Why can weak and bridging ties provide opportunities unavailable through close relationships?
  6. How do institutionalization and path dependence make a practice durable?
  7. How might marriage, government, or education be interpreted through functionalist, conflict, interactionist, and network approaches?
  8. How do Tönnies’s and Durkheim’s accounts of modernization differ?
  9. What are practical, theoretical, substantive, and formal rationality?
  10. Why did Weber describe modern formal organization as an iron cage?
  11. How do trained incapacity, goal displacement, and the iron law of oligarchy challenge the bureaucratic ideal?
