Global Stratification

How countries develop unevenly, how global relationships shape opportunity, and why different regions follow different paths.

Global stratification is the patterned inequality among countries in wealth, health, education, infrastructure, technology, political capacity, and influence. It does not mean that one country is permanently destined to remain poor or that economic growth automatically produces broad prosperity. It asks why development begins from unequal conditions, why countries move at different speeds, and what kinds of institutions and connections help translate growth into improved life chances.

A single smartphone makes the problem visible. Its minerals may be extracted in several countries, its leading chips fabricated in another, its software and intellectual property owned elsewhere, and its assembly performed where large workforces and supplier networks are available. Consumers participate in one product, yet wages, environmental risks, profits, tax revenues, technical learning, and control over design are distributed unevenly. This chapter moves from measuring those inequalities to comparing theories of how history, geography, institutions, ideas, domestic coalitions, and global relationships produce them.

A world of unequal development

Global stratification refers to systematic inequalities between and among nations. Countries vary dramatically in average income, life expectancy, education, access to electricity and the internet, industrial capacity, financial resources, and ability to respond to crises. These differences shape the opportunities available to people before individual effort enters the picture.

Three related distributions should not be confused. Between-country inequality compares countries’ average incomes or capabilities. Within-country inequality compares people or households inside each country. Global interpersonal inequality places individuals throughout the world in one distribution. Rapid growth in a populous lower-income country can reduce between-country inequality even while inequality rises within that country. A statement that “global inequality fell” is therefore incomplete unless the measure and unit of comparison are specified.

Older textbooks often divided the world into “First,” “Second,” and “Third” Worlds. Those Cold War labels are historically important but analytically limited. Contemporary researchers more often use low-, lower-middle-, upper-middle-, and high-income economies, regional classifications, or measures of human development. Each system is a tool, not a complete description of a country.

Concept: development

A broad process of expanding people’s capabilities and choices. Economic growth matters, but development also includes health, education, security, infrastructure, productive employment, political capacity, and the ability to withstand shocks.

The United Nations’ Human Development Index (HDI) makes this broader point. It combines life expectancy, education, and income rather than treating gross domestic product alone as the definition of progress. A country can grow rapidly while leaving important gaps in health, schooling, inclusion, or environmental resilience. Conversely, a country with modest income can make meaningful gains through effective investments in people and institutions.

Amartya Sen: development as freedom and capability

Economist and philosopher Amartya Sen argues that development should be judged by people’s capabilities: their substantive freedom to be and do things they have reason to value. Income is an important means, but the same income can produce different freedoms depending on health, disability, public services, discrimination, safety, and political rights. A school building matters only if children can attend and learn; formal voting rights matter differently where information is restricted or participation is dangerous.

The capability approach influenced human-development measures, but it also exposes their limits. HDI compresses three dimensions into one national average. It cannot represent every valued capability, unequal distribution, political freedom, or ecological cost. It is best treated as a corrective to GDP, not a complete scoreboard.

Callout: compare carefully

Cross-country comparisons require caution. A dollar buys different amounts in different places, so researchers often use purchasing power parity (PPP) to compare living standards. National averages can also conceal wide regional, class, gender, ethnic, and urban-rural differences. A country’s average income is useful, but it is not the same thing as every resident’s lived experience.

Concept: Gini coefficient

A summary measure of inequality that ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). A higher Gini coefficient indicates a more unequal distribution. It is best used alongside median income, poverty, wealth, and information about public services.

The World Bank revised its international poverty lines in 2025 using updated 2021 PPP data. The international extreme-poverty line is now $3.00 per person per day in 2021 international dollars; the lines more typical of lower-middle- and upper-middle-income economies are $4.20 and $8.30. Under the new $3.00 line, about 838 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022. The revision does not mean that 125 million people suddenly became poorer; it improves the comparability of prices and consumption across countries.

The current pattern: progress, divergence, and new risks

Global development has produced major long-run gains in income, health, education, and connectivity. At the same time, progress is uneven and has slowed since the pandemic. The World Bank reports that global poverty reduction has stalled and that high inequality is concentrated especially in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America and the Caribbean. Conflict, debt-service burdens, climate shocks, weak job creation, and slow investment can make it difficult for growth to reach households with the fewest resources.

Development also involves a digital divide. Mobile networks now reach most of the world, yet access is not the same as meaningful use. In low- and middle-income countries, the World Bank’s 2025 tracker estimates that about 1.4 billion adults in 81 countries do not use the internet. Affordability, electricity, language, digital skills, gender gaps, safety, and the relevance of online services all affect whether connectivity becomes a source of learning and economic opportunity.

The AI and data-infrastructure divide

The next layer of global stratification is not simply who can get online; it is who owns, hosts, and can afford the infrastructure behind cloud services and artificial intelligence. AI depends on reliable electricity and broadband, data centers, cloud platforms, advanced chips, locally useful data, technical skills, and institutions that can govern data responsibly. The World Bank summarizes these foundations as the “four Cs”: connectivity, compute, context, and competency.

These foundations are distributed very unevenly. As of June 2025, high-income countries held 77% of global data-center capacity, middle-income countries held 23%, and low-income countries held less than 0.1%. The 2026 Stanford AI Index reports that the United States hosted 5,427 data centers—more than ten times the number in any other country. Maps of data-center locations make the pattern visible, but facility counts are only a rough indicator: power capacity, cloud access, network quality, chip supply, price, and local skills determine what a country can actually do with that infrastructure.

This is a new form of unequal interdependence. Countries without large domestic compute capacity can still use cloud and open-source AI tools, and they may gain from cross-border services. Yet reliance on external platforms can raise questions about cost, data sovereignty, language coverage, security, and whether local firms and researchers can build systems suited to local needs. The policy choice is not always to build a national frontier-model data center; it may be to secure affordable cloud access while investing in reliable power, open standards, local data, universities, and practical AI skills.

Reading a data-center map sociologically

The map shows that data centers exist in many countries but are heavily concentrated in a relatively small number of high-income and upper-middle-income locations. Ask three questions: Who controls the servers and cloud platforms? Who has the affordable electricity, fiber connections, and chips needed to scale AI? Who can turn data and compute into local research, public services, and businesses? The answers reveal a hierarchy of digital power, not merely a map of buildings.

Example: growth versus inclusive growth

A country can report strong GDP growth because commodity prices rise or a capital-intensive sector expands. If the gains do not create productive jobs, raise wages, expand skills, or strengthen public services, households may see little improvement. Inclusive growth asks not only whether the economy grows, but who participates in and benefits from that growth.

Theoretical explanations of global stratification

There is no single explanation for unequal development. The major theories in this chapter emphasize different mechanisms: history and power, institutions and innovation, and unequal interdependence. Each identifies part of the story; each has limits when treated as a complete explanation.

Colonialism and neocolonialism: historical starting points matter

Colonialism theory emphasizes how early-industrializing powers accumulated wealth and power through conquest, land control, extraction of labor and resources, and trade arrangements that favored the colonizer. Colonial administrations often built infrastructure around extraction or administration rather than broad-based national development. Borders, property systems, educational systems, and political institutions could outlast formal colonial rule.

Neocolonialism refers to continuing influence after formal independence through debt, corporate ownership, trade rules, technology control, military relationships, or dependence on external capital. This perspective asks whether countries can make key economic decisions independently and whether they retain value from their own resources.

The approach is strongest when it connects history to specific institutions and relationships. Its limitation is that history alone cannot explain why countries with similar colonial experiences have since diverged. Domestic policy, leadership, social coalitions, geography, regional ties, and global timing also matter.

Modernization theory: productivity, institutions, and structural transformation

Modernization theory argues that countries tend to develop by expanding education, infrastructure, markets, technology, administrative capacity, and productive investment. Walt Rostow’s famous stages model portrayed development as movement from traditional society toward higher mass consumption through investment and industrialization.

Contemporary versions are more cautious than simple stage models. They emphasize structural transformation: moving workers and firms into more productive activities; improving state capacity; building reliable rules; investing in health, education, energy, and transport; and linking domestic firms to larger markets. The theory helps explain why skills, institutions, and innovation are important.

Its weakness is the assumption that every country can follow the same path or that “traditional values” are the main obstacle to development. Countries face different histories, resource constraints, geopolitical conditions, and terms of trade. Modernization is more useful as a set of questions about productivity and institutions than as a one-size-fits-all script.

Institutions, geography, and historical contingency

Institutional explanations emphasize rules governing property, taxation, public administration, political participation, and constraints on rulers. Inclusive institutions may encourage broad investment and innovation; extractive institutions can concentrate gains and discourage productive activity. Critics caution that institutions are not detached policy switches: they emerge from conflict, colonial history, social coalitions, and distributions of power.

Geographic explanations draw attention to disease environments, access to coasts and navigable rivers, soil and climate, transport costs, and natural-resource endowments. Geography affects constraints but rarely dictates one outcome. Technologies, infrastructure, public health, trade routes, and state capacity change its consequences. The disagreement between institutional and geographic accounts is most useful when framed as a search for interactions: under what political arrangements does a resource become a source of public investment rather than conflict or rent seeking?

Dependency and world-systems theory: unequal interdependence

Dependency theory argues that many lower-income countries are linked to wealthier countries in relationships that can limit autonomous development. The basic concern is that countries exporting raw materials or low-value labor may capture a smaller share of the value created in global supply chains, while high-value design, finance, technology, and branding remain concentrated elsewhere.

World-systems theory, associated with Immanuel Wallerstein, describes a global economy organized around core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral positions. Core activities tend to have high value and strong bargaining power; peripheral activities often involve lower-value extraction or labor-intensive production; semi-peripheral countries combine elements of both and can serve as important sites of industrial upgrading.

These theories sharpen questions about supply chains, debt, bargaining power, and who captures profits. Their limitation is that they can understate the capacity of countries and firms to upgrade, diversify, build domestic capability, and negotiate different relationships with global markets.

Developmental states and embedded autonomy

Research on the developmental state rejects a simple choice between free markets and total state control. Chalmers Johnson, Alice Amsden, Peter Evans, and others studied governments that coordinated investment, disciplined or supported firms, promoted exports, and built technical capacity. Evans described effective agencies as having embedded autonomy: professional coherence and insulation from narrow capture, combined with dense ties to firms that provide information needed for policy.

This approach explains why similar subsidies or tariffs can yield different outcomes. Industrial policy may foster learning when support is conditional on performance and agencies can withdraw it; the same policy may preserve politically connected firms where accountability is weak. State capacity is therefore relational, not merely the size of government.

Feminist development theory: production depends on social reproduction

National accounts and development strategies often emphasize paid production while overlooking unpaid and underpaid care, subsistence work, and household labor. Feminist scholars show that education, health, migration, austerity, and labor-market reforms redistribute this work, often toward women and girls. A factory can expand only because workers are fed, cared for, and reproduced through households and communities.

Adding gender does more than disaggregate a statistic. It can change the causal account: safe transport alters women’s employment options; childcare changes the effective return to education; property and inheritance rules affect access to credit; and cuts in public services shift labor into households. Development can raise GDP while limiting capabilities if its hidden care costs are ignored.

A synthetic approach: development is shaped by both domestic capability and global position

The most useful approach combines the theories. Historical power affects starting conditions. Domestic institutions, human capital, infrastructure, and policy choices affect whether a country can learn, invest, and coordinate development. Global trade, finance, technology, and geopolitics shape the opportunities and constraints facing those choices.

A theory is a tool. Use it flexibly: identify the mechanism it highlights, test it against evidence, and ask what it leaves out.

Trade, foreign investment, and outsourcing: opportunity, risk, and bargaining power

Foreign direct investment (FDI) occurs when a firm or investor establishes a lasting interest in an enterprise in another country. International outsourcing occurs when a company contracts work to a supplier in another country. Both can connect local workers and firms to technology, capital, export markets, training, and jobs. Both can also create risks if wages remain low, environmental costs are shifted locally, profits are rapidly repatriated, or local firms cannot move beyond low-value activities.

The key is not whether trade or FDI is simply “good” or “bad.” Outcomes depend on sector, contract terms, local supplier development, worker protections, environmental enforcement, tax capacity, skills, infrastructure, and whether domestic firms can acquire higher-value functions such as engineering, logistics, design, quality control, or branding.

Global commodity chains and value-chain governance

Global commodity-chain and global value-chain research opens the black box between “trade” and outcomes. Lead firms govern chains through product specifications, purchasing contracts, intellectual property, delivery schedules, and quality standards even when they do not own supplier factories. Suppliers may learn and upgrade from assembly into components, design, or branding, but they can also face intense price pressure and bear the cost of demand fluctuations.

The approach bridges modernization and dependency. Participation can transmit knowledge and create employment, while the chain’s governance determines bargaining power and who captures value. Empirical analysis therefore follows a product across organizations rather than assigning benefits or harms to international trade in the abstract.

Supply-chain question

Consider a smartphone, shoe, or garment. Design, intellectual property, finance, marketing, component production, assembly, logistics, retail, and after-sales service may occur in different countries. A sociological analysis asks: Which activities generate the most value? Where are the risks located? Which workers and firms can move into higher-value tasks over time?

East Asia: export growth, state capacity, and industrial upgrading

East Asia is often used as evidence for modernization because economies such as Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore achieved rapid industrialization and major gains in living standards. The region also demonstrates why modernization should not be understood as a simple free-market formula. The historical record includes land reform in some cases, high investment in education, capable public agencies, export-oriented strategies, disciplined macroeconomic policy, infrastructure, and active efforts to help firms learn and move into more complex production.

The “Four Asian Tigers”—Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan—followed different institutional paths, but each used access to global markets while developing domestic capabilities. South Korea and Taiwan, for example, moved from labor-intensive exports toward automobiles, electronics, advanced manufacturing, and high-technology sectors. Singapore used its location, logistics, education, and public administration to become a major service and manufacturing hub.

China and Viet Nam show another pattern: large-scale integration into world markets combined with major domestic investment, industrial policy, and infrastructure development. The World Bank’s 2026 East Asia and Pacific update projects the region to continue growing faster than the global average, while warning that growth is slowing and that productivity, services, innovation, and resilience are increasingly important. East Asia’s experience supports neither pure dependency nor pure modernization: global integration mattered, but so did the ability to bargain, learn, coordinate, and upgrade.

Lesson from East Asia

Access to global markets can support development when it is paired with domestic capability. Exporting low-wage goods may be a starting point, not an endpoint. The critical question is whether workers, firms, and institutions gain the skills, infrastructure, technology, and bargaining power needed to move upward in the value chain.

Latin America: high inequality, commodity dependence, and inclusive-growth challenges

Latin America and the Caribbean contains high-income, upper-middle-income, and lower-middle-income economies, so it should never be treated as a single case. The region has made gains in education, health, poverty reduction, and democratic participation, yet it remains one of the world’s most unequal regions. ECLAC reports that in 2024, 25.5% of the region’s population—about 162 million people—lived in income poverty. It also reports that the richest 10% received 34.2% of total income while the poorest 10% received 1.7%.

Dependency theory draws attention to the region’s long history of exporting commodities and importing higher-value manufactured goods and capital. This pattern can create vulnerability when global prices fluctuate. Modernization-oriented approaches emphasize productivity, education, infrastructure, formal employment, investment, and institutional capacity. Both perspectives are relevant: many countries face the challenge of diversifying production while also ensuring that growth reaches households through better jobs and reliable social protection.

Recent regional evidence shows why job quality matters. The World Bank’s 2026 update projects only 2.1% regional growth and highlights weak investment, persistent informality, and difficulty creating high-quality jobs. ECLAC notes that social protection can reduce poverty and inequality, but coverage and benefit levels remain uneven. The practical challenge is to combine productive transformation—more competitive and innovative firms—with inclusive institutions that connect people to education, care, finance, and formal work.

Lesson from Latin America

Growth can reduce poverty, but the distribution of gains matters. Policies that expand skills, formal employment, infrastructure, entrepreneurship, and effective social protection can make growth more inclusive. Stronger domestic capability and stronger inclusion are complements, not alternatives.

Africa: demographic change, productive jobs, and regional opportunity

Africa is a continent, not a single development case. Its countries differ widely in population, resources, governance, conflict exposure, geography, economic structure, and regional ties. Some have rapidly growing service, technology, manufacturing, tourism, agricultural-processing, and renewable-energy sectors; others face severe constraints from conflict, climate shocks, debt, infrastructure gaps, or dependence on a narrow range of exports.

The World Bank’s October 2025 Africa’s Pulse projects Sub-Saharan African growth at 3.8% in 2025 and an average of 4.4% in 2026–27. The major development challenge is converting growth into enough productive jobs. The report notes that wage-paying jobs account for only 24% of employment and that most new labor-market entrants work in low-productivity informal sectors. Between 2025 and 2050, the region’s working-age population is projected to expand by more than 620 million people.

This creates both pressure and opportunity. A large, young workforce can support innovation, entrepreneurship, regional markets, and demographic dividends when countries invest in health, education, electricity, transport, digital access, and enterprises that can scale. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) offers one route toward larger regional markets and more diversified production, although implementation, infrastructure, and trade facilitation remain essential.

Dependency theory is useful for analyzing commodity dependence, external debt, and unequal terms of trade. Modernization theory is useful for analyzing public capacity, infrastructure, skills, and productivity. Neither alone captures the full picture. Development pathways will differ across countries and sectors, especially as climate adaptation, mineral value chains, digital services, renewable energy, and regional trade reshape opportunity.

Lesson from African development

Economic growth alone does not guarantee poverty reduction or mobility. The quality and scale of employment matter. Policies that lower the cost of doing business, expand reliable energy and transport, strengthen education and health, and help firms enter larger markets can increase the chance that growth becomes broadly shared.

The comparison points to a central conclusion: development is not a race along one predetermined path. Countries develop through combinations of historical conditions, institutions, human capital, natural resources, regional geography, global market access, political coalitions, and policy choices. The question is not whether a country is simply “developed” or “underdeveloped.” It is which capabilities are growing, which groups are included, and what constraints prevent the next stage of progress.

Development, extraction, and ecological inequality

Economic development transforms energy and materials as well as income. Ecologically unequal exchange theory argues that richer economies can appropriate labor, energy, land, and raw materials from poorer regions while shifting pollution, waste, and ecological damage toward them. Market prices may not capture depleted ecosystems, carbon emissions, occupational disease, or communities’ loss of livelihood. From this perspective, a favorable trade balance can coexist with a net transfer of ecological capacity.

A contrasting ecological modernization perspective emphasizes that regulation, innovation, cleaner energy, circular production, and institutional learning can decouple some forms of economic activity from environmental harm. Critics question how far absolute decoupling can proceed when total consumption grows. The debate asks whether development should reproduce today’s resource-intensive pathway, whether technology can transform it, and how the costs of transition should be shared between countries with different historical emissions and capabilities.

Example: critical-mineral upgrading

Demand for batteries can bring investment to mineral-producing countries. A dependency account asks whether foreign firms capture processing and intellectual-property rents while local communities bear extraction costs. A developmental-state account asks whether policy can build domestic refining, engineering, and supplier skills. A capability approach asks whether residents gain health, education, voice, and secure livelihoods. An ecological account asks who bears water loss, pollution, and eventual waste. The same project can look different because each theory traces a different causal chain.

Applying global stratification: ask better questions

When you encounter a claim about a country’s development, use the following questions to turn a headline into sociological analysis.

  1. Which outcome is being measured? GDP growth, poverty, income inequality, health, education, employment, internet access, or environmental resilience may tell different stories.
  2. Who benefits? Look for regional, class, gender, ethnic, and urban-rural variation rather than assuming the national average applies equally.
  3. What is the historical context? Consider colonial legacies, conflict, trade relationships, debt, migration, and the timing of industrialization.
  4. What is the country’s position in global value chains? Does it mainly export raw materials, assemble products, provide services, develop technology, or control brands and finance?
  5. What domestic capabilities are present? Examine education, health, infrastructure, state capacity, entrepreneurship, firm size, and the ability to learn and upgrade.
  6. What would inclusive progress look like? Identify a policy or institutional change that could increase productivity while also improving access to jobs, services, or security.

Chapter review

Essential ideas

  • Global stratification is the patterned inequality among countries and people in resources, capabilities, and influence.
  • Between-country, within-country, and global interpersonal inequality describe different distributions.
  • Development should be measured with income, poverty, inequality, health, education, infrastructure, capabilities, and human-development indicators rather than GDP alone.
  • PPP adjusts cross-country comparisons for differences in purchasing power.
  • Colonialism and neocolonialism emphasize historical power and continuing external influence.
  • Modernization theory emphasizes productive transformation, institutions, education, infrastructure, and innovation.
  • Institutional and geographic theories emphasize different constraints, but institutions, power, technology, and geography interact.
  • Dependency and world-systems theories emphasize unequal interdependence and positions in global value chains.
  • Developmental-state theory explains how capable public agencies can coordinate learning while remaining vulnerable to capture.
  • Feminist development theory makes unpaid care and social reproduction part of the explanation.
  • East Asia, Latin America, and Africa show that development follows varied pathways and cannot be explained by one theory alone.
  • Trade and foreign investment can create opportunities, but their effects depend on chain governance, domestic capability, bargaining power, job quality, and upgrading.
  • Ecologically unequal exchange and ecological modernization disagree about whether and how development can escape unequal environmental burdens.

Questions for study

  1. Why is GDP per capita an incomplete measure of development?
  2. How do between-country and within-country inequality differ?
  3. How do PPP and national poverty lines improve cross-country comparison?
  4. How does Sen’s capability approach change the meaning of development?
  5. What does each major theory explain well, and what does it leave out?
  6. How can a country benefit from foreign investment while reducing dependency risks?
  7. What is embedded autonomy, and why might the same industrial policy succeed in one state and fail in another?
  8. What features of East Asian development challenge a simple version of modernization theory?

  10. How can global production shift environmental costs across borders?
  11. Choose a country. What evidence would you need to evaluate whether its recent growth has been inclusive?
